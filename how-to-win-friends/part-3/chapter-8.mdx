#### The most underrated edge in business is seeing the trade from the other side of the table

## Everyone thinks they're right

Look, here's the starting point.

The other person might be completely wrong. **But they don't think so.**

Condemning them is free. Any fool can do it. Understanding them is expensive, which is exactly why the few who bother have an edge.

Here's the thing: nobody acts randomly. Find the reason behind someone's behavior and you've found the key to their actions. Maybe to their whole personality.

So the formula is simple. Ask: **"How would I feel, and how would I react, if I were in their shoes?"**

That saves you time and frustration. As Carnegie puts it, "by becoming interested in the cause, we are less likely to dislike the effect."

---

## You care about your stuff. So does everyone else.

Carnegie quotes Kenneth M. Goode, author of *How to Turn People Into Gold*, who makes a point that should be printed on every founder's wall.

Stop and compare how intensely you care about your own affairs with how mildly you care about everything else. Now realize that **every single person on earth is running the same software.**

Goode's conclusion: success with people depends on a sympathetic grasp of their viewpoint. Lincoln and Roosevelt got it. That's the foundation.

Think about the last cold DM you sent. You were focused on your launch. They were focused on theirs. If your message doesn't start from their world, it's noise.

## The weeds story

Carnegie tells a story about one of his students, Sam Douglas of Hempstead, New York.

For years, Sam told his wife she spent way too much time weeding, fertilizing and mowing a lawn that looked no better than when they'd moved in. Every time, the evening was ruined.

After the course, Sam realized she actually *enjoyed* the work, and might appreciate a compliment instead.

One night she asked him to keep her company while she weeded. He said no, then reconsidered and went out to help. She was visibly happy. After that, he helped often and praised what she'd done with soil like concrete.

Result: a happier marriage, because he looked at it from her side.

**Even when the subject is weeds, the principle compounds.**

## Lead with their interests

Dr. Gerald S. Nirenberg, in *Getting Through to People*, breaks it down into an operating playbook. Cooperation in conversation happens when you show that you treat the other person's ideas and feelings as important as your own.

His stack:

- **Tell them where the conversation is going.** Give them the purpose up front.
- **Say what you'd want to hear** if you were on the receiving end.
- **Accept their viewpoint** as legitimate.

Do that and the listener opens up to your ideas. Skip it and you're pitching to a closed door.

## The park, the fires and two very different playbooks

Here's the story that makes this chapter click.

Carnegie tells a story about a park near his home where he loved to ride. He all but worshipped its oak trees, so it killed him to watch young trees burn, season after season, in fires kids started to cook hot dogs.

The warning sign sat in a corner nobody visited. The mounted cop didn't care. Once, told about a spreading fire, he said it wasn't his precinct.

So Carnegie made himself the park's one-man enforcement team.

### Playbook one: command and control

He'd ride up, tell the boys they could be jailed and order the fire out. If they pushed back, he threatened arrest. In his words, he was "merely unloading" his feelings.

The result? They complied, sullenly. Carnegie figured that once he rode over the hill, they rebuilt the fire and wished they could burn down the whole park.

**Compliance is not buy-in.** You can force behavior in the moment. You can't force alignment.

### Playbook two: align the incentives

Years later, with more tact, he changed the approach:

- "Having a good time, boys? What are you cooking for supper?"
- He loved building fires as a kid, and still did.
- The risk isn't you. It's *other* kids who copy you, don't put theirs out, and the trees die.
- Yes, technically you could be jailed. But I don't want to be bossy or ruin your fun.
- The ask: rake the leaves back, bury it in dirt before you leave, and next time use the sandpit over the hill.
- "Thanks so much, boys. Have a good time."

The kids *wanted* to cooperate. No resentment. **They saved face.** Everyone felt better.

The fascinating thing here is that the rules and the penalty didn't change. The only variable was whose perspective the message started from.

Imagine you're shipping a product update. You can tell users "do X or your account gets suspended." Or you can show them why X protects something they care about. Both get clicks. Only one gets loyalty.

## The car payment call

This works under pressure too. Maybe especially under pressure.

Elizabeth Novak of New South Wales, Australia, was six weeks behind on her car payment. On a Friday, the guy handling her account called: $122 by Monday morning, or expect "further action." She had no way to raise it.

When he called Monday, she looked at it from his side. She apologized for the hassle and said she must be his most troublesome customer.

His tone flipped. She was nowhere near his worst, he said, and then he vented about customers who were rude, who lied, who dodged his calls. She just listened.

Then, unprompted, he said $20 by month's end was fine, with the rest whenever it suited her.

Let that sink in. **She had no leverage. She had empathy. Empathy turned out to be the leverage.**

## Do the diligence before the meeting

So here's the practical move.

Tomorrow, before you ask anyone for anything (to put out a fire, buy your product, fund your round, donate to your cause), pause. Close your eyes. Run the whole thing from their side.

Ask: **"Why would they want to do this?"**

It takes time. It also avoids enemies and gets better results with less friction.

Carnegie quotes Dean Donham of the Harvard Business School, and thought it mattered enough to print twice:

> "I would rather walk the sidewalk in front of a person's office for two hours before an interview than step into that office without a perfectly clear idea of what I was going to say and what that person, from my knowledge of his or her interests and motives, was likely to answer."

Imagine you're a founder walking into a pitch. If you can't predict the partner's objections and their fund's thesis, you aren't ready. You're hoping.

**Hope is not a strategy. Diligence is.**

## The one thing

Carnegie's bold claim: if you take only one thing from his book, make it the habit of seeing things from the other person's angle as well as your own. That alone could be a stepping-stone of your career.

That's not hype. Every deal, hire and relationship is a two-sided trade. Whoever models both sides wins more trades.

<Tip>**Principle:** Honestly try to see things from the other person's point of view. Model their incentives before you make your ask.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

Everyone thinks they're right. Fighting that is a losing trade.

Find the reason behind their behavior and you hold the key.

Run their model before you run your pitch. That's the formula, and it works wonders.
