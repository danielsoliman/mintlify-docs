#### Every person has two reasons for every decision. Pitch the one that sounds good.

## Everyone is the hero of their own story

Look, start with the most extreme example possible.

Carnegie grew up near Jesse James country in Missouri and visited the James farm, where Jesse's son still lived. Jesse's daughter-in-law told him how Jesse robbed trains and banks, then gave money to neighboring farmers to pay off their mortgages.

A train robber who probably saw himself as an idealist.

Same with the gangsters who came later: Dutch Schultz, "Two Gun" Crowley, Al Capone.

**If outlaws think they're noble, what do you think your customer, your co-founder or your landlord thinks?**

Everyone you meet wants to be fine and unselfish in their own estimation. That's not a bug in human nature. It's a feature.

---

## Two reasons for everything

Here's the thing. J. Pierpont Morgan had a framework for decoding any decision.

A person usually has two reasons for doing something: **one that sounds good, and the real one.**

The real reason takes care of itself. You don't need to hammer it.

But all of us are idealists at heart. We want to believe our motives are the good-sounding ones.

So if you want to change someone's behavior, **appeal to the nobler motive.** Give them the version of the decision they'd be proud to tell their friends about.

Think product positioning. The feature list is the real reason. The mission is the one that sounds good. The best companies lead with the mission.

---

## The tenant who was about to walk

Is this too soft for actual business? Let's look at the tape.

Hamilton J. Farrell, a Pennsylvania landlord and one of Carnegie's students, had a tenant announce he was leaving immediately, with four months left on the lease. He'd lived there all winter, the most expensive stretch of the year. Re-renting before fall would be tough.

Farrell watched all that rent income about to evaporate. He "saw red." His default play: tell the guy to re-read his lease, point out that the full balance comes due if he leaves, and threaten to collect.

Instead, he told the tenant he didn't believe he really intended to move. Years in the rental business had taught him to read people, and he'd pegged this man as someone who keeps his word. He was so sure that he'd make a bet.

His offer: take a few days. If you still want to move by the first of the month, I'll accept it as final and admit I misjudged you. But I believe you're a man of your word.

He closed with: "we are either men or monkeys — and the choice usually lies with ourselves!"

When the new month came, the tenant showed up and paid the rent in person. He and his wife had talked it over. Living up to the lease was the only honorable thing to do.

**Farrell didn't win on contract terms. He won by handing the tenant an identity worth protecting.**

## Reframe the ask around something sacred

The fascinating thing here is how small the move can be. Often it's one sentence.

- **Lord Northcliffe** found a newspaper running a photo of him he didn't like. He didn't write "I don't like it." He wrote, "Please do not publish that picture of me any more. My mother doesn't like it." Same ask. Totally different motive.
- **John D. Rockefeller, Jr.** wanted photographers to stop snapping pictures of his kids. He didn't say "I don't want them published." He reminded the photographers that some of them had kids too, and too much publicity isn't good for youngsters.
- **Cyrus H. K. Curtis**, the poor kid from Maine who built *The Saturday Evening Post* and the *Ladies' Home Journal*, couldn't afford the rates other magazines paid writers. So he got Louisa May Alcott, at the peak of her fame, to write for him by offering a hundred-dollar check — not to her, but to her favorite charity.

Curtis couldn't compete on cash comp, so he paid in meaning.

Imagine you're a seed-stage founder hiring an engineer who could make twice as much at big tech. You lose the salary war every time. You win when the pitch is "you'll fix this for millions of people."

## "That won't work on my customers"

The skeptic jumps in: fine for millionaires and novelists, but try it on the people who owe me money.

Carnegie's answer is honest. **Nothing works in every case, and nothing works with everyone.** If you're happy with your current results, don't change. If you're not, why not run the experiment?

Then he picks the hardest test case: debt collection.

---

## The six bills nobody could collect

James L. Thomas, a former student, told this one.

Six customers of an auto company refused to pay their service bills, each disputing one charge. All had signed off on the work, so the company knew it was right and said so. Mistake number one.

The credit department's playbook:

1. Show up and announce they're there to collect a long-overdue bill.
2. Make it clear the company was completely right, so the customer was completely wrong.
3. Imply the company knew far more about cars than the customer ever could.
4. Result: arguments.

Nobody paid. The credit manager was about to call the lawyers. Then the general manager noticed something. **All six customers had reputations for paying their bills promptly.** The problem was the method. So he handed the "uncollectible" accounts to Thomas.

Thomas ran a completely different playbook:

1. He went to collect the same correct bill, but never said so. He said he'd come to find out what the company had done, or failed to do.
2. He told the customer he had no opinion until he'd heard their side, and that the company didn't claim to be infallible.
3. He said the customer knew more about his own car than anyone alive.
4. He let them talk, and listened with all the interest and sympathy they wanted.
5. Once they cooled down, he apologized for how they'd been treated and praised their fairness and patience. Then he handed over the bill and asked them to adjust it themselves, as if they were the company's president. Whatever they said would stand.

The results:

- The bills ranged from $150 to $400.
- One customer refused to pay a penny of the disputed charge.
- **The other five all gave the company the best of it.**
- And within two years, the company sold new cars to all six.

Read that again. The aggressive approach produced zero revenue and six enemies. The trust-first approach collected five of six bills and turned all six into repeat buyers. That's not soft. That's lifetime value.

---

## Default to assuming people are honest

Thomas's takeaway is the real alpha here.

When you know nothing about someone, assume they're sincere, honest and willing to pay once convinced the charges are right. Put simply: **people are honest and want to meet their obligations.** The exceptions are comparatively few.

And even the ones inclined to chisel will usually respond well if you make them feel you see them as honest, upright and fair.

Incentive design 101. Treat someone like a suspect and they have nothing to lose by acting like one. Treat them like a person of integrity and you've given them something to protect.

<Tip>**Principle:** Appeal to the nobler motives. Frame your ask so the other person gets to be the version of themselves they're proud of.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

Everyone has two reasons for what they do. The real one, and the one that sounds good.

You don't need to sell the real one. Sell the good one.

Bet on people's best selves. Most of the time, they'll cover the bet.
