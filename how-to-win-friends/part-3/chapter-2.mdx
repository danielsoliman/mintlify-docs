#### "You're wrong" is the most expensive sentence in business

Look, let's start with the scoreboard.

Theodore Roosevelt, sitting in the White House, admitted that if he could be right 75 percent of the time, he'd have hit the ceiling of his own expectations.

Seventy-five percent. That was the dream for one of the most accomplished people of the twentieth century.

Carnegie's follow-up is brutal: if you can be right just 55 percent of the time, you can walk onto Wall Street and print money. If you can't even guarantee 55 percent, **why are you telling other people they're wrong?**

A trader with a 55 percent hit rate and decent risk management is a legend. Yet most of us walk into meetings and group chats acting like our hit rate is 100.

The math doesn't support the confidence.

---

## Here's what actually happens when you say "you're wrong"

You don't even need words to do it.

An eye roll, a sigh on a Zoom call, a "well, actually" reply. People read all of it instantly.

And when they read it, do they want to agree with you? Never.

You just hit them in the intelligence, the judgment, the pride and the self-respect. All at once. Their incentive is no longer to find the truth. **Their incentive is to hit back.**

Bring the logic of Plato or Kant, or a 40-slide deck. Doesn't matter. You've hurt their feelings, and feelings don't respond to spreadsheets.

Here's the thing most smart people miss: changing someone's mind is already hard under perfect conditions. Why would you add friction?

The worst opener in the world is "Let me prove this to you."

Translation: "I'm smarter than you, and I'm about to show it."

That's a challenge, and it puts the other person in a defensive crouch before your first point.

---

## The best persuaders hide the ball

If you're going to prove something, don't let anyone know you're doing it.

Carnegie stacks up a few centuries of wisdom on this:

- Alexander Pope: "Men must be taught as if you taught them not / And things unknown proposed as things forgot."
- Galileo: you can't teach a man anything; you can only help him find it within himself.
- Lord Chesterfield to his son: "Be wiser than other people if you can; but do not tell them so."
- Socrates, over and over: "One thing only I know, and that is that I know nothing."

Carnegie figured he couldn't out-think Socrates, so he quit telling people they were wrong. And he found that it **paid**. This isn't about being nice. It's about a better return.

So when someone says something you think is wrong, even something you *know* is wrong, try this script:

> "Look, I thought otherwise, but I may be wrong. I frequently am. And if I'm wrong, I want to be put right. Let's look at the facts."

There's real leverage in that sentence. Nobody on earth will object to "I may be wrong. Let's look at the facts."

It costs nothing, lowers everyone's guard and puts the conversation back on the data.

## A car dealer runs the experiment

Harold Reinke was a Dodge dealer in Billings, Montana, and one of Carnegie's students.

Reinke admitted the pressure of the car business had made him hard-boiled with complaints. Result: flared tempers and lost business.

So he changed his opening line. When a customer came in hot, he'd say something like: our dealership has made so many mistakes that I'm frequently ashamed. We may have messed up in your case. Tell me about it.

That one move disarmed people. By the time customers had vented, they were far more reasonable about settling the issue. Some thanked him for being understanding. Two of them came back with friends to buy new cars.

Reinke's takeaway: respecting every customer's opinion is a competitive edge.

Here's why it works. **Admitting you might be wrong flips the other person's incentive.** You stop the fight, and you invite them to be just as fair and open-minded as you are. It makes them want to admit that they might be wrong too.

## Being right can still cost you the case

Now let's look at the other side. What happens when you *know* the other person is wrong and you tell them bluntly?

Carnegie tells the story of a young New York attorney, Mr. S., who argued an important case before the U.S. Supreme Court, Lustgarten v. Fleet Corporation. Big money, important legal question.

Mid-argument, one of the justices asked: "The statute of limitations in admiralty law is six years, is it not?"

Mr. S. stopped, stared, and said flatly: "Your Honor, there is no statute of limitations in admiralty."

He later told one of Carnegie's classes that a hush fell over the court and the temperature in the room seemed to drop to zero.

He was right. The justice was wrong. He'd said so in front of everyone.

He still believed he had the law on his side, and said he spoke better than ever. **But he didn't persuade.** He'd made what he called the enormous blunder of telling a very learned, famous man that he was wrong.

Being right and winning are two different trades. You can nail the analysis and still lose the deal.

## The fascinating thing here is how beliefs actually work

Most people aren't logical. We're biased. We carry around preconceived notions, jealousy, suspicion, fear, envy and pride.

And most people don't want to change their minds about their religion, their haircut, their politics or their favorite celebrity.

Carnegie tells readers who like correcting people to reread a passage from James Harvey Robinson's *The Mind in the Making* every morning before breakfast. The core of it:

- We sometimes change our minds easily, with no resistance at all, until someone *tells* us we're wrong. Then we dig in.
- We form beliefs carelessly, but defend them with an intense passion when anyone tries to take them away.
- **It's not the idea we're protecting. It's our self-esteem.**
- The most important word in human affairs is "my." My dinner, my dog, my house, my country, my god. We resent the claim that our watch is wrong just as much as the claim that our view of history or science needs revision.
- So most of what we call reasoning is just finding new arguments for believing what we already believe.

That's the operating system everyone runs, including you.

Think about the last time someone quote-posted your take with "this is just wrong." Did you update your model, or start drafting a reply? Once a belief has your name on it, it's part of your portfolio, and nobody likes being told their portfolio is garbage.

The psychologist Carl Rogers made a related point in *On Becoming a Person*. Our first reaction to almost anything someone says is to evaluate it: "that's right," "that's stupid," "that's unreasonable." We very rarely let ourselves understand what the statement actually *means* to the other person.

You have to *permit* yourself to understand someone. Judgment is the default. Understanding takes effort.

## The draperies test

Carnegie tells a story on himself.

He once hired an interior decorator to make draperies for his home. When the bill showed up, he was dismayed.

A few days later a friend came over, heard the price and said, with a note of triumph: "What? That's awful. I'm afraid he put one over on you."

Was she right? Yes. But nobody enjoys a truth that reflects badly on their judgment. So Carnegie did what humans do. He defended himself. Quality is cheapest in the long run. You can't get artistic taste at bargain prices. And so on.

The next day, a different friend came over, admired the draperies and gushed about how she wished she could afford something that beautiful.

Carnegie's reaction was the complete opposite: "To tell the truth, I can't afford them myself. I paid too much. I'm sorry I ordered them."

Same facts. Opposite outcome.

**When we're wrong, we'll often admit it to ourselves.** If we're handled gently, we'll admit it to others and even feel proud of how open-minded we are. But not when someone tries to force the fact down our throat.

Imagine you're a founder who overpaid for a SaaS tool. An advisor says "you got ripped off" and you'll defend the purchase all day. Another says "that looks like a premium setup" and you'll tell them you probably overspent. The incentive to admit the mistake only shows up when your ego isn't under attack.

## Abuse never moves the market

Horace Greeley was the most famous newspaper editor in America during the Civil War, and he violently disagreed with Lincoln's policies.

His strategy was to drive Lincoln into agreement through argument, ridicule and abuse. He ran that campaign month after month, year after year. He wrote a brutal, sarcastic, personal attack on Lincoln the very night Booth shot him.

Did any of it make Lincoln agree with Greeley? Not at all.

**Ridicule and abuse never work.** Not in 1865 and not in your replies.

## Ben Franklin's rebrand

If you want a playbook, Carnegie points you to Benjamin Franklin's autobiography.

As a young man, Franklin was insufferable. An old Quaker friend pulled him aside and delivered some hard feedback. In short: Ben, you're impossible. Your opinions slap everyone who disagrees with you. Your friends have more fun when you're not around. You act like you know so much that nobody can tell you anything, so nobody bothers to try. Which means you'll never know more than you do now, and that's very little.

What Carnegie admires is how Franklin took it. He saw it was true, saw he was headed for failure, and made a full reversal.

Franklin's new rules:

- No direct contradiction of other people's views.
- No confident assertions of his own.
- He banned words that signaled a fixed opinion, like "certainly" and "undoubtedly."
- He swapped in softer framing: "I conceive," "I imagine," "it so appears to me at present."
- When someone said something he thought was wrong, he skipped the pleasure of shooting it down. Instead, he'd say that in certain cases their opinion would be right, but in this case there seemed to be some difference.

The results, in Franklin's own telling:

- Conversations went more smoothly.
- His opinions got a readier reception and less pushback.
- When he was wrong, it hurt less.
- When he was right, he more easily got others to drop their mistakes and join him.

It felt unnatural at first, then became habit. Franklin said that for fifty years nobody heard a dogmatic expression from him.

And here's the kicker. Franklin said he was a bad speaker. Never eloquent. Hesitant with his words. Often not even grammatically correct. **And yet he generally carried his points.** He credited this habit, second only to his reputation for integrity, for the weight he had with his fellow citizens and his influence in public councils.

That's the ROI. Franklin had no charisma edge. He just stopped picking fights, and his influence compounded for decades.

## Two case studies from the field

Does Franklin's method hold up in business? Two examples.

**Case one: the incentive system nobody wanted.**

Katherine A. Allred was an industrial engineering supervisor at a yarn-processing plant in Kings Mountain, North Carolina. Part of her job was designing incentive systems so operators could earn more by producing more.

The old pay system worked fine when the plant ran two or three types of yarn. Now it ran more than twelve. The system no longer paid operators fairly or gave them a reason to push production. Allred built a better one that paid by the class of yarn being run.

She walked into the management meeting ready to prove she was right. She told them in detail how they were wrong, where they were unfair, and how she had all the answers.

She failed miserably. She was so busy defending her system that she left management no graceful way to admit the old one had problems. The idea died in the room.

After a few sessions of Carnegie's course, she tried again. This time she asked management where *they* saw problems. They walked through each point. She asked for their opinions on the best path forward. With a few low-key suggestions at the right moments, she let them build her system themselves.

When she finally presented it at the end of the meeting, they enthusiastically accepted it.

Her conclusion: telling someone flatly they're wrong strips their dignity and makes you unwelcome in the discussion.

Same product, different go-to-market.

**Case two: the lumber shipment.**

R. V. Crowley sold lumber for a company in New York. For years he'd been telling tough lumber inspectors they were wrong, and he'd been winning the arguments. But it didn't help. As he put it, lumber inspectors are like baseball umpires. Once they make a call, they never reverse it.

Crowley realized his company was losing thousands of dollars through the arguments he *won*. Read that again. Winning the argument was the losing trade.

So he changed tactics. Then the test came.

A customer called, furious. A carload of lumber had arrived, and after unloading about a quarter of it, their inspector said it was running 55 percent below grade. They stopped unloading and wanted it hauled away.

Normally Crowley would have quoted the grading rules to prove the lumber met spec. Instead, he found the purchasing agent and inspector ready for a fight and simply asked them to keep unloading and sorting rejects so he could watch.

As he watched, he realized the inspector was being far too strict and misreading the rules. The lumber was white pine. The inspector knew hardwoods well but wasn't experienced with white pine, which happened to be Crowley's specialty.

Did Crowley object? Not once.

He just watched and asked friendly questions about why certain pieces didn't make the cut. He made it clear his only goal was to send exactly what they wanted in future shipments. He kept agreeing they were right to reject boards that didn't suit their purpose.

The tension thawed. A few careful remarks planted the idea that some rejects were actually within the grade they'd bought, and that they really needed a more expensive grade. Crowley never made it an issue.

Gradually the inspector admitted he wasn't experienced with white pine and started asking Crowley about each piece. Eventually he felt guilty every time he tossed a board on the reject pile, and saw the mistake was theirs for not ordering the grade they needed.

The outcome: after Crowley left, the inspector went through the whole carload again, accepted all of it, and Crowley's company got paid in full.

A little tact saved his company substantial cash, plus goodwill that was hard to price.

**That's the incentive lesson in one story.** He won the argument for years and lost money. He stopped arguing and got paid.

## Judge people by their principles, not yours

Martin Luther King Jr. was asked how, as a pacifist, he could admire Air Force General Daniel "Chappie" James, then the highest-ranking Black officer in the country. King's answer: "I judge people by their own principles, not by my own."

General Robert E. Lee once praised a certain officer to Confederate president Jefferson Davis in glowing terms. Another officer was stunned and pointed out that this man was one of Lee's bitterest enemies, who never missed a chance to attack him.

Lee replied, in effect: the president asked for my opinion of him. He didn't ask for his opinion of me.

Separate the signal from your ego.

## None of this is new

Two thousand years ago, Jesus said: "Agree with thine adversary quickly."

More than four thousand years ago, King Akhtoi of Egypt told his son: "Be diplomatic. It will help you gain your point."

When advice survives that many market cycles, it's not a trend. It's a fundamental.

So don't argue with your customer, your partner or your opponent. Don't tell them they're wrong. Don't get them worked up. Use a little diplomacy.

<Tip>**Principle:** Respect the other person's position. Never say "you're wrong." Give them room to update on their own.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

You will be wrong a lot. Everyone is.

Telling people they're wrong doesn't change their minds. It just makes them defend their position harder.

Say "I may be wrong, let's look at the facts," ask questions, and let people reach the right answer themselves.

**Winning the argument is a vanity metric. Winning the outcome is the real return.**
