#### Nobody buys what you sell. They buy what they discover.

## The ownership premium

Look, here's a question worth sitting with.

Do you trust an idea more when someone hands it to you, or when you figure it out yourself?

Be honest. It's the second one. Every time.

Self-discovered conclusions come with a built-in **ownership premium**. You defend them harder and execute faster.

So why do most of us spend our days trying to ram our conclusions down other people's throats?

**It's bad strategy.** The smarter play is to suggest, and let the other person close the loop.

---

## Make them write the term sheet

Here's the thing about incentives: people honor deals they help write.

Carnegie tells the story of Adolph Seltz, a sales manager at a Philadelphia car dealership. His team was discouraged and disorganized. Morale was in the gutter.

Seltz didn't deliver a speech. He called a meeting and asked the team what they expected from *him*. He wrote every answer on the blackboard.

Then he flipped it: "I'll give you all of this. Now tell me what I have a right to expect from you."

The answers came fast:

- Loyalty
- Honesty
- Initiative
- Optimism
- Teamwork
- Eight hours a day of real, enthusiastic work

One salesperson volunteered to work fourteen hours a day. Sales went through the roof.

Seltz called it a "moral bargain." His team had set their own terms. As long as he delivered, they were determined to deliver too.

**That's not motivation. That's alignment.** They negotiated the terms, so they owned them.

## 150 meetings, zero deals

The fascinating thing here is how long smart people can run the wrong playbook.

Eugene Wesson sold design sketches to stylists and textile manufacturers. He pitched one of the top buyers in New York every single week for three years.

The buyer always took the meeting and always said some version of: "No, Wesson, I guess we don't get together today."

150 meetings. Zero conversions.

Imagine you're a founder who's pitched the same VC 150 times and gotten a polite pass every time. At some point, the problem isn't the market. It's the pitch.

Wesson figured that out, started studying influence one evening a week, and changed his approach.

He grabbed half a dozen *unfinished* sketches and went to the buyer with a request: "Would you do me a favor? How could we finish these so you could actually use them?"

The buyer studied them in silence. "Leave these with me for a few days."

Three days later, Wesson picked up the feedback, had the studio finish the sketches to spec, and every single one got bought. After that, the buyer ordered scores more, all built around his own ideas.

Wesson nailed the diagnosis himself: "I had urged him to buy what I thought he ought to have... I urged him to give me his ideas. This made him feel that he was creating the designs. And he was. I didn't have to sell him. He bought."

**Stop shipping finished products to people who want to co-build.** Ship the beta. Ask for input. Let them become a stakeholder.

## It works at the dinner table too

This isn't just a sales tactic. It's how human systems work everywhere.

Paul Davis of Tulsa wanted a family vacation on the East Coast: Gettysburg, Independence Hall, Washington, Williamsburg. His wife, Nancy, had been wanting a western road trip through New Mexico, Arizona, California and Nevada for years.

Two proposals, one budget.

Davis didn't argue. Their daughter, Anne, had just finished a U.S. history class and was fired up about it. So he asked her how she'd like to see the places she'd been studying. She loved the idea.

Two nights later at dinner, Nancy announced that if everyone agreed, they'd do the eastern trip. It would be great for Anne and thrilling for all of them.

Everyone agreed. Nancy made the call. It was her decision, and that's exactly why it stuck.

## Don't pitch. Ask for feedback.

A Brooklyn hospital was building out what it wanted to be the best X-ray department in America. The doctor in charge was buried in sales reps, each one hyping their own equipment.

Think about the last time your inbox got flooded with cold DMs that all said the same thing. You tuned out every one.

One manufacturer did it differently. He wrote something like this: *Our new line just arrived. It isn't perfect. We know that, and we want to improve it. Would you look it over and tell us how to make it more useful for your profession? We'll send a car whenever works for you.*

The doctor was floored. No manufacturer had ever asked for his advice before. It made him feel important.

He was booked every night that week. He cancelled a dinner to go look at the machines.

"The more I studied it, the more I discovered for myself how much I liked it," he said. "Nobody had tried to sell it to me. I felt that the idea of buying that equipment for the hospital was my own."

He sold himself. He ordered it installed.

**The best sales pitch is a sincere request for feedback.** It flips the status game. The buyer becomes the expert, and experts love to put their name on things.

## Credit is a cheap currency. Spend it.

Emerson put it beautifully: "In every work of genius we recognize our own rejected thoughts; they come back to us with a certain alienated majesty."

Colonel Edward House understood this better than almost anyone. He was Woodrow Wilson's most trusted advisor, more influential than most of the cabinet.

His method? House explained that the best way to convert Wilson to an idea was "to plant it in his mind casually," so Wilson would start thinking about it on his own.

He found this out by accident. He'd pitched Wilson on a policy, and Wilson seemed to reject it. A few days later at dinner, Wilson pitched the exact same idea back as his own.

Did House say, "Hey, that was my idea"?

No. House cared about outcomes, not credit. He let Wilson keep it. He even gave Wilson public credit for it.

Here's the market reality: **credit is cheap, results are expensive.** If giving away credit buys you the outcome, take that trade every time. Everyone you deal with is just as human as Wilson.

## Let them sell themselves

Carnegie tells a story about planning a fishing and canoeing trip to New Brunswick, Canada. He asked the tourist bureau for information and immediately got buried in letters, booklets and testimonials from camps and guides.

Then one camp owner did something clever. He sent the names and phone numbers of several New Yorkers who'd stayed at his camp and invited Carnegie to call them and find out for himself.

Carnegie actually knew one of the men on the list. He called, got the real story, and booked his trip.

Every other camp was selling. One camp let the customer sell himself. That camp won.

Social proof you verify yourself beats any marketing copy.

## Lead from below

Twenty-five centuries ago, Lao-tse made the same point. Rivers and seas receive the tribute of a hundred mountain streams because they sit *below* them.

So the wise leader who wants to be above people puts himself beneath them. The one who wants to lead stands behind them. Then people don't feel his weight, and they don't resent his position.

That's the whole game. The most powerful operators are the ones who make other people feel like the protagonist.

<Tip>**Principle:** Let the other person feel the idea is theirs. Ownership drives commitment.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

Pushing ideas creates resistance. Planting them creates owners.

Ask more questions. Ship the unfinished draft. Give away the credit.

You don't need to win the argument. You need the outcome.
