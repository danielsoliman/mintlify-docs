#### Every negotiation is a momentum trade. Open with "yes" or lose the position.

Look, most people walk into a disagreement and lead with the disagreement.

That's backwards.

Carnegie's advice is simple: don't open with the things you differ on. Open with the things you agree on. Then keep hammering them. Make it obvious that you both want the same outcome and the only gap is method, not purpose.

**Same destination, different route. That's the frame.**

---

## Why "no" is so expensive

Here's the thing about "no." It isn't just a two-letter word. It's a position.

Carnegie leans on the psychologist Harry Overstreet here. Once someone says "no," their pride takes over. They might realize five minutes later that the "no" was dumb. Doesn't matter. They've staked their credibility on it, and now they have to defend it to stay consistent with themselves.

Think of it like a founder who publicly declared they'd never raise a down round. The market moves, the math changes, and they still fight it, because reversing feels like losing.

Overstreet goes further. A real "no" is physical. The whole body tenses into rejection: muscles, nerves, glands, all braced against accepting anything. A "yes" does the opposite. The system opens up and leans forward.

So the incentive is obvious:

- Every early "yes" builds momentum in your direction.
- Every early "no" builds a wall you'll have to tear down later.

Carnegie uses a billiard ball. A modern version: it's a flywheel. Get it spinning one way and it takes real force to slow it down, and far more to reverse it.

And yet people blow this constantly. **A lot of folks get a hit of self-importance from antagonizing someone right out of the gate.** Get a customer, a kid, or a partner to say "no" first, and you'll need the patience of a saint to flip it.

## The bank teller who stopped flexing

The fascinating thing here is how small the change is.

James Eberson was a teller at the Greenwich Savings Bank in New York. A man came in to open an account and refused to answer some of the questions on the form.

Old Eberson would have issued an ultimatum: give us the info or no account. He admits that felt good. It showed who was boss. It also treated a new customer like a problem to be managed.

This time he flipped the script. He agreed the info wasn't strictly required. Then he asked questions.

If you have money here when you die, wouldn't you want the bank to transfer it to your next of kin? "Yes, of course."

Wouldn't it make sense to give us their name so we can carry out your wishes without error or delay? "Yes."

Once the customer realized the bank wanted the information for **his** benefit, not the bank's, everything changed. He filled out the whole form. Then he opened a trust account naming his mother as beneficiary and happily answered every question about her too.

Same form. Same data. Different incentive framing.

## Thirteen years, one thermometer

Joseph Allison sold for Westinghouse Electric. There was one account the company badly wanted. His predecessor had called on it for ten years with zero sales. Allison called for three more. Thirteen years in, they finally bought a few motors, with several hundred more promised if those worked out.

Three weeks later, the chief engineer told him: "I can't buy the remainder of the motors from you." Why? "Your motors are too hot. I can't put my hand on them."

Allison had learned the hard way that arguing doesn't close. So he ran the yes play.

- If the motors run too hot, you shouldn't buy more, right? **Yes.**
- Industry standards say a properly designed motor can run 72 degrees Fahrenheit above room temperature. Correct? **Yes, but yours are hotter.**
- How hot is the mill room? **About 75.**
- So 75 plus 72 is 147 degrees. Wouldn't you scald your hand under 147-degree tap water? **Yes.**
- Then maybe keep your hands off the motors? **"Well, I guess you're right."**

The engineer called his secretary and lined up roughly $35,000 of business for the next month.

Allison's takeaway: it cost him years and countless thousands in lost deals to learn that arguing doesn't pay. Seeing it from the other side and stacking yeses does.

**He didn't win the argument. He removed the need for one.**

## The bow shop that turned a "no" into a customer

Eddie Snow, who sponsored Carnegie's courses in Oakland, got into bow hunting and wanted to rent a bow for his visiting brother. His usual shop just said they don't rent. Dead end.

He called a second shop. They didn't rent either. But the guy on the phone ran a completely different playbook:

- Have you rented before? Yes, years ago.
- Probably paid $25 to $30? Yes.
- You like saving money? Yes.

Then the offer: a complete bow set on sale for $34.95. Just $4.95 more than a rental. That's why they stopped renting. Reasonable?

Yes.

Eddie bought the set, bought more gear when he picked it up, and became a regular. The first store said "no" and lost him. The second store said "no" too, but framed it as a better deal, and gained a customer for life.

Imagine a pizza place that's out of your usual order. One says "we don't have that." The other says "you like the spicy one, right? Try this. Same heat, half off tonight." Same constraint. Wildly different lifetime value.

## Socrates ran this play 2,400 years ago

Socrates, "the gadfly of Athens," changed the course of human thought. Carnegie calls him one of the wisest persuaders who ever lived.

His method? He never told people they were wrong. Too smart for that.

He asked questions his opponent had to agree with. One admission, then another, then another, until he had an armful of yeses. Eventually the other person found themselves embracing a conclusion they'd have rejected outright minutes earlier.

That's the Socratic method. It's not a debate tactic. **It's an incentive-alignment engine.** You never ask anyone to switch sides. You just let them walk there one step at a time.

So next time you feel the urge to tell someone they're wrong, in a meeting, in a DM, in a reply thread, don't. Ask a gentle question that gets a "yes."

Carnegie closes with a Chinese proverb: "He who treads softly goes far."

<Tip>**Principle:** Get the other person saying "yes, yes" right away. Stack small agreements before you ever ask for the big one.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

"No" is a position people feel forced to defend.

"Yes" is momentum, and momentum compounds.

Find the common ground, ask the questions they can't help but agree with, and let them reach the conclusion on their own. The person who stacks the yeses wins the deal.
