#### Your idea is losing to a 15-second video. Here's the fix.

## The attention market is brutal

Look, being right isn't enough anymore.

Every idea you have is competing in the most crowded market on earth: human attention. Your proposal is up against notifications, group chats, and an infinite feed engineered by the smartest people alive to keep eyes glued.

Carnegie saw this coming decades ago. His line: "Merely stating a truth isn't enough. The truth has to be made vivid, interesting, dramatic."

The movies do it. TV does it. Every good product launch does it.

**Why don't you?**

---

## The 307-page rebuttal

Here's the thing. The best response to a bad narrative is rarely an argument.

Years ago, the *Philadelphia Evening Bulletin* got hit with a whisper campaign. The rumor told to advertisers: the paper was all ads and no news. Readers were supposedly leaving. That's an existential threat to an ad-funded business.

The paper could have published a memo. Charts. A rebuttal op-ed.

Instead, it took every piece of reading material from one ordinary day, organized it, and published it as a book called *One Day*. It ran 307 pages. The size of a hardcover. All printed in a single day and sold for a few cents.

Debate over.

**One physical object did what a thousand data points couldn't.** It made the value impossible to ignore.

## Markets reward the demo

The fascinating thing here is how consistently the data backs this up.

Carnegie points to a rat poison maker that gave retailers a window display with two live rats. Sales that week hit five times normal.

Then there's classic TV advertising. The antacid that changes the color of acid in a test tube while the competitor doesn't. The detergent that gets the greasy shirt clean while the other brand leaves it gray. The car carving through tight curves.

Nobody tells you the product works. **They show you, and your brain does the rest.**

Think about the last product launch that actually stuck with you. It probably wasn't the spec sheet. It was the live demo, the side-by-side, the moment the thing just worked on stage.

That's not fluff. That's conversion optimization.

## Pennies on the floor

Jim Yeamans sold cash registers for NCR in Richmond, Virginia. He walked into a neighborhood grocery running ancient checkout machines.

He told the owner: "You are literally throwing away pennies every time a customer goes through your line."

Then he threw a handful of pennies on the floor.

The words alone should have landed. The sound of money hitting the ground is what actually stopped the owner. Yeamans walked out with an order to replace every machine.

**The pitch was the same. The packaging changed the outcome.**

## It works at home, too

Carnegie notes the old-school suitor didn't just say "I love you." He got down on one knee. The gesture signaled commitment in a way words couldn't.

Joe B. Fant, Jr., of Birmingham, Alabama, couldn't get his five-year-old son and three-year-old daughter to pick up their toys. So he built a game. His son became the train engineer on his tricycle. His daughter's wagon became the caboose. She loaded up the "coal" (the toys), hopped in, and her brother drove her around the room.

Room cleaned. Zero lectures. Zero threats.

He didn't fight the kids' incentives. He redesigned the game so cleaning up *was* the fun.

## The calendar hack

Mary Catherine Wolf of Mishawaka, Indiana, needed time with her boss. His assistant kept stalling. A full week passed. Nothing.

So on Friday morning, she wrote him a formal letter. She acknowledged how busy he was, said it mattered, and included a fill-in-the-blank form and a self-addressed envelope:

*"Ms. Wolf, I will be able to see you on ___ at ___. I will give you ___ minutes of my time."*

She dropped it in his inbox at 11 A.M. By 2 P.M., the envelope was back, filled out by the boss himself. Ten minutes, that afternoon.

They talked for over an hour and solved her problems.

Imagine the 2026 version: instead of a fifth "just bumping this" message, you send a one-click booking link with three time slots and a one-line agenda. **You lowered the friction and made the stakes obvious.** That's the whole play.

## Same facts, different result

This is the story that should stick.

James B. Boynton had to present a big market research report on cold cream to one of the most formidable figures in advertising.

Round one, he walked in with the data. The client immediately challenged the methodology. They argued. Boynton eventually won the point, at least in his own mind. But the meeting was over and he'd gotten nowhere.

**Winning the argument cost him the deal.**

Round two, he skipped the tables entirely. While the client was on the phone, Boynton opened a suitcase and dumped thirty-two jars of cold cream on the desk. Every one a competitor the man knew. Each jar had a tag summarizing the research findings, short and sharp.

No argument this time. The client picked up jar after jar, read the tags, asked questions. He'd budgeted ten minutes. An hour later they were still talking.

As Boynton put it, he was presenting the same facts. But this time he used showmanship, "and what a difference it made."

Same data. Different interface. Completely different ROI.

Being right is table stakes.

Attention is the scarce asset.

**If you want your idea to win, stop explaining it and start showing it.**
