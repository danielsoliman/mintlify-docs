#### Stop trying to win the reply thread. You're paying for a trophy nobody wants.

## The night Carnegie got it wrong by being right

Look, Carnegie tells a story from just after World War I. He was managing Sir Ross Smith, the Australian war ace who had just flown halfway around the world in thirty days.

At a banquet in Sir Ross's honor, the guy next to Carnegie tells a funny story built around the line "There's a divinity that shapes our ends, rough-hew them how we will." He says it's from the Bible.

It's not. It's Shakespeare. Carnegie knew it cold.

So he appointed himself, in his words, "an unsolicited and unwelcome committee of one" to correct the man. Pure status play. He wanted to feel important.

The storyteller dug in. Bible. Definitely the Bible.

Sitting on Carnegie's other side was his old friend Frank Gammond, who had spent years studying Shakespeare. They put the question to him. Gammond kicked Carnegie under the table and said: "Dale, you are wrong. The gentleman is right. It is from the Bible."

On the way home, Carnegie pressed him. Gammond didn't flinch: *Hamlet*, Act Five, Scene Two. Of course it was Shakespeare. But they were guests at a celebration. Why prove a man wrong? Does that make him like you? He didn't ask for your opinion.

**"Always avoid the acute angle."**

He'd embarrassed the storyteller and his friend, all to be right about a quote.

## The data from thousands of arguments

Here's the thing: Carnegie was a pro arguer. He debated in college, taught argumentation in New York, and once planned a book on it.

After watching thousands of arguments play out, he landed on one conclusion:

**The only way to get the best of an argument is to avoid it.**

Avoid it like rattlesnakes and earthquakes.

Nine times out of ten, an argument ends with both sides more convinced they were right than when they started. You didn't move the needle. You just hardened the position.

Run the payoff matrix:

- **You lose the argument.** You lose.
- **You win the argument.** You still lose.

Why? Say you shred the other person's logic and prove they have no idea what they're talking about. You feel great. But you've made them feel inferior. You've hit their pride. Now they resent you.

Think about the last time someone "won" a group-chat debate against you. Did you change your mind, or just like them less?

Carnegie quotes the old couplet:

> A man convinced against his will
> Is of the same opinion still.

Winning on points transfers resentment, not conviction.

## The truck salesman who kept winning and never closing

The fascinating thing here is how expensive this habit gets when money is on the line.

Patrick J. O'Haire joined one of Carnegie's classes. Former chauffeur, not much formal education, and he loved a fight. He was trying to sell trucks and struggling.

The problem was obvious. Any time a prospect said something negative about his trucks, Pat went straight for the throat. He won a lot of arguments. As he put it later: "I often walked out of an office saying: 'I told that bird something.' Sure I had told him something, but I hadn't sold him anything."

Carnegie's job wasn't to teach Pat to talk. It was to teach him to stop talking.

Pat went on to become one of the star salesmen for the White Motor Company in New York. Here's how his playbook changed.

A buyer says: White trucks are garbage, I'm buying the Whose-It.

Old Pat would start attacking the Whose-It. And the more he trashed the competitor, the more the buyer defended it. **He was literally selling the buyer on the other guy's product.**

New Pat says: The Whose-It is a good truck. Fine company, good people. You won't go wrong.

Now the buyer has nowhere to go. He can't spend the afternoon insisting it's the best when Pat agrees. The conversation moves on, and Pat starts talking about what his trucks do well.

"I lost years of my life in scrapping and arguing," Pat said. "I keep my mouth shut now. It pays."

Imagine you're a founder and a prospect says your competitor is better. Every second you trash the competitor, you're running their ad for them. Concede and redirect.

## Empty victories and dead-right drivers

Ben Franklin framed it as a trade-off:

If you argue and contradict, you may win sometimes. But it's an empty victory, because you never get the other person's goodwill.

So pick your asset. **A theatrical win, or the other person's goodwill?** You can rarely hold both.

The *Boston Transcript* once ran a little piece of verse about a man named William Jay, who died insisting on his right of way. He was right, dead right, as he sped along. But he was just as dead as if he'd been wrong.

Being right is not the same thing as getting results.

## The tax inspector who just wanted to matter

Look at what happened when Frederick S. Parsons, an income tax consultant, stopped fighting.

He'd spent an hour wrangling with a government tax inspector over a nine-thousand-dollar item. Parsons said it was a bad debt that would never be collected and shouldn't be taxed. The inspector wasn't having it.

Parsons described him as cold, arrogant, and stubborn. Facts didn't work. Reason didn't work. The longer they argued, the harder the guy dug in.

So Parsons changed strategy. He dropped the argument and gave the man genuine appreciation. Something like: this is probably a petty issue compared to the real decisions you deal with. I've studied taxation, but only from books. You're learning it on the front lines. I sometimes wish I had your job.

And he meant it.

The inspector leaned back and talked for a long time about his work, the clever frauds he'd uncovered, and eventually his kids. He said he'd think it over.

Three days later he showed up at Parsons's office. Decision: leave the return exactly as filed.

**Here's what was actually going on.** The inspector wanted to feel important. As long as Parsons argued, the only way to get that feeling was to flex his authority. The moment his importance was acknowledged, the fight had no purpose. He became reasonable.

## Lincoln on the cost of contention

Buddha said hatred is never ended by hatred, only by love. Carnegie's version: a misunderstanding is never ended by an argument. It ends with tact, diplomacy, and a real attempt to see the other side.

Lincoln once scolded a young army officer for getting into a heated feud with a colleague. His point was about opportunity cost. Anyone serious about making the most of himself can't spare time for personal fights, let alone pay for the damage to his temper and self-control.

Then he gave the line that sticks:

"Better give your path to a dog than be bitten by him in contesting for the right. Even killing the dog would not cure the bite."

**Winning doesn't refund the damage.**

## How to keep a disagreement from becoming a fight

Here's the thing: avoiding arguments doesn't mean avoiding disagreement. Disagreement is signal. Arguments are noise.

Carnegie pulls a playbook from the publication *Bits and Pieces*. Here it is, upgraded:

- **Welcome the disagreement.** "When two partners always agree, one of them is not necessary." If someone flags something you missed, that's free due diligence before you make an expensive mistake.
- **Distrust your first instinct.** Your default reaction is defensiveness. That might be you at your worst, not your best.
- **Control your temper.** You can measure a person by what makes them angry.
- **Listen first.** Let them finish. Don't resist, defend, or debate. You're building bridges, not walls.
- **Look for areas of agreement.** Once you've heard them out, start with where you overlap.
- **Be honest.** Find where you were wrong and say so. Apologize. It lowers everyone's guard.
- **Promise to think it over, and mean it.** They might be right. Much better to consider it now than to hear "we tried to tell you" later.
- **Thank them sincerely.** Anyone who bothers to disagree with you cares about the same things you do. Treat them like people trying to help, and they might become allies.
- **Postpone action.** Give both sides time. Reconvene later that day or tomorrow with all the facts.

Before that next meeting, run yourself through the hard questions:

- Could they be right? Partly right?
- Will my reaction solve the problem, or just vent my frustration?
- Will it push them away or pull them closer?
- Will it raise or lower how good people see me?
- If I win, what does the win cost me?
- If I stay quiet, will this blow over on its own?
- Is this actually an opportunity?

Most conflicts are mispriced. They look like threats. They're often information.

## When two people yell, nobody's listening

Opera tenor Jan Peerce was married for nearly fifty years. He said he and his wife made a pact early on and kept it no matter how angry they got: when one yells, the other listens.

Because when two people yell, there's no communication. Just noise.

Good rule for marriages, boardrooms, and comment sections.

<Tip>**Principle:** The only way to win an argument is to not have one.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

Arguments are a negative-sum trade.

You pay with goodwill, and the best case is a win that changes nobody's mind.

Treat disagreement as signal. Treat arguments as a cost.

**Skip the fight. Keep the relationship. Close the deal.**
