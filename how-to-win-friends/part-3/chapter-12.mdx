#### Pay gets people in the door. The game is what makes them run.

## Six, seven, ten

Look, this is one of the cleanest case studies in the whole book.

Charles Schwab had a mill manager who couldn't hit quota. The manager had tried everything. Coaxing. Pushing. Swearing. Threatening to fire people. Nothing moved the number.

So Schwab asked a worker on the day shift one question: how many heats did you make today?

"Six."

Schwab chalked a big "6" on the floor and walked away. No speech. No memo.

The night shift came in, saw the "6," and asked what it meant. The boss had been in, they were told. He'd asked for the day shift's number and written it down.

Next morning, the "6" was gone. In its place: a big "7."

The day shift saw it and took it personally. So the night crew thought they were better? By the end of that shift they'd left behind a swaggering "10."

Before long, the mill that had been lagging the whole plant was out-producing every other mill in it.

Here's the thing: **Schwab didn't change the pay, the hours or the threats. He changed the scoreboard.**

In his own words: "The way to get things done is to stimulate competition. I do not mean in a sordid, money-getting way, but in the desire to excel."

## A dare can change history

The fascinating thing here is how far up the stakes this works.

Theodore Roosevelt came back from Cuba and was picked to run for governor of New York. Then the opposition found he was no longer a legal resident of the state. Roosevelt got scared and wanted to pull out.

Senator Thomas Collier Platt turned on him and asked: "Is the hero of San Juan Hill a coward?"

Roosevelt stayed in. The rest is history. One challenge changed his life and the country's future.

Al Smith pulled the same move as governor of New York. Sing Sing, the most notorious prison of its day, had no warden. Scandals everywhere. One warden had lasted three weeks. Smith wanted Lewis E. Lawes for the job.

Lawes hesitated. Fair enough. It was a political seat, a career risk with ugly downside.

Smith leaned back and smiled. "I don't blame you for being scared. It's a tough spot. It'll take a big person to go up there and stay."

That was it. Lawes went, and stayed, and became the most famous warden of his time. His book *20,000 Years in Sing Sing* sold hundreds of thousands of copies, and his push to humanize prisoners drove real reform.

**Smith didn't sell the job. He dared Lawes to be big enough for it.**

## The work is the incentive

Now let's get to the fundamentals.

Harvey Firestone, founder of Firestone Tire and Rubber, said pay alone never brought together or held good people. "I think it was the game itself."

The behavioral scientist Frederick Herzberg backed this up with data. He studied the work attitudes of thousands of people, from factory floors to the executive suite. The top motivator wasn't money, working conditions or benefits.

**It was the work itself.** If the work was exciting, people wanted to do it well.

Think about what that means. Imagine you're a founder whose team is coasting. You can throw more equity at them. Or you can post the weekly shipping number in the team channel and let two squads chase each other's record.

The first is a cost. The second is a game. Games compound.

That's what every high performer actually wants. A chance to prove their worth. To excel. To win. It's why people run races and enter pie-eating contests. The desire to excel is the desire to feel important, and it's free to tap.

<Tip>**Principle:** Throw down a challenge. Give people a scoreboard and a reason to beat it.</Tip>

## The stack, in a nutshell

That closes Part Three. Here's the full playbook for winning people to your way of thinking:

1. The only way to win an argument is to not have it.
2. Respect the other person's view. Never say "you're wrong."
3. When you're wrong, own it fast and loud.
4. Open friendly. Always.
5. Get them saying "yes, yes" from the first minute.
6. Let them do most of the talking.
7. Let them feel the idea is theirs.
8. Honestly model the trade from their side of the table.
9. Be sympathetic to their ideas and desires.
10. Appeal to their nobler motives.
11. Dramatize your ideas. Don't just state them.
12. Throw down a challenge.

---

**Bottom line:**

Threats cap out fast. Money only goes so far.

People want to win. Give them a number to beat.

When nothing else works, put a "6" on the floor and walk away.
