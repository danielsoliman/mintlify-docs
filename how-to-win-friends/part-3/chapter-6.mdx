#### The best pitch you'll ever make is the one you never give

## The talking trap

Look, most people trying to win someone over make the same mistake.

They talk too much. They walk in with a deck and spend 45 minutes broadcasting.

Here's the problem. The other person knows more about their business, their problems and their constraints than you ever will. **So ask them questions. Let them tell you things.**

And when you disagree? You'll be tempted to jump in. Don't.

Nobody processes your argument while their own ideas are still stuck in the queue. So listen patiently, with an open mind, and mean it. Encourage them to get everything out.

Let them vent. That's the safety valve.

## The deal that was won by losing a voice

Does this actually pay in business? Carnegie tells a story that answers the question better than any theory.

One of the largest carmakers in America was picking a supplier for a full year of upholstery fabric. Three manufacturers had submitted samples. Each one got a final shot to pitch the executives.

A sales rep Carnegie calls G.B.R. showed up with severe laryngitis. He walked into a room with the textile engineer, the purchasing agent, the sales director and the company president. He tried to speak. All that came out was a squeak.

So he wrote on a notepad: "Gentlemen, I have lost my voice. I am speechless."

The president said, "I'll do the talking for you."

And he did. He showed the samples, praised their strengths and, when a debate broke out, ended up arguing the rep's side. The rep's entire contribution was smiles, nods and a few gestures.

He won the contract. Over half a million yards of fabric, worth $1.6 million. The biggest order of his career.

His own takeaway: he would have lost that deal if he hadn't lost his voice. He'd had the wrong idea about the whole thing.

**Think about the incentive structure here.** When the buyer makes the case, the buyer owns the case. Nobody argues against their own conclusion.

## It works at home, too

Here's the thing: this isn't just a sales tactic. It's how humans work.

Barbara Wilson's relationship with her teenage daughter, Laurie, was falling apart. Laurie had gone from a quiet kid to an uncooperative, sometimes hostile teenager. Wilson lectured. She threatened. She punished. Zero results.

One day Laurie skipped her chores to go see a friend. When she came home, Wilson was ready to yell for the ten-thousandth time. But she was out of gas. She just looked at her daughter and asked, sadly, "Why, Laurie, why?"

Laurie asked, "Do you really want to know?"

Wilson nodded. And it all came out. Her mom never listened. Every time Laurie tried to share what she was thinking or feeling, she got hit with another order. She didn't need a boss. She needed someone to talk to.

Wilson's realization was brutal and simple: "I had been talking when I should have been listening."

From then on, she let Laurie talk. The relationship turned around, and Laurie became cooperative again.

Imagine a founder whose every 1:1 is a monologue. Nobody tells them the truth until people start quitting. Same bug, different system.

## Do the diligence, then shut up

The fascinating thing here is that listening also wins the job interview.

Charles T. Cubellis answered a newspaper ad for a role that called for unusual ability and experience. Before the interview, he spent hours on Wall Street researching the man who founded the firm.

In the interview, he opened with this: he'd be proud to join an organization with that kind of record, and he understood the founder had started 28 years earlier with nothing but desk space and one stenographer. Was that true?

That was all it took.

Almost every successful person loves to talk about the early grind. This founder talked for a long time. He started with $450 and an idea. He fought through doubt and ridicule. He worked Sundays and holidays, 12 to 16 hours a day, until the biggest executives on Wall Street were coming to him for advice.

He had a great time telling it. Then he asked Cubellis a few quick questions, called in a vice president and said, "I think this is the person we are looking for."

**That's the play.** Imagine the 2026 version: you read the founder's old podcast appearances, then ask the one question they actually want to answer. That's not flattery. It's airtime on the topic they care about most.

## Let them sell themselves

Roy G. Bradley, who ran a small brokerage in Sacramento, saw the same principle from the other side of the table.

His firm had no perks. No health insurance, no pension, no leads. Every rep was an independent agent. A strong candidate named Richard Pryor came in, and Bradley's assistant walked him through every downside first. By the time Pryor reached Bradley's office, he looked discouraged.

Bradley mentioned just one upside: being an independent contractor meant being essentially self-employed.

Then he stopped talking.

Pryor started working through the advantages out loud. One by one, he talked himself out of every objection he'd walked in with. Bradley was tempted to jump in more than once. He didn't.

By the end, Pryor had convinced himself he wanted the job. They hired him, and he became an outstanding rep.

**People don't fight conclusions they reach themselves.** If you do the convincing, you own the risk. If they do it, they own the decision.

## Let your friends win

Even your friends would rather tell you about their wins than hear about yours.

The French philosopher La Rochefoucauld put it perfectly: "If you want enemies, excel your friends; but if you want friends, let your friends excel you."

Why? When your friends outshine you, they feel important. When you outshine them, some of them feel inferior and envious.

Carnegie tells the story of Henrietta G., who became the best-liked placement counselor at a New York staffing agency. It didn't start that way. In her first few months she didn't have a single friend at work, because every day she bragged about her placements, her new accounts and every other win.

She was good at her job, but her colleagues resented her wins. So she talked about herself less and listened more. Her coworkers had their own wins, and they were far more excited to share them than to hear hers. Now she asks them about their good news and only mentions her own when they ask.

Think about the friend who drops their portfolio returns in the group chat unprompted, versus the one who asks how *your* quarter went. You know whose call you'd take.

## The principle

<Tip>**Principle:** Let the other person do most of the talking. Your silence is their runway.</Tip>

**Bottom line:**

Talking feels like control. It isn't.

The person who does the talking is the one who gets convinced.

Ask the question, then get out of the way.
