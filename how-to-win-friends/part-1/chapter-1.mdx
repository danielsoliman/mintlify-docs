#### Nobody thinks they're the villain. Price that in before you hit send.

## Look, even the worst people think they're the good guys

Start with the most extreme data point you can find.

May 7, 1931. New York City. "Two Gun" Crowley, a killer who didn't smoke or drink, is cornered in his girlfriend's top-floor apartment on West End Avenue. One hundred and fifty police officers surround the building. They chop holes in the roof. They throw tear gas. They mount machine guns on the neighboring rooftops. For over an hour, one of the city's nicest neighborhoods sounds like a war zone while ten thousand people watch from the street.

The police commissioner later called Crowley one of the most dangerous criminals New York had ever seen. A man who would kill "at the drop of a feather."

Here's the fascinating thing. While the bullets were flying into that apartment, Crowley was writing a letter "To whom it may concern." Bleeding onto the page, he wrote: **"Under my coat is a weary heart, but a kind one — one that would do nobody any harm."**

This is the same guy who, not long before, had been parked on a country road on Long Island when a cop walked up and asked to see his license. Crowley shot him without a word. Then he got out, grabbed the dying officer's revolver, and fired another round into the body.

Kind heart. Wouldn't harm anybody.

When Crowley arrived at the death house in Sing Sing, he didn't say, "This is what I get for killing people." He said, "This is what I get for defending myself."

**Crowley didn't blame himself for anything.**

---

## Here's the thing: this isn't a bug in criminals, it's the default setting in humans

You might think Crowley is an outlier. He isn't.

Al Capone, the most notorious gangster in American history, described himself as a guy who'd spent the best years of his life "giving people the lighter pleasures," and got nothing back but abuse. In his own model of the world, he was a public benefactor. An unappreciated one.

Dutch Schultz said the same thing in a newspaper interview before he was gunned down in Newark. Public benefactor. And he believed it.

Carnegie corresponded with Lewis Lawes, the longtime warden of Sing Sing, on this exact question. Lawes' answer: very few of the men in his prison regarded themselves as bad. They rationalized. They explained. They could walk you through exactly why they had to crack the safe or pull the trigger. Most of them sincerely believed they should never have been locked up at all.

So run the logic.

If the most violent people in the system don't blame themselves for anything, what are the odds that your co-founder, your direct report, your landlord, or your teenager is going to blame themselves because you pointed out their mistake?

Close to zero.

John Wanamaker, the department store pioneer, put it simply: he learned thirty years earlier that it's foolish to scold. He had enough trouble managing his own limitations without stressing about the fact that intelligence isn't evenly distributed.

Carnegie admits it took him a third of a century to figure out what Wanamaker learned early: **ninety-nine times out of a hundred, people don't criticize themselves for anything, no matter how wrong they are.**

---

## The mechanics: why criticism is a bad trade

Let's break down what actually happens when you criticize someone. It's a two-part failure.

- **Criticism is futile.** It puts the other person on defense. Their entire processing power shifts from "was I wrong?" to "how do I justify what I did?"
- **Criticism is dangerous.** It hits their pride and their sense of importance. That creates resentment, and resentment compounds.

So you pay a cost, and you don't even get the outcome you wanted.

This isn't just vibes. B. F. Skinner showed that animals rewarded for good behavior learn faster and retain more than animals punished for bad behavior. Later research found the same pattern in humans. Punishment doesn't produce lasting change. It produces resentment.

The psychologist Hans Selye framed it well: "As much as we thirst for approval, we dread condemnation."

Think about incentive design. If you're building a system and you want a behavior to repeat, you reward it. Nobody designs a growth loop around shaming users. Yet that's exactly how most people try to change the humans around them.

## The hard hat test

Here's a clean A/B test from Carnegie's files.

George B. Johnston was a safety coordinator for an engineering company in Enid, Oklahoma. His job included making sure field workers wore their hard hats. His original playbook: find someone without a hat, cite the regulation with full authority, and demand compliance.

Result? Sullen agreement. And as soon as he walked away, the hats came off.

So he changed the approach. Next time he found workers without hats, he asked if the hats were uncomfortable or didn't fit right. Then he reminded them, pleasantly, that the hat exists to protect them from getting hurt, and suggested they keep it on while on the job.

Result? More compliance. No resentment. No drama.

Same rule. Same workers. Same goal. **The only variable he changed was whether he made people defend themselves.**

## The historical receipts

Look, history is full of case studies showing criticism doesn't work. Carnegie picks two big ones.

**Roosevelt vs. Taft.** When Theodore Roosevelt left the White House in 1908, he backed William Howard Taft as his successor. Taft won. Roosevelt went to Africa to hunt lions. When he came back, he blew up. He attacked Taft as too conservative, tried to win a third term for himself, launched the Bull Moose Party, and nearly destroyed the GOP. In the next election, Taft and the Republicans carried just two states, Vermont and Utah. The worst defeat in the party's history.

Did Taft accept the blame? No. With tears in his eyes, he said: "I don't see how I could have done any differently from what I have."

Carnegie's take: he doesn't know who was right and doesn't care. The point is that all of Roosevelt's criticism didn't move Taft an inch. It just made him dig in.

**Teapot Dome.** In the early 1920s, Albert B. Fall, Secretary of the Interior under President Harding, controlled the leasing of government oil reserves at Elk Hill and Teapot Dome, land set aside for the Navy's future use. No competitive bidding. He handed the contract straight to his friend Edward L. Doheny. Doheny then gave Fall what he called a "loan" of one hundred thousand dollars. Fall even sent in the Marines to push competitors off neighboring wells. Those competitors went to court, and the whole thing blew open. The scandal wrecked the Harding administration and sent Fall to prison.

Fall was condemned about as hard as any public official ever has been. Did he repent? Never.

Years later, when Herbert Hoover suggested in a speech that Harding had died partly from the stress of being betrayed by a friend, Fall's wife jumped out of her chair, crying, and insisted her husband had never betrayed anyone. In her telling, *he* was the one who had been betrayed and crucified.

That's human nature in action. **Wrongdoers blame everybody but themselves. And we're all like that.**

Carnegie's line here is worth keeping: criticisms are like homing pigeons. They always come home. The person you go after will justify themselves and condemn you right back.

## The Lincoln playbook

On the morning of April 15, 1865, Abraham Lincoln was dying in a cheap boarding house across the street from Ford's Theatre. As he lay there, Secretary of War Edwin Stanton said: "There lies the most perfect ruler of men that the world has ever seen."

So what was Lincoln's edge with people? Carnegie spent ten years studying him and three years writing *Lincoln the Unknown* to answer that question.

Here's the surprise. **Young Lincoln was a critic.** Growing up in Indiana, he wrote letters and poems mocking people and dropped them on country roads where they'd be found. One of those letters created resentment that lasted a lifetime. Even as a lawyer in Springfield, he went after opponents in letters published in the newspaper.

Then he did it one time too many.

In 1842, he anonymously ridiculed a proud, combative politician named James Shields in the *Springfield Journal*. The whole town laughed. Shields found out who wrote it and challenged Lincoln to a duel. Lincoln opposed dueling but couldn't back out without losing his honor. He got to choose the weapons, and with his long arms, he picked cavalry broadswords and took lessons from a West Point graduate. On the appointed day, the two men met on a sandbar in the Mississippi River, ready to fight to the death. Their seconds stopped it at the last minute.

That near-death moment rewired him. Lincoln never wrote another insulting letter. Never ridiculed anyone again. From then on, he almost never criticized anybody for anything.

Fast forward to the Civil War. Lincoln cycles through generals at the head of the Army of the Potomac: McClellan, Pope, Burnside, Hooker, Meade. Each one fails, sometimes tragically. Half the country is savaging them. Lincoln holds his peace, "with malice toward none, with charity for all." When his wife and others spoke harshly about Southerners, he said: "Don't criticize them; they are just what we would be under similar circumstances."

## The letter Lincoln never sent

Now here's the ultimate stress test.

Gettysburg, July 1863. On the night of July 4, Robert E. Lee retreats south in a storm. When he reaches the Potomac, the river is flooded and impassable. A victorious Union army is behind him. Lee is trapped.

Lincoln sees it instantly. This is the chance to capture Lee's army and end the war. He orders General Meade to attack immediately, without calling a council of war. He telegraphs the order and sends a special messenger to make sure.

Meade does the opposite. He calls a council of war. He hesitates. He sends excuses. He refuses to attack. The water drops, and Lee escapes across the Potomac.

Lincoln is furious. "We had them within our grasp," he tells his son Robert, and nothing he could say or do would make the army move. He says he could have whipped Lee himself.

So he sits down and writes Meade a letter. By Lincoln's restrained standards, it's brutal. He tells Meade he doesn't appreciate the magnitude of the misfortune, that the war will now drag on indefinitely, and that "your golden opportunity is gone, and I am distressed immeasurably because of it."

What did Meade do when he read it?

**Nothing. Meade never saw it.** Lincoln never sent it. It was found in his papers after his death.

Carnegie's guess — and he flags it as a guess — is that Lincoln looked out the window and ran the scenario. It's easy to order an attack from the quiet of the White House. If he'd been at Gettysburg, seen the blood, heard the wounded, maybe he wouldn't be so eager to attack either. Sending the letter would make Lincoln feel better. But it would also make Meade defend himself, condemn Lincoln, and maybe resign, destroying whatever value he still had as a commander.

That's the trade in one sentence: **sending it relieves your feelings and destroys the asset.**

Theodore Roosevelt said that when he faced a hard problem as president, he'd look up at the portrait of Lincoln above his desk and ask, "What would Lincoln do in my shoes?" Carnegie's suggestion: next time you want to chew someone out, pull out a five-dollar bill, look at Lincoln's face, and ask the same thing.

Mark Twain had a similar system, sort of. He'd write scorching letters when someone made him angry — he once told a man the thing for him was "a burial permit." The letters let him blow off steam and did zero damage, because his wife quietly pulled them out of the mail.

Think about the 2026 version. Imagine you're a founder who just got burned by a vendor. You draft the angry email. You draft the subtweet. Maybe that's fine. **Writing it is free. Sending it is expensive.** Lincoln and Twain both understood the difference between processing your emotions and shipping them.

## Start with the one person you actually control

Do you know someone you'd like to change, fix, or improve? Great. Carnegie is all for it. But why not start with yourself?

From a purely selfish standpoint, it's a much higher return. And a lot less risky.

Confucius said it centuries ago: don't complain about the snow on your neighbor's roof when your own doorstep is dirty.

Carnegie tells a story on himself here. When he was young and trying hard to look important, he wrote to Richard Harding Davis, a famous author at the time, asking about his writing process for a magazine article. A few weeks earlier, Carnegie had received a letter stamped "Dictated but not read," and he thought it made the sender seem incredibly busy and important. Carnegie wasn't busy at all, but he signed off his note to Davis the same way.

Davis didn't reply. He just sent the letter back with one line scribbled on the bottom: "Your bad manners are exceeded only by your bad manners."

Fair? Probably. Carnegie admits he blundered. But being human, he resented it. He resented it so much that ten years later, when he read that Davis had died, the main thing in his head was still the sting of that note.

That's the real cost of criticism. **One sharp line can create a grudge that outlives the relationship.**

If you want to create resentment that lasts for decades, Carnegie says, just indulge in a little stinging criticism. No matter how justified you think it is.

## The fascinating thing: you're not dealing with rational actors

This is the core insight, and it's the one most smart people miss.

When you deal with people, you are not dealing with creatures of logic. You are dealing with creatures of emotion, full of biases, and driven by pride and vanity.

Most of us model other people like they're algorithms. Input a correct argument, receive updated behavior. That's not how the system works. The system runs on ego. If your input threatens someone's sense of importance, the output is defense, every time.

The stakes can be enormous. Harsh criticism led Thomas Hardy, one of the great English novelists, to quit writing fiction forever. Criticism drove the poet Thomas Chatterton to suicide.

Compare that to Benjamin Franklin. Tactless as a young man, he became so skilled at handling people that he was made America's ambassador to France. His secret: "I will speak ill of no man... and speak all the good I know of everybody."

Carnegie's verdict is blunt: **any fool can criticize, condemn, and complain — and most fools do.** It takes character and self-control to be understanding and forgiving.

As Thomas Carlyle put it: "A great man shows his greatness by the way he treats little men."

## The jet fuel story

Here's the cleanest case study in the whole chapter.

Bob Hoover was a famous test pilot and air show performer. He was flying home to Los Angeles from a show in San Diego when, at three hundred feet, both engines quit. He managed to land, but the plane was badly damaged. Luckily, nobody was hurt.

The first thing Hoover did was check the fuel. Just as he suspected, his World War II propeller plane had been filled with jet fuel instead of gasoline.

Back at the airport, he asked to see the mechanic who had serviced the plane. The young guy was devastated, crying as Hoover walked up. He had just cost them an expensive aircraft and nearly killed three people.

Everyone would expect Hoover to unload on him. He had every right to.

Instead, Hoover put his arm around the mechanic's shoulder and said: "To show you I'm sure that you'll never do this again, I want you to service my F-51 tomorrow."

Think about the incentives there. The mechanic already knew he'd failed. Yelling wouldn't add information. It would only add shame and fear. What Hoover did instead was make a bet on him. **He turned a liability into the most careful mechanic on the airfield.** That's not softness. That's smart risk management.

## Father Forgets

Carnegie knows parents are especially tempted to criticize their kids. He doesn't tell you to stop. He just asks you to read "Father Forgets" by W. Livingston Larned first. It's a short piece that ran in countless magazines, newspapers, and radio programs because it hit a nerve.

It's written as a father talking to his son, who's asleep. The father has just been hit with a wave of guilt and snuck into the kid's room.

He runs back through the day. He snapped at the boy for barely washing his face. Got on him about dirty shoes. Yelled when he threw things on the floor. At breakfast, more fault-finding: spilling, gulping, elbows on the table, too much butter. When the kid waved and called "Goodbye, Daddy!" on the way out, the father just frowned and told him to stand up straight.

In the afternoon, the father found him playing marbles with holes in his stockings and humiliated him by marching him home in front of his friends. That evening, the boy came into the room hesitantly. The father, annoyed by the interruption, snapped, "What is it you want?"

The boy didn't say anything. He just ran over, threw his arms around his dad's neck, kissed him, and ran upstairs.

That's when it hit the father. Constant fault-finding had become a habit. Not because he didn't love his son, but because he expected too much. He was "measuring you by the yardstick of my own years." He promises to do better tomorrow — to be a real dad, to bite his tongue, and to keep reminding himself, "He is nothing but a boy — a little boy!"

The lesson goes way beyond parenting. **Most criticism comes from judging people by a standard they haven't had the chance to reach yet.** Your new hire isn't you with ten years of reps. Your kid isn't a small adult. Price that in.

## So what do you do instead?

Here's the alternative.

Instead of condemning people, try to understand them. Figure out why they do what they do. Map their incentives. Ask what constraints they were under.

That's far more profitable — and far more interesting — than criticism. It builds sympathy, tolerance, and kindness. And it actually gives you a chance to change the outcome.

As the old saying goes: "To know all is to forgive all."

Dr. Samuel Johnson said it even better: "God himself, sir, does not propose to judge man until the end of his days."

So why should you and I?

<Tip>**Principle:** Don't criticize, condemn, or complain. It's a negative-ROI trade every single time.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

Nobody thinks they're the villain. Not Capone, not Crowley, not your co-founder.

Criticism doesn't update anyone's behavior. It just makes them defend it, and it costs you trust.

Write the angry letter if you need to. Then do what Lincoln did, and don't send it.
