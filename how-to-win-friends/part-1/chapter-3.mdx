#### Nobody cares what you want. Here's how to make them want it anyway.

## The fish don't care about your favorite snack

Look, this is the most important idea in the entire book. If you only take one thing from Carnegie, take this.

Carnegie tells a story about fishing in Maine every summer. He personally loved strawberries and cream. The fish, for some strange reason, preferred worms.

So when he went fishing, he didn't bait the hook with what *he* wanted. He dangled a worm and let the fish decide.

That's it. That's the whole game.

**Why don't we use the same common sense when we're fishing for people?**

Lloyd George, Britain's Prime Minister during World War I, figured it out. Wilson, Orlando and Clemenceau, the other wartime leaders, had faded from power. Lloyd George was still standing. When someone asked how he pulled it off, he said that if it came down to one thing, it was learning that you have to bait the hook to suit the fish.

Here's the thing. Talking about what you want is childish.

Of course you care about what you want. You care about it every waking second. But nobody else does. Everyone else is running the exact same software: they care about what *they* want.

So the only way to influence anyone is simple:

- Figure out what they want.
- Show them how to get it.

Imagine you don't want your kid vaping. Don't lecture. Don't talk about what *you* want. Show them it'll wreck their lungs and cost them their spot on the varsity team. Now you're speaking their language.

## Emerson vs. the calf

This works on kids, on customers, and apparently on livestock.

Ralph Waldo Emerson and his son once tried to get a calf into the barn. They made the classic mistake. They thought only about what they wanted. Emerson pushed. His son pulled.

The calf did exactly what they were doing. It thought only about what *it* wanted. It locked its legs and refused to move.

The family's Irish housemaid watched this unfold. She couldn't write essays like Emerson. But on this particular day, she had way more calf sense than he did.

She thought about what the calf wanted. She put her finger in its mouth, let it suck, and gently walked it into the barn.

**One of America's greatest minds lost a negotiation to a calf. The housemaid won it in thirty seconds by reading the incentives.**

## Every action is a trade

The fascinating thing here is that every single thing you've ever done, you did because you wanted something.

"Not me," you say. "I gave a big donation to charity."

Same rule. You gave because you wanted to help. You wanted to do something generous and meaningful. If you hadn't wanted that feeling more than you wanted the money, you'd have kept the money.

Maybe you gave because you were embarrassed to say no. Maybe a client asked you to. Doesn't matter. You still gave because you wanted something.

Harry Overstreet nailed it in his book *Influencing Human Behavior*. Action springs out of what we fundamentally desire. His advice to anyone trying to persuade, whether in business, at home, at school or in politics:

> "First, arouse in the other person an eager want. He who can do this has the whole world with him. He who cannot walks a lonely way."

## Andrew Carnegie's five-dollar growth hack

Andrew Carnegie, the steel magnate, started out as a broke Scottish kid making two cents an hour. He ended up giving away $365 million. He had four years of formal schooling. But he understood people.

His sister-in-law was worried sick about her two sons at Yale. They were too busy to write home. They ignored her increasingly frantic letters.

Carnegie bet a hundred dollars he could get a reply by return mail without even asking for one. Someone took the bet.

So he wrote his nephews a chatty letter. In the P.S., he casually mentioned he was sending each of them a five-dollar bill.

He didn't enclose the money.

Replies came back immediately, thanking "Dear Uncle Andrew" for his kind note and... you can finish the sentence yourself.

**Mom was asking for what she wanted. Uncle Andrew offered what they wanted. Only one of them got a response.**

Think about the last time you sent a cold DM that started with "Hey, can I pick your brain?" Now think about one that started with something the recipient actually cared about. You know which one got answered.

## The kindergarten pitch

Stan Novak, a student in Carnegie's course from Cleveland, came home one night to find his youngest son, Tim, kicking and screaming on the living room floor. Tim was supposed to start kindergarten the next day. He was refusing to go.

Stan's default move would have been to send Tim to his room and tell him he had no choice.

Instead, he asked himself a better question: "If I were Tim, why would I be excited about kindergarten?"

He and his wife made a list. Finger painting. Singing songs. Making new friends.

Then they executed. The whole family sat down at the kitchen table and started finger painting, having a great time. Pretty soon Tim was peeking around the corner. Then he was begging to join.

"Oh, no," they told him. "You have to go to kindergarten first to learn how to finger-paint."

Stan walked through the whole list, talking about all the fun Tim would have, in terms Tim understood.

The next morning, Stan thought he was the first one up. He came downstairs and found Tim asleep in the living room chair.

"What are you doing here?"

"I'm waiting to go to kindergarten. I don't want to be late."

**No threat or argument could have done that. Creating an eager want did.**

So here's your operating rule. Before you try to persuade anyone tomorrow, stop and ask:

**"How can I make this person *want* to do it?"**

## How Carnegie negotiated a 300% rent hike down to 50%

This one is a masterclass in incentive alignment.

Carnegie tells a story about renting the grand ballroom of a New York hotel for twenty nights a season to run a lecture series. One season, right after the tickets had been printed and distributed and every announcement made, the hotel told him his rent was going up almost 300%.

Obviously, he didn't want to pay it. But what was the point of telling the hotel what he wanted? They only cared about what they wanted.

So a couple of days later, he went to see the manager.

He opened like this: he wasn't surprised, and he didn't blame the manager at all. In his shoes, he'd probably have done the same. The manager's job was to make the hotel as much profit as possible. If he didn't, he'd be fired, and he'd deserve to be.

Then Carnegie drew a line down a sheet of paper and labeled two columns, "Advantages" and "Disadvantages," all from the hotel's side.

**Advantages of the rent hike:**
- The ballroom is freed up for dances and conventions, which pay a lot more than a lecture series. Locking it up for twenty nights costs the hotel profitable business.

**Disadvantages of the rent hike:**
- Instead of increasing revenue from Carnegie, the hotel loses it entirely. He can't pay that price, so he'll take the lectures somewhere else.
- The lectures bring crowds of educated, influential people through the hotel's doors. That's advertising. The hotel could spend five thousand dollars on newspaper ads and not get that many eyeballs on the property.

He handed the sheet to the manager and asked him to weigh both sides carefully before giving his final answer.

The next day, a letter arrived. The rent would go up 50%, not 300%.

Here's the kicker. **Carnegie never said one word about what he wanted.** He talked the entire time about what the manager wanted and how he could get it.

Now run the counterfactual. Imagine Carnegie storms in: "Three hundred percent? The tickets are already printed! That's ridiculous! I won't pay it!"

You know how that ends. An argument. Egos dig in. And even if Carnegie won the argument, the manager's pride would have made it nearly impossible to back down.

**Being right is worthless if it makes the other side lose face. Framing the deal around their P&L lets them win and say yes.**

## The one secret Henry Ford would bet on

Henry Ford put it perfectly:

> "If there is any one secret of success, it lies in the ability to get the other person's point of view and see things from that person's angle as well as from your own."

Carnegie liked it so much he repeated it twice. It's simple. It's obvious. And yet, by Carnegie's estimate, 90% of people ignore it 90% of the time.

Want proof? Open your inbox tomorrow morning.

## The worst cold email ever written

Carnegie shares a real letter from the head of the radio department at a big national ad agency. It went to local radio station managers across the country. Carnegie annotated it with what the recipient was actually thinking.

Here's the letter, paragraph by paragraph, with the reader's inner monologue.

**The letter opens:** our company wants to keep its position as a leading agency in radio.

**The reader thinks:** Who cares? The bank is foreclosing on my house, the market tanked, and my doctor says I have high blood pressure. And some guy in New York is yapping about what *his* company wants?

**The letter continues:** our national accounts were the backbone of the network, and we've stayed at the top year after year.

**The reader thinks:** So you're big and rich and on top. So what? I care about how big *I* am, not how big you are. All your bragging just makes me feel small.

**The letter continues:** we want to service our accounts with the latest station information.

**The reader thinks:** *You* want. *You* want. I don't care what you want. I care what *I* want, and you still haven't said a word about it.

**The letter asks:** put us on your "preferred list" for weekly station information.

**The reader thinks:** You make me feel insignificant, then ask for "preferred" treatment, and you don't even say please.

**The letter closes:** a prompt reply with your latest "doings" will be mutually helpful.

**The reader thinks:** You blast out a form letter and expect me to personally write back, *promptly*? You finally say "mutually helpful," but never explain how it helps me.

**Then the P.S.:** here's a newspaper reprint you might want to broadcast on your station.

**The reader thinks:** *Finally*, buried in the P.S., something that might actually help me. Why didn't you lead with that?

Here's the thing. This was written by someone whose entire job was persuading people to buy. If the professionals write like this, imagine what the rest of us are sending.

Swap "letter" for "pitch email" and you've seen this a thousand times. Company-first. Ask-heavy. Value buried in the footer.

## Rewriting the freight terminal letter

Another real example. The superintendent of a large freight terminal wrote to Edward Vermylen, a student in Carnegie's course and sales manager at A. Zerega's Sons, a pasta manufacturer.

The original letter went roughly like this:

- Our operations are handicapped because too much freight arrives late in the afternoon.
- That causes congestion, overtime and delays.
- On November 10, you sent us 510 pieces at 4:20 p.m.
- We request your cooperation in fixing this. Can you get your trucks here earlier or send part of the freight in the morning?
- The advantage to you would be faster unloading of your trucks and assurance your freight goes out the same day.

Vermylen said the letter had the opposite of its intended effect. It opened with the terminal's problems, which he didn't care about. It asked for cooperation without considering whether it would inconvenience him. And the only part he actually cared about, faster service for his trucks, was buried at the very end.

**Net result: antagonism instead of cooperation.**

So Carnegie rewrote it. Same facts, flipped stack:

- You've been a great customer for fourteen years, and we're grateful. We want to give you the fast, efficient service you deserve.
- We can't do that when your trucks show up with big loads late in the afternoon, like on November 10. Lots of other customers deliver late too, so things back up. Your trucks get stuck and your freight sometimes gets delayed.
- That's bad, but it's avoidable. If you deliver in the morning when you can, your trucks keep moving, your freight gets immediate attention, and our crew gets home in time to enjoy a dinner of the macaroni and noodles you make.
- Whenever your shipments arrive, we'll always do everything we can to serve you quickly. You're busy, so please don't bother replying.

**Lead with their win. Bury your problem, or better yet, reframe it as theirs.**

## The cover letter that got 11 out of 12 interviews

Barbara Anderson worked at a bank in New York. She needed to move to Phoenix for her son's health. Using what she'd learned in the course, she sent a short letter to twelve banks in Phoenix.

The structure was simple:

- My ten years of banking experience should interest a fast-growing bank like yours.
- I've worked across depositor relations, credit, loans and administration, rising to branch manager.
- I'm moving to Phoenix in May, and I'm confident I can contribute to your growth and profit. I'll be in town the week of April 3 and would love to show you how I can help your bank hit its goals.

Eleven of the twelve banks invited her to interview. She got to choose which offer to take.

Why? **She didn't say what she wanted. She said what they'd get.**

Compare that to the average job application in 2026: "I'm looking for a role where I can grow my skills." Nobody is hiring you to grow your skills. They're hiring you to solve their problems.

## Nobody wants to be sold

Carnegie wrote about thousands of salespeople pounding the pavement, tired, discouraged and underpaid. The reason? They only think about what they want.

Here's the market reality. Neither you nor I want to *buy* anything. If we did, we'd go buy it. What we want is to solve our problems.

If a salesperson shows us how their product solves our problem, they don't need to sell. We'll buy. **And people love to feel like they're buying, not being sold.**

Carnegie tells a story about living in Forest Hills, in New York. One day, rushing to the train, he ran into a local real estate agent who had worked the area for years. Carnegie asked him a quick question: was his stucco house built with metal lath or hollow tile?

The agent said he didn't know and told Carnegie what he already knew: he could find out by calling the local garden association.

The next morning, Carnegie got a letter from the agent. Did it answer the question? A sixty-second phone call would have gotten the answer. Nope. The letter repeated that Carnegie could call the association himself. Then it asked to handle his insurance.

**He wasn't interested in helping. He was only interested in helping himself.** And it showed.

## Carl vs. John: same product, different outcome

J. Howard Lucas of Birmingham, Alabama, shared a story about two agents from the same insurance company. Same territory. Same product. He called them Carl and John.

One morning, Carl stopped by Lucas's office and casually mentioned that his company had a new life insurance policy for executives. Maybe they'd be interested later. He'd follow up when he had more info.

That same day, John spotted Lucas and his colleagues on the sidewalk coming back from a coffee break. He shouted, "Hey Luke, hold up, I have some great news for you fellows!"

He rushed over, buzzing about the exact same executive policy, launched that very day. He wanted them to have some of the first ones issued. He gave them a few key facts and said someone from the home office would come out tomorrow to explain it. In the meantime, let's get the applications signed so he has more to work with.

His enthusiasm created an eager want before they even had the details. When the details came, they matched what John had said. He sold each of them a policy. Later, he doubled their coverage.

**Carl could have had those sales. He just never made anyone want them.**

## The competitive moat nobody uses

Here's the fascinating thing. The world is full of people grabbing for themselves. So the rare person who genuinely tries to serve others has a massive advantage.

**They have almost no competition.**

Owen D. Young, the lawyer and business leader, said it this way:

> "People who can put themselves in the place of other people, who can understand the workings of their minds, need never worry about what the future has in store for them."

Carnegie says that if you get just one thing from this book, a stronger habit of thinking from the other person's point of view, it could easily become one of the building blocks of your career.

## This is not manipulation

Let's be clear about something. Creating an eager want is not about tricking someone into doing something that helps you and hurts them.

**Both sides should win.** If they don't, it's not influence. It's extraction.

- **The freight terminal:** the shipper got faster service, and the terminal got less congestion.
- **Barbara Anderson:** the bank got a great employee, and she got the right job.
- **John's insurance sale:** Lucas's team got coverage they valued, and John got the sale.

That's a positive-sum trade. That's the only kind worth making, because it's the only kind that compounds.

Mike Whidden, a Shell Oil territory salesman in Warwick, Rhode Island, is another example. He wanted to be the top salesperson in his district. One service station was dragging him down. It was run by an older manager who wouldn't clean the place up, and sales were falling hard.

Mike pleaded. He had heart-to-heart talks. Nothing worked.

So he changed strategies. He invited the manager to visit the newest Shell station in the territory.

The manager was blown away by the facility. The next time Mike visited, the old station was cleaned up and sales were up. Mike hit number one in his district.

**All the talking got him nowhere. Showing the manager something he wanted got him everything.** And both of them came out ahead.

## The basketball pitch that failed

Here's the thing about education. Most people get through college mastering calculus and the classics without ever learning how their own minds work.

Carnegie tells a story about teaching a public speaking course for young college grads joining the Carrier Corporation, the big air conditioning company. One participant tried to get the others to play basketball. His pitch went roughly like this:

"I want you to come play basketball. I like basketball, but the last few times I went to the gym there weren't enough people for a game. A couple of us were tossing the ball around the other night and I got a black eye. I wish you'd all come down tomorrow. I want to play basketball."

Count how many times he talked about what *you* want. Zero.

You don't want to go to a gym nobody shows up to. You don't care what he wants. And you definitely don't want a black eye.

Could he have sold it? Easily. More energy. A better appetite. A clearer head. Fun. Competition. Actual basketball.

## Kids are the hardest negotiators

If you want to stress-test this principle, try it on a three-year-old.

One of Carnegie's students had a son who was underweight and refused to eat. The parents did what most parents do. They scolded. They nagged. "Mom wants you to eat this." "Dad wants you to grow up big and strong."

The kid cared about those pleas about as much as you care about a single grain of sand on a beach.

Expecting a three-year-old to adopt a thirty-year-old's viewpoint is absurd. The father finally saw that. So he asked himself: "What does my son want? How can I connect what I want to what he wants?"

The boy loved riding his tricycle on the sidewalk in front of their house in Brooklyn. But a bigger kid down the street kept knocking him off and taking it. The little boy would run screaming to his mom, and she'd have to go out and put him back on. Almost every day.

What did the boy want? You don't need to be Sherlock Holmes. His pride, his anger, his need to feel important all pointed one direction: he wanted to beat that bully.

So his father made him a promise. Eat what your mom gives you, and one day you'll be big enough to take that kid down.

The eating problem disappeared overnight. He'd eat spinach, sauerkraut, salt mackerel, anything, to get big enough to settle the score.

## The bed he bought himself

With that solved, the parents moved on to the next problem. The boy wet the bed.

He slept with his grandmother. Every morning she'd feel the sheets and say, "Look, Johnny, what you did again last night."

He'd answer, "No, I didn't do it. You did it."

Scolding, spanking, shaming, repeating that the parents didn't want him to do it. None of it kept the bed dry. So the parents asked the right question: "How can we make this boy *want* to stop?"

What did he want?

- First, he wanted pajamas like Daddy instead of a nightgown like Grandma. Grandma was more than happy to buy them if he'd stop.
- Second, he wanted his own bed. Grandma had no objections there either.

His mom took him to a department store in Brooklyn, winked at the salesgirl, and said, "Here is a little gentleman who would like to do some shopping."

The salesgirl played along: "Young man, what can I show you?"

He stood a couple of inches taller and said, "I want to buy a bed for myself."

When they showed him the bed his mother wanted, she winked again, and he was persuaded to buy it.

The bed was delivered the next day. That night, when his dad got home, the boy ran to the door yelling, "Daddy! Daddy! Come upstairs and see my bed that I bought!"

His father followed Charles Schwab's rule from the last chapter. He was "hearty in his approbation and lavish in his praise."

"You're not going to wet this bed, are you?"

"Oh, no, no! I am not going to wet this bed."

And he kept his word. His pride was on the line. It was *his* bed. He'd bought it. He was wearing pajamas like a grown man, so he acted like one.

**Ownership changes behavior. People protect what they feel they chose.**

## Let them cook the idea

Another student, K. T. Dutschmann, a telephone engineer, couldn't get his three-year-old daughter to eat her breakfast cereal. Scolding, pleading and coaxing all failed. So the parents asked: "How can we make her *want* to do it?"

She loved imitating her mom and feeling grown up. So one morning they stood her on a chair and let her make the cereal herself.

At just the right moment, her dad wandered into the kitchen. She lit up: "Oh, look, Daddy, I am making the cereal this morning."

She ate two helpings without anyone asking. She was invested. She felt important. Making the cereal gave her a way to express herself.

William Winter once said that "self-expression is the dominant necessity of human nature."

When you have a great idea, stop trying to get credit for it. Let the other person cook and stir it themselves. They'll see it as theirs. They'll like it. They might even go back for seconds.

Imagine you're a founder trying to get your team behind a new strategy. You can announce it from the top and get grudging compliance. Or you can lay out the problem, ask the right questions, and let the team land on the answer. Same strategy. Completely different level of commitment.

**People don't fight for your ideas. They fight for theirs.**

<Tip>**Principle:** Create an eager want in the other person. Lead with their incentives, not yours.</Tip>

## The stack, in a nutshell

These are the fundamental techniques in handling people. Three principles. One operating system.

1. **Don't criticize, condemn or complain.** Criticism triggers defensiveness and kills the deal before it starts.
2. **Give honest and sincere appreciation.** People crave feeling important. Real recognition, not flattery, is the highest-ROI move you have.
3. **Create an eager want in the other person.** Price every ask in their incentives, not yours.

---

**Bottom line:**

Nobody wakes up caring about what you want.

They care about what they want. Always have. Always will.

Stop pitching your needs. Start mapping their incentives.

Bait the hook for the fish, and you'll have the whole world with you. Bait it for yourself, and you'll walk a lonely way.
