#### Everyone you know is starving for the one asset you can print for free

## There is only one lever

Look, there is exactly one way to get anybody to do anything.

One.

**You make them want to do it.**

That's it. That's the whole game. Carnegie says it plainly: "Remember, there is no other way."

Sure, you can get "cooperation" with a weapon, a firing threat, or yelling at a kid.

But force has terrible unit economics. You get compliance until your back is turned, minimum viable effort, and people quietly updating their LinkedIn.

So if force is a bad trade, what's left?

**The only way I can get you to do something is by giving you what you want.**

So: what do you actually want?

---

## The hidden objective function

Here's the thing. Sigmund Freud said everything we do comes from two motives: the sex drive and the desire to be great.

John Dewey, one of America's most serious philosophers, put it a little differently. He said the deepest urge in human nature is **"the desire to be important."**

Write that down. It's the thesis of this book.

Carnegie lays out the short list of what most people want:

1. Health and staying alive
2. Food
3. Sleep
4. Money and what money buys
5. Life after death
6. Sex
7. The well-being of their kids
8. A feeling of importance

Most of that list gets satisfied most of the time. Except number eight.

The desire to feel important is almost as deep as hunger. Almost as urgent as sleep. And it is almost never fully satisfied.

That's a massive supply-demand imbalance.

Lincoln once opened a letter with: "Everybody likes a compliment." William James went further: "The deepest principle in human nature is the craving to be appreciated."

Pay attention to the word. Not the "wish." Not the "preference." The **craving.**

In market terms: an asset everyone craves, almost nobody supplies, and costs nothing to produce. Whoever supplies it honestly gets enormous pull. Carnegie says that person will "hold people in the palm of his or her hand," and "even the undertaker will be sorry when he dies."

---

## Why the blue ribbons matter

The fascinating thing here is that this desire is one of the clearest lines between humans and animals.

Carnegie tells a story from his childhood on a Missouri farm. His father bred prize hogs and pedigreed cattle, and they won first place at fairs all over the Midwest. His father pinned the blue ribbons to a long sheet of white muslin. When visitors came over, he'd pull it out. He'd hold one end, young Dale would hold the other, and they'd show off the ribbons.

The hogs didn't care about the ribbons.

Dad did.

**The ribbons weren't for the hogs. They were for the ego.**

That same urge built civilization. Without it, we'd basically be the hogs.

It's what pushed a broke, uneducated grocery clerk to study a set of law books he found at the bottom of a barrel of junk he'd bought for fifty cents. That clerk's name was Lincoln.

It drove Dickens to write his novels. It drove Sir Christopher Wren to design his buildings. It drove Rockefeller to pile up millions he never spent. And it's why the richest family in your town built a house way bigger than they'll ever need.

It's also why you want the new phone, the nicer car, and why you keep mentioning how well your kids are doing.

---

## Tell me how you get it, and I'll tell you who you are

Look, the desire for importance is fuel. What matters is what you burn it on.

The same urge pulls young people into gangs and crime. E. P. Mulrooney, a former New York police commissioner, said the average young criminal's first request after arrest was for the newspapers that made him look like a hero.

Today you'd call that chasing the algorithm.

Then Carnegie drops one of the best lines in the book:

**"If you tell me how you get your feeling of importance, I'll tell you what you are."**

That's your character. That's the most significant data point about you.

Rockefeller got his by funding a modern hospital in Peking to care for millions of poor people he'd never meet.

John Dillinger got his by robbing banks and killing people. When the FBI was hunting him, he burst into a Minnesota farmhouse and announced, "I'm not going to hurt you, but I'm Dillinger!" He was proud to be Public Enemy Number One.

**The only real difference between Dillinger and Rockefeller is how they chose to feel important.**

---

## Even the legends wanted the title

If you think you're above this, history has receipts.

- George Washington wanted to be called "His Mightiness, the President of the United States."
- Columbus lobbied for the title "Admiral of the Ocean and Viceroy of India."
- Catherine the Great refused to open letters not addressed to "Her Imperial Majesty."
- Mrs. Lincoln snapped at Mrs. Grant in the White House: "How dare you be seated in my presence until I invite you!"
- Wealthy backers funded Admiral Byrd's 1928 Antarctic expedition on the understanding that mountain ranges would be named after them.
- Victor Hugo wanted Paris renamed after him.
- Even Shakespeare went out and got his family a coat of arms.

That's not a bug in human nature. That's the operating system.

## When people can't get it, they manufacture it

Now it gets darker, and this is where the argument really earns its weight.

Carnegie says some people literally become invalids to get attention and a feeling of importance.

Mrs. McKinley made the President of the United States neglect affairs of state to lie beside her for hours, soothing her to sleep. She once made a scene because he left her alone at the dentist to meet his Secretary of State, John Hay.

The novelist Mary Roberts Rinehart told Carnegie about a vigorous young woman who took to her bed when life stopped offering much to look forward to. For ten years, her elderly mother carried trays up to the third floor. Then the mother, worn out, lay down and died. A few weeks later, the "invalid" got up, got dressed, and went back to living.

Carnegie pushes this to the extreme. He asked the head physician of a major psychiatric hospital why people went insane. The doctor admitted nobody fully knew. About half of cases had physical causes, but the other half showed no organic damage at all. Many of those patients, he said, found in insanity a feeling of importance they could never get in reality.

Then he told Carnegie about a patient. Her marriage had been a disaster. Her husband didn't love her and wouldn't even eat with her, making her bring his meals upstairs. No children, no social standing. In her mind, she had now divorced him, married into English aristocracy, and insisted on being called Lady Smith. And every time the doctor visited, she told him: "Doctor, I had a baby last night."

The doctor's verdict? If he could restore her sanity with a wave of his hand, he wouldn't. "She's much happier as she is."

Sit with that.

**If some people are so hungry for importance that they'd leave reality to get it, imagine what you can unlock by giving people honest appreciation right here in the real world.**

## The million-dollar soft skill

Look, let's talk about compensation, because incentives never lie.

Charles Schwab was one of the first people in American business to earn more than a million dollars a year. This was back when there was no income tax and fifty dollars a week was a good living. Andrew Carnegie picked him to be the first president of the newly formed United States Steel Company when Schwab was just thirty-eight. Later, Schwab took over a struggling Bethlehem Steel and turned it into one of the most profitable companies in America.

So why was Andrew Carnegie paying this guy more than three thousand dollars a day?

Because he was a genius? No.

Because he knew more about making steel than anyone else? Not even close. Schwab told Dale Carnegie himself that plenty of his people knew more about steel than he did.

Schwab said he was paid that much largely because of **his ability to deal with people.**

Think like an investor. The market priced "can motivate humans" far above "knows the technical product." That's where the leverage is.

Here's Schwab's secret, in his own words:

> "I consider my ability to arouse enthusiasm among my people the greatest asset I possess, and the way to develop the best that is in a person is by appreciation and encouragement."

And the key part:

> "There is nothing else that so kills the ambitions of a person as criticisms from superiors. I never criticize anyone. I believe in giving a person incentive to work. So I am anxious to praise but loath to find fault. If I like anything, I am hearty in my approbation and lavish in my praise."

---

## Most managers run the playbook backwards

Here's the thing. The average person does the exact opposite.

If they don't like something, they rip into their team. If they do like it, they say nothing. There's an old couplet that nails it: "Once I did bad and that I heard ever / Twice I did good, but that I heard never."

Imagine you're running a startup. An engineer ships a bug, and it gets dissected in a public Slack channel. The same engineer ships three clean features early, and the response is silence. You've built a system where the only feedback signal is punishment. People will optimize for not getting caught, not for great work.

Schwab said that in his wide experience with great people around the world, he had never found anyone, however senior, who didn't do better work under approval than under criticism.

He said that was one of the main reasons for Andrew Carnegie's success. Carnegie praised his people publicly and privately. He even wanted to praise them on his tombstone. The epitaph he wrote for himself: "Here lies one who knew how to get around him men who were cleverer than himself."

Rockefeller ran the same play. When his partner Edward T. Bedford lost the firm a million dollars on a bad deal in South America, Rockefeller could have torn him apart. But he knew Bedford had done his best. So he found something to praise. He congratulated Bedford on saving 60 percent of the investment. "That's splendid," he said. "We don't always do as well as that upstairs."

**That's not soft. That's protecting your best asset after a loss so it keeps compounding.**

## The hay story

Carnegie admits this next one never happened, but it illustrates the point too well to skip.

A farm woman, after a long day, serves her family a big pile of hay for dinner. They're outraged and ask if she's lost her mind. She says: "Why, how did I know you'd notice? I've been cooking for you men for the last twenty years and in all that time I ain't heard no word to let me know you wasn't just eating hay."

Carnegie cites a study on runaway wives that found the number one reason they left was "lack of appreciation." He bets the same would be true for husbands. We take the people closest to us so much for granted that we stop signaling we value them at all.

**Silence isn't neutral. Over time, silence reads as "I don't notice you."**

---

## Six roses instead of six notes

One of Carnegie's students told a story that captures this perfectly.

His wife was in a self-improvement group at church. She asked him to list six things she could do to be a better wife. He said it would have been easy to list six things he'd like to change. And she could have listed a thousand about him. But he didn't. He told her he'd think about it overnight.

The next morning, he called a florist and had six red roses sent to her with a note: "I can't think of six things I would like to change about you. I love you the way you are."

When he got home, she met him at the door, almost in tears. The next Sunday, several women from her group told him it was the most considerate thing they'd ever heard. That, he said, was when he realized the power of appreciation.

He had an open invitation to criticize. He delivered value instead. Massive return on a tiny investment.

## Feeding the body, starving the ego

Florenz Ziegfeld, Broadway's most spectacular producer, built his name on his ability to "glorify the American girl." He made performers nobody noticed feel beautiful through sheer appreciation. He raised chorus girls' pay from thirty dollars a week to as high as one hundred seventy-five. And on opening night, he sent telegrams to the stars and flooded every chorus girl with roses.

**Pay them well and make them feel seen.** Both levers. Not one or the other.

Carnegie once fasted for six days and says he was less hungry on day six than on day two. His point: most of us would feel like criminals if we let our families or employees go six days without food. But we'll let them go six days, six weeks, even sixty years without the appreciation they crave almost as much as food.

The actor Alfred Lunt, while starring in *Reunion in Vienna*, said: "There is nothing I need so much as nourishment for my self-esteem."

We feed the bodies of our kids, friends and teams. We almost never feed their self-esteem.

---

## One sentence can rewrite a life

Paul Harvey shared a story on his radio show, *The Rest of the Story*, about a teacher in Detroit. She asked a student named Stevie Morris to help her find a mouse that had gotten loose in the classroom.

She recognized that Stevie, who was blind, had extraordinary hearing. It was the first time anyone had appreciated that gift. Years later, he said that moment was the beginning of a new life. He developed his hearing, and under the stage name Stevie Wonder, became one of the great singers and songwriters of the 1970s.

**One act of genuine recognition. A lifetime of compounding returns.**

## Appreciation is not flattery

Look, some of you are rolling your eyes right now. "This is just flattery. I've tried it. Smart people see right through it."

You're right. Flattery usually fails with discerning people. It's shallow, selfish, and fake. It should fail.

Some people are starved enough to swallow anything. Even Queen Victoria fell for it. Prime Minister Benjamin Disraeli admitted he laid it on thick with her. His words: he "spread it on with a trowel." But Disraeli was one of the most skilled operators who ever ran the British Empire. What worked for him won't necessarily work for you.

**Flattery is counterfeit currency.** It might clear once or twice. Eventually it blows up on you.

So what's the difference between appreciation and flattery? Simple:

- One is sincere. The other is fake.
- One comes from the heart. The other comes from the teeth.
- One is unselfish. The other is a play for something.
- One is admired by everyone. The other is despised by everyone.

Carnegie saw a bust of the Mexican general Álvaro Obregón in Mexico City with these words carved below it: "Don't be afraid of enemies who attack you. Be afraid of the friends who flatter you."

King George V kept six maxims on his study wall at Buckingham Palace. One read: "Teach me neither to proffer nor receive cheap praise." That's all flattery is. Cheap praise.

Another definition worth keeping: "Flattery is telling the other person precisely what he thinks about himself."

And Emerson: "Use what language you will, you can never say anything but what you are."

Carnegie is emphatic: this isn't a tactic. He's talking about **a new way of life.**

If flattery worked, everyone would already be a master of human relations.

## The 95 percent problem

Here's the fascinating thing. Carnegie says that when we're not focused on some specific problem, we spend about 95 percent of our time thinking about ourselves.

Flip it. Spend some of that time thinking about other people's genuine strengths, and you'll never need flattery. You'll have real material. And real material is what lands.

Appreciation is one of the most neglected habits in everyday life:

- Your kid brings home a great report card or bakes a first cake, and you barely look up.
- You have a great meal and never tell the kitchen.
- A tired support rep goes above and beyond, and you just close the chat.

Every speaker knows the pain of pouring everything into a talk and getting zero response. Now multiply that across every office, warehouse, home and group chat.

Carnegie calls appreciation "the legal tender that all souls enjoy." That's a great way to put it. **It's the one currency every person on Earth accepts.**

Leave a trail of small, honest thank-yous wherever you go.

## The janitor turnaround

Pamela Dunham of New Fairfield, Connecticut, supervised a janitor who was doing a bad job. Other employees mocked him and even littered the hallways to rub it in. It got bad enough that productive time was being lost.

Nothing she tried worked. Then she noticed that every so often, he did a really good piece of work. So she started praising him for it, in front of everyone.

His work got better every day. Before long, he was doing everything well. Now he does an excellent job, and the same coworkers who mocked him give him recognition.

**Honest appreciation got results where criticism and ridicule failed.**

That's the whole chapter in one case study.

---

## Everyone is better than you at something

Carnegie says hurting people doesn't change them, and it's never called for. He kept an old saying taped to his mirror:

> I shall pass this way but once; any good, therefore, that I can do or any kindness that I can show to any human being, let me do it now. Let me not defer nor neglect it, for I shall not pass this way again.

Emerson said: "Every man I meet is my superior in some way. In that, I learn of him."

If that was true for Emerson, it's a thousand times more true for the rest of us.

So stop obsessing over your own wins. Figure out what the other person is genuinely good at. Forget flattery. Give honest, sincere appreciation. Be, as Schwab put it, "hearty in your approbation and lavish in your praise."

Do that, and people will remember your words for years. They'll repeat them long after you've forgotten you said them.

<Tip>**Principle:** Give honest, sincere appreciation. Not flattery, not a tactic. Real recognition for real value.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

Everyone you meet is running the same hidden objective function: they want to feel important.

Almost nobody is supplying it.

Criticism is expensive and gets you compliance. Flattery is counterfeit and gets you caught.

**Honest appreciation is free, scarce, and compounds forever.** Supply it, and you'll have more leverage than any title could give you.
