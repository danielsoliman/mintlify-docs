#### The cheapest performance upgrade on the market is a reputation

Look, here's the problem every manager eventually runs into.

Your best performer starts slipping. Deadlines stretch. Quality drops.

You have two obvious moves. Fire them, which burns the asset you spent years building. Or chew them out, which buys you resentment and a resume on LinkedIn by Friday.

Both are bad trades. Carnegie's point in this chapter is that there's a third move, and it's almost free.

---

## The mechanic who got his standard back

Carnegie tells the story of Henry Henke, a service manager at a truck dealership in Lowell, Indiana. One of his mechanics, Bill, had gotten slow and sloppy.

Henke didn't threaten him. He called Bill in and laid out the record first: you're a fine mechanic, you've been doing this for years, customers have complimented your work. Then the gap: lately the jobs are taking longer and the work isn't up to **your own** old standards.

Notice the framing. Henke didn't benchmark Bill against the shop. He benchmarked Bill against Bill.

Bill hadn't even realized he'd slipped. He committed to fixing it, and he did. He went right back to being fast and thorough.

Here's the thing: **once you've been publicly priced as excellent, underperforming becomes the expensive option.**

Samuel Vauclain, who ran the Baldwin Locomotive Works, put it simply: the average person can be led readily if you have their respect and show you respect them for some ability.

---

## Assume the virtue

The core move is this. If you want someone to improve in a specific area, act as though that trait is already one of their defining strengths.

Shakespeare said, "Assume a virtue, if you have it not." Carnegie flips it around: assume it **for** them, and say so out loud.

Give people a fine reputation to live up to, and they'll work incredibly hard rather than watch you lose faith in them.

A reputation is an asset. Nobody wants to watch their own asset get marked down.

## Marie the Dishwasher

The fascinating thing here is how far this can go.

Georgette Leblanc, in her memoir about life with Maeterlinck, describes a servant girl who brought her meals. Everyone called her "Marie the Dishwasher." She was plain, awkward, and, in Leblanc's harsh words, "poor in flesh and spirit."

One day, while Marie held a plate of macaroni, Leblanc told her point-blank: "Marie, you do not know what treasures are within you."

Marie set the dish down and said, "Madame, I would never have believed it."

She went back to the kitchen and repeated it. Nobody mocked her. People started treating her with more consideration.

And Marie changed. She began taking care of herself. Her whole bearing shifted. Two months later she announced she was marrying the chef's nephew. "I'm going to be a lady," she said.

**One sentence repriced her entire life.**

That's a person updating their own valuation on new information.

## It works on buyers and contractors, too

This isn't just a manager trick. It works anywhere you need someone to move.

**The second pitch.** Bill Parker, a food company sales rep in Daytona Beach, got turned down by a big independent grocery manager named Jack. Parker stewed on it all day, then went back that evening.

His opener: I realized I didn't give you the full picture, and I've always respected that you're willing to listen and big enough to change your mind when the facts warrant it.

Could Jack refuse a second hearing after that? Not with that reputation on the line.

Imagine the 2026 version: a founder whose seed pitch got passed on. The weak follow-up is "just circling back." The strong one is "You're one of the few investors who actually updates on new data, so I wanted to show you what I left out." You've handed them an identity, and saying no now means breaking it.

**The cup holder.** Dr. Martin Fitzhugh, a dentist in Dublin, was embarrassed when a patient pointed out that the metal cup holder at her chair was tarnished.

He didn't write his cleaner, Bridgit, a complaint. He wrote her a thank-you note for her fine work. Then he added that since two hours twice a week is tight, she should feel free to take an extra half hour now and then for "once-in-a-while" things like polishing the cup holders, and he'd pay for it.

The next morning his desk shone like a mirror. His chair was so polished he nearly slid out of it. The cup holder was the cleanest he'd ever seen.

How much extra time did she bill? Zero.

**He offered more money. She delivered on the reputation instead.** That tells you which incentive actually moved her.

## The notorious kid

There's an old saying: "Give a dog a bad name and you may as well hang him." But give him a good name, and see what happens.

Ruth Hopkins, a fourth-grade teacher in Brooklyn, opened her roster on day one and saw Tommy T., the school's most notorious troublemaker. Fights, teasing, mouthing off to teachers, getting worse every year. His only bright spot: he learned fast.

Hopkins greeted every student with a small comment. A compliment on a dress. A note about someone's drawing.

When she got to Tommy, she looked him in the eye and said: "Tommy, I understand you are a natural leader. I'm going to depend on you to help me make this class the best class in the fourth grade this year."

Then she reinforced it for days, pointing to everything he did as proof he was a good student.

He didn't let her down. Even a nine-year-old understands what it costs to default on a reputation like that.

## Why this actually works

- **Criticism attacks identity.** People defend identity. You get resistance.
- **A good reputation is an identity worth protecting.** You get effort.
- **It's nearly free.** No raise, no PIP, no drama.

If you want to change how someone behaves, this is the move.

<Tip>**Principle:** Give the other person a great reputation to live up to.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

People don't rise to your complaints. They rise to your expectations.

Tell someone who they are at their best, and most of them will spend real effort proving you right.

It's the highest-ROI sentence in leadership. Use it.
