#### Being right in public is the most expensive win in business.

## Look, GE solved a hard problem with a job title

General Electric had a problem. Charles Steinmetz was a genius of the first order when it came to electricity. He was also a failure running the calculating department.

The obvious move is a demotion. But Steinmetz was indispensable, and he was sensitive. Bruise his ego and you risk losing your best engineer.

So GE gave him a new title: Consulting Engineer of the General Electric Company. Same work he was already doing. Someone else took over the department.

Steinmetz was happy. So was GE.

**They let him save face.**

---

## Here's the thing: we skip this step constantly

Most people never stop to think about it. We push to get our way. We find fault. We issue threats. We call out a kid or an employee in front of everyone, and never price in the damage to their pride.

The highest-stakes version is the one nobody enjoys: letting someone go.

Carnegie shares a letter from Marshall Granger, a CPA whose firm was seasonal. After tax season, people got cut. The industry habit was to rip off the bandage: "Sit down. The season's over. You knew this was temporary." Fast and cold.

The result? People walked out feeling let down. Most of them stayed in accounting for life, and they had zero love for the firm that dropped them so casually.

So Granger changed the process. Before each conversation, he reviewed the person's actual work. Then he told them something real: you handled that tough assignment in Newark, you came through, the firm is proud of you, and we're rooting for you wherever you go next.

Same job loss. Totally different exit.

People left feeling better about being fired. And when the firm needed them again, they came back with genuine loyalty.

Think about it in 2026 terms. Imagine a startup doing a round of layoffs. One founder sends a two-line calendar invite and cuts Slack access mid-meeting. Another takes ten minutes per person to name what they actually did well. Both cut costs. **Only one keeps a talent pool, a reputation, and a network that still answers the phone.**

## The fascinating thing is how fast trust gets destroyed

Two students in Carnegie's course told stories that make the contrast obvious.

Fred Clark described a production meeting where a vice president grilled a supervisor with an aggressive, gotcha tone. The supervisor didn't want to be humiliated in front of his peers, so he got evasive. The VP lost his temper and accused him of lying.

The working relationship was gone in a few moments. A good worker became useless to the company. A few months later he left for a competitor, where he was reportedly doing a fine job.

That's the full cost of one public takedown: you lose the asset, and your competitor picks it up for free.

Anna Mazzone had the opposite experience. She was a marketing specialist at a food packer, running her first major assignment, a test market for a new product. The results came in and she realized she'd made a serious planning error. The whole test had to be redone. And she had no time to warn her boss before presenting.

She gave a shaky, brief report, said she'd rerun the study, and sat down waiting for the explosion.

It never came. Her boss thanked her for the work. He said errors on a first project were normal and that he was confident the rerun would be accurate and useful. **He told her, in front of everyone, that he had faith in her**, and that the problem was inexperience, not ability.

She left that meeting with her head up, determined never to let him down again.

One leader burned an employee. The other bought loyalty for the price of a few sentences.

---

## Being right doesn't give you the right

Here's the part people miss. **Even if you're right and they're clearly wrong, making someone lose face destroys their ego and gains you nothing.**

Antoine de Saint-Exupéry, the French aviation pioneer and author, put it perfectly: "I have no right to say or do anything that diminishes a man in his own eyes. What matters is not what I think of him, but what he thinks of himself."

How someone sees themselves is the asset you're actually managing. Protect it, and you can correct almost anything.

<Tip>**Principle:** Let the other person save face. Correct the mistake in a way that protects their dignity, especially in public.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

Public humiliation feels like accountability. It isn't. It's value destruction.

You can fire someone, demote them, or flag their mistake and still leave their dignity intact.

The leaders who do that keep their best people. Everyone else trains their talent for the competition.
