#### Critique is a bad trade until you price in the praise. Here's how the best operators front-load it.

## The president who buttered up a secretary

Look, Carnegie opens Part Four with a story from a friend who spent a weekend at the White House during the Coolidge administration.

Walking into the President's private office, the friend heard Coolidge tell one of his secretaries: "That's a pretty dress you are wearing this morning, and you are a very attractive young woman."

From Silent Cal, that was about as effusive as it got. She blushed.

Then came the real message: "Now, don't get stuck up. I just said that to make you feel good. From now on, I wish you would be a little bit more careful with your punctuation."

But the psychology was dead on. **People can absorb bad news far more easily after they've heard what they're doing right.**

## McKinley and the speech that couldn't ship

Here's the thing. A barber lathers before he shaves. William McKinley ran the same playbook in 1896.

A prominent Republican wrote a campaign speech he thought beat Cicero, Patrick Henry and Daniel Webster combined. It had good lines. It also would have set off a firestorm of criticism. It could not go out.

McKinley had two constraints:

- He had to say no.
- He couldn't kill the man's enthusiasm, because he needed that energy for the campaign.

So he opened with the upside. It was "a splendid speech, a magnificent speech." Nobody could have written a better one. There were plenty of occasions where it would be exactly right.

Then the pivot. Was it right for *this* occasion? From the writer's view it was sound. But McKinley had to weigh the effect on the whole party. Go home, he said, write one along the lines I suggest, and send me a copy.

The man did exactly that. McKinley edited the second draft with him, and the guy became one of the most effective speakers of the campaign.

Think about what that means in 2026 terms. Imagine you're a founder and your best engineer ships a pitch deck for a launch that would torch your brand. You can reject it in one Slack message and lose a motivated person. Or you can tell them what's strong, explain the context they can't see, and send them back to iterate.

Same no. Completely different outcome.

## Lincoln's letter to Hooker

The fascinating thing here is that the highest-stakes version of this play came from Abraham Lincoln.

The context was brutal. April 1863. Eighteen months of Union defeats. Mass desertions. Republican senators wanted Lincoln out. "We are now on the brink of destruction," he said. He could hardly see a ray of hope.

He was handing the Army of the Potomac to General Joseph Hooker, a man with real problems, at a moment when the country's fate might ride on him.

So how did Lincoln open? Not with the problems. He wrote that there were "some things in regard to which I am not quite satisfied with you." That's how he described grave faults. Talk about tact.

Then he stacked the credit first:

- You're a brave and skillful soldier.
- You keep politics out of your profession.
- You have confidence in yourself, which is valuable if not indispensable.
- You're ambitious, which within reason does more good than harm.

Only then did the hammer come down. Under General Burnside, Hooker had let ambition drive him to undermine his commander, a great wrong to the country and to an honorable fellow officer. He'd been quoted saying the army and the government needed a dictator. Lincoln's reply is legendary: only generals who win can set up as dictators. "What I now ask of you is military success and I will risk the dictatorship."

He warned that the second-guessing culture Hooker had spread would now turn on him, and closed with an order: beware of rashness, but go forward "and give us victories."

The sharpest letter Lincoln wrote as President still led with praise.

**If Lincoln could open with the upside while the Union was falling apart, you can do it in a performance review.**

## The bronze contractor in Brooklyn

Look, you're not Coolidge, McKinley or Lincoln. Does this work in normal business? Carnegie says yes and brings receipts.

W. P. Gaw of the Wark Company in Philadelphia was finishing a large office building on a hard deadline. Nearly done. Then the subcontractor making the exterior bronze work said he couldn't deliver on time.

Heavy penalties loomed. Calls and heated arguments went nowhere. So Gaw went to New York in person.

His opener had nothing to do with bronze. He said: did you know you're the only person in the Brooklyn phone book with your name? He'd looked it up that morning to find the address.

The owner lit up and talked for several minutes about his family, Dutch settlers in New York for almost two hundred years.

Gaw then called the plant one of the cleanest, neatest bronze factories he'd ever seen. The owner, who'd spent his life building it, offered a tour. Gaw praised the fabrication system and some unusual machines, which turned out to be the owner's own inventions. Lunch followed.

Not one word about the deadline.

After lunch, the subcontractor raised it himself. He knew why Gaw was there. He hadn't expected to enjoy the meeting. Gaw could go home with a promise: the material would be made and shipped, even if other orders had to wait.

Gaw got everything he wanted without asking. The building finished on the contract date.

Here's the incentive math. The subcontractor had a queue of customers. Every one of them was yelling. Gaw was the only one who made him feel respected. **When everyone else is applying pressure, appreciation is the scarce asset, and scarce assets get priority.**

## The teller who almost got fired

Here's the thing: this isn't just for big deals. It's for managing people.

Dorothy Wrublewski, a branch manager at the Fort Monmouth Federal Credit Union in New Jersey, shared this one with one of Carnegie's classes.

She'd hired a teller trainee who was great with customers and accurate on individual transactions. The problem was balancing out at the end of the day. She was slow, and it held everyone up.

The head teller wanted her gone: "I've shown her over and over, but she can't get it. She's got to go."

Wrublewski watched her the next day. Fast, accurate, pleasant. The issue was narrow.

After closing, she sat down with the trainee, who was clearly nervous. She opened by praising how friendly she was with customers and how quick and accurate her daily work was. Then she suggested they go over the cash drawer procedure together.

Once the trainee knew her manager had confidence in her, she picked it up fast. No problems after that.

The other path? Fire, rehire, retrain, maybe hit the same wall. One conversation saved all of it.

## Why the sequence matters

Carnegie's analogy: starting with praise is like a dentist using Novocain. You still get drilled. It just doesn't hurt as much.

Here's the logic stack:

- Criticism hits ego first and logic second. Lead with it, and the other person spends the conversation defending, not listening.
- Honest praise lowers the defenses. It signals you see the whole picture, not just the miss.
- Once people believe you have confidence in them, they want to prove you right.

The key word is *honest*. Fake praise gets spotted fast and burns trust. Coolidge got away with a clumsy version because the compliment was real.

<Tip>**Principle:** Open with genuine praise and honest appreciation before you raise the problem.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

Feedback is a trade. The other person has to buy what you're selling.

Lead with the problem and you get defense. Lead with real appreciation and you get attention.

**Same message. Better sequence. Way higher hit rate.**
