#### The cheapest trust you'll ever buy: admit you've blown it too

## The setup

Look, giving feedback is a trade.

You want someone to change their behavior. They want to protect their ego. If your opening move threatens the ego, the trade fails before it starts.

Carnegie's fix: **talk about your own mistakes before you point out theirs.** It sounds soft. It's one of the highest-ROI moves in leadership.

---

## Carnegie checks his own track record

Carnegie tells a story about his niece, Josephine. She came to New York at nineteen to work as his secretary. Her business experience was, in his words, "a trifle more than zero."

One day he was about to criticize her. Then he stopped himself.

He was twice her age. He had thousands of times more experience. Why would he expect her to have his judgment already? And what was *he* doing at nineteen? He remembered the blunders. Plenty of them.

His honest conclusion: Josephine's batting average was better than his had been at her age.

So he changed the opening. When she made a mistake, he'd start with something like: "You've made a mistake, but it's no worse than many I've made. Judgment comes with experience, and you're ahead of where I was." Then he'd suggest the better approach.

Here's the thing. **It's far easier to hear a list of your faults when the person delivering it admits they're not perfect either.**

## The engineer and the spelling book

E. G. Dillistone, an engineer in Manitoba, had a new secretary whose letters came back with two or three spelling errors per page. Pointing out the errors didn't work.

So he tried something else. Dillistone was a bad speller himself. For years he'd kept a little black book of words that tripped him up.

The next time a letter had errors, he sat down with her and said, roughly: this word doesn't look right, and it's one I always struggle with. That's why I started this book. He flipped to the page and showed her.

He never knew if she copied his system. But her error rate dropped significantly.

He didn't issue a correction. He shared a playbook, and led with his own weakness to make it safe to adopt.

## The Kaiser and the chancellor

The fascinating thing here is that this works even at the very top of the power ladder.

In 1908, Prince Bernhard von Bülow was Imperial Chancellor of Germany. On the throne sat Kaiser Wilhelm II: arrogant, boastful, the last of the German emperors.

Then Wilhelm blew himself up. While a guest in England, he gave remarks that ran in the *Daily Telegraph*. He claimed he was the only German who felt friendly toward the English. He said he was building a navy against Japan. He said he alone had saved England from being humbled by Russia and France, and that his campaign plan had helped Britain win in South Africa.

Europe erupted. England was furious. German statesmen were horrified.

The Kaiser panicked and asked von Bülow to take the blame. Say you advised me to say it.

Von Bülow pushed back: nobody in Germany or England would believe he could have advised the Kaiser to say such things.

Wrong move. The Kaiser exploded: "You consider me a donkey, capable of blunders you yourself could never have committed!"

Von Bülow knew he should have praised before he criticized. Too late for that. So he did the next best thing and praised *after*.

He told Wilhelm he surpassed him in many areas: naval and military knowledge, and above all natural science. He said he'd often listened in admiration as the Kaiser explained the barometer, wireless telegraphy and X-rays. He confessed he himself was hopeless at chemistry and physics. All he brought to the table, he said, was some history and a knack for diplomacy.

The Kaiser lit up. "Haven't I always told you that we complete one another famously? We should stick together, and we will!"

Later that day he declared that if anyone spoke against von Bülow, he'd punch them in the nose.

Von Bülow recovered. But even this seasoned diplomat made one error: **he should have opened with his own shortcomings and the Kaiser's strengths**, not with a line implying the Kaiser was a fool who needed a babysitter.

If a few sentences of humility can turn an insulted emperor into a loyal ally, imagine what they can do in your next one-on-one.

## You don't even have to be fixed yet

Here's the part most people miss. You can admit a mistake you *haven't* corrected, and it still works.

Clarence Zerhusen of Timonium, Maryland, found out his fifteen-year-old son, David, was experimenting with cigarettes. The problem: Zerhusen and his wife both smoked. They were the bad example.

So he didn't lecture. No threats, no health warnings. He told David he'd started smoking at about the same age, that nicotine had taken hold, and that now he could barely quit. He reminded David of his irritating cough, and how David used to beg him to stop.

David thought it over and decided not to smoke until he finished high school. Years later, he still had never started and had no plans to.

And the conversation changed the father too. Zerhusen decided to quit, and with his family's support, he did.

That's the compounding effect: honesty about your flaws can move you, too.

---

## Why this works

Strip it down to incentives:

- **Criticism triggers defense.** When you open with their mistake, they spend all their energy protecting status instead of processing the information.
- **Self-disclosure lowers the cost of admitting error.** If the boss has screwed up too, then screwing up isn't fatal. It's just part of the learning curve.
- **It signals you're on the same side.** You're not the judge. You're a few steps further down the same road.

Imagine you're a founder and a junior engineer just shipped a bug to production. You can open with "How did this get past review?" Or you can open with "I took down prod twice in my first year. Here's what I learned." Same feedback, very different result.

<Tip>**Principle:** Talk about your own mistakes before you criticize anyone else's.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

Humility isn't weakness. It's a pricing strategy for feedback.

Pay the cost of admitting your own mistakes first, and the other person can afford to hear theirs.

Lead with your losses. Then make the call.
