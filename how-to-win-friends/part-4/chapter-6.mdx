#### Your team is a flywheel. Praise is the fuel.

## The dog trainer knows something you don't

Look, this one starts with a dog.

Carnegie tells a story about his old friend Pete Barlow, who ran a dog-and-pony act and spent his life on the road with circuses and vaudeville shows. Carnegie loved watching Pete train new dogs.

The moment a dog showed even the slightest improvement, Pete went all in. Pats. Praise. Meat. A big show about it.

Animal trainers have run that playbook for centuries. So here's Carnegie's question, and it's a good one: **why do we use more common sense training dogs than we do leading people?**

Think about the incentive design. If all feedback is negative, the best move is to stay invisible. If you reward progress, the best move is to keep improving.

**Praise even the slightest improvement. That's what keeps the improvement coming.**

The psychologist Jess Lair put it this way: "Praise is like sunlight to the warm human spirit; we cannot flower and grow without it." And yet most of us hand out the cold wind of criticism for free while hoarding praise like it costs us something.

It doesn't. It's the cheapest asset you own.

## A few words can reprice an entire life

Here's the thing. Carnegie says he can look back at his own life and point to moments where a few words of praise changed his whole trajectory. You can probably do the same.

History backs him up. Three stories.

**Caruso.** A ten-year-old boy worked in a factory in Naples and dreamed of singing. His first teacher shut it down: "You can't sing. You haven't any voice at all. It sounds like the wind in the shutters."

His mother, a poor peasant woman, disagreed. She told him she knew he could sing and could already hear him improving. She went barefoot to save money for his lessons.

That boy was Enrico Caruso. He became the greatest and most famous opera singer of his era.

**Dickens.** In early 19th-century London, a young man wanted to write. Everything was stacked against him. Four years of school, total. His father was in debtors' prison. He pasted labels on bottles in a rat-infested warehouse and slept in a grim attic with two street kids.

He mailed his first manuscript in the dead of night so nobody would laugh at him. Rejection after rejection. Then one story got accepted. He wasn't paid a cent. But one editor praised him.

He wandered the streets in tears. That one signal kept him out of the factories for good. His name was Charles Dickens.

**H. G. Wells.** Another London kid worked as a clerk in a dry-goods store. Fourteen-hour days of drudgery. After two years, he walked fifteen miles without breakfast to beg his mother to get him out. He swore he'd kill himself if he had to stay.

Then he wrote a desperate letter to his old schoolmaster. The schoolmaster wrote back with a little praise, told him he was genuinely intelligent and built for better things, and offered him a teaching job.

That boy went on to write a stack of bestsellers and earn over a million dollars with his pen.

Notice the pattern. Nobody handed over capital or connections. **They handed over belief.** And that belief was the seed round for everything that followed.

## The science says reinforce the signal

The fascinating thing here is that this isn't just feel-good stuff. It's mechanics.

B. F. Skinner built much of his work on it. His experiments with animals and humans showed that when you minimize criticism and emphasize praise, the good behavior gets reinforced and the bad behavior withers from lack of attention.

Translate that into 2026 terms: **whatever you pay attention to, you get more of.** Attention is the reward function. If you only show up when something breaks, you're optimizing for breakage.

John Ringelspaugh of Rocky Mount, North Carolina, ran this experiment at home. Like a lot of families, he and his wife mostly communicated with their kids by yelling. Every yelling session made the kids a little worse. And the parents too.

So they flipped the strategy. Praise instead of harping on faults.

It wasn't easy to find anything to praise. But they found something. Within a day or two, some of the most upsetting behavior stopped. Then other problems faded. The kids started going out of their way to do things right.

It didn't stay perfect. But the new baseline was far better than the old one.

That's the whole game. You don't need a perfect outcome. You need a better baseline.

## Specific beats generic every time

Look, this works at work too.

Keith Roper of Woodland Hills, California, got some exceptionally high-quality material back from his print shop. The printer who did the job was a new hire who'd been struggling to adjust. His supervisor thought he had a bad attitude and was close to firing him.

Roper heard about it and walked over to the shop himself. He told the young man how pleased he was. He said it was the best work he'd seen from that shop in a long time. And he explained **exactly why** it was superior and why that contribution mattered to the company.

Within days, he turned around completely, telling coworkers that someone actually noticed good work. From then on, he was loyal and dedicated.

Think about the last time someone replied to your work with "nice job" in a Slack thread. Now think about the last time someone told you exactly which decision you made was the right one and why. Only one of those stuck.

**Generic praise reads as noise. Specific praise reads as signal.** It proves you actually paid attention.

## This is not a growth hack

Here's the thing. Everyone craves recognition. But nobody wants flattery. Nobody wants the fake stuff.

Carnegie is blunt: this only works when it comes from the heart. It's not a bag of tricks. It's a way of living.

If you treat praise like a manipulation tactic, people will smell it instantly. The market for sincerity is extremely efficient.

---

## Most people are running at half capacity

Now zoom out. Carnegie argues that if you help people see the hidden talent they already have, you don't just change them. You transform them.

Sounds like hype? William James, one of America's great psychologists, wrote that "compared with what we ought to be, we are only half awake," and that people habitually fail to use powers they already possess.

That's an enormous amount of untapped upside sitting on everyone's balance sheet.

Imagine you're a founder with a ten-person team, each running at half capacity. You may not need to hire your way to 2x output. You may just need to notice what they get right.

**Abilities wither under criticism. They blossom under encouragement.**

<Tip>**Principle:** Praise the slightest improvement, and praise every improvement. Be "hearty in your approbation and lavish in your praise." Reward progress early and often, and be specific about it.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

Criticism is a cost. Praise is an investment.

Reward the smallest wins and they compound into big ones.

Be specific, be sincere, and you'll unlock the half of people's potential that's still sitting idle.
