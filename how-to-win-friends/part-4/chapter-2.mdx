#### The best feedback doesn't feel like feedback. It feels like a nudge.

## Look, everyone already knows the rule

Charles Schwab is walking through one of his steel mills at noon. He finds a group of workers smoking. Right above their heads: a "No Smoking" sign.

The obvious move is to point at the sign and ask, "Can't you read?"

Schwab doesn't. He walks over, hands each man a cigar, and says he'd appreciate it if they smoked these outside.

Think about the incentives here. The workers knew they broke the rule. Schwab knew they knew. Saying it out loud adds zero new information. All it does is make them feel small.

**Instead, he gave them a gift and let them correct themselves.** They walked away admiring the boss instead of resenting him.

John Wanamaker ran the same play in his Philadelphia store. He saw a customer standing at a counter, ignored, while the clerks laughed in a huddle at the far end. He didn't lecture anyone. He slipped behind the counter, served the woman himself, and handed the purchase to the clerks to wrap.

Message received. No speech required.

## Here's the thing: actions are louder than memos

Carl Langford, longtime mayor of Orlando, kept telling his staff he had an "open-door" policy. Yet residents who called kept getting blocked by assistants.

So he took the door off his office.

That's it. His aides got the message, and his administration stayed genuinely open from then on.

Imagine you're a founder who keeps saying "we're transparent" while every decision happens in a private channel. Another all-hands about transparency won't fix it. Making the channel public will.

**When the system contradicts your words, change the system.**

---

## The three-letter word that kills your credibility

Most people deliver criticism as a sandwich: sincere praise, then "but," then the hit.

"We're proud of you for raising your grades this term, Johnnie. But if you'd worked harder on algebra, you'd have done better."

Johnnie feels good right up until "but." Then he discounts everything before it. The praise now looks like a setup, a contrived lead-in to the real message: you failed.

Credibility tanks. Behavior doesn't change.

The fix is almost too simple. Swap "but" for "and."

"We're proud of you for raising your grades this term, and if you keep up the same effort next term, your algebra grade can catch up with the rest."

Now the praise stands on its own. There's no hidden failure signal. You've pointed at the behavior you want, and Johnnie has a reason to live up to it.

Think about the last time a manager's Slack DM started with "Great work on the launch, but..." You didn't read the first half. Nobody does.

## The fascinating thing is how well indirect signals work

Marge Jacob, a student in one of Carnegie's classes, had builders adding onto her house. They did excellent work. They also left the yard covered in scrap lumber every night.

She didn't want to antagonize a great crew. So one evening she and her kids cleaned up and stacked the debris themselves. The next morning she told the foreman how pleased she was with how clean the lawn had been left.

From then on, the crew stacked the scraps every day. The foreman even checked in for her approval.

Master Sergeant Harley Kaiser had a classic problem: Army reservists who saw themselves as civilians and hated regulation haircuts. An old-school sergeant might yell. Kaiser told them they were leaders who had to set the example, said he was getting his own hair cut that day (even though it was shorter than theirs), and invited them to look in the mirror and decide.

Several went to the barbershop that afternoon.

Then there's Lyman Abbott. In 1887, he was asked to fill the pulpit after Henry Ward Beecher died. He polished a written sermon for days and read it to his wife. It was dull, like most written speeches.

She could have torn it apart. Instead, she said it would make an excellent article for the *North American Review*.

Praise and a verdict in one sentence. Abbott got it, tore up the manuscript, and preached without notes.

<Tip>**Principle:** Flag people's mistakes indirectly. Let them spot the problem and fix it themselves.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

Direct criticism makes people defend themselves. Indirect signals give them room to change.

Kill the "but." Lead by example. Change the system instead of shouting at it.

The goal is to fix the problem, not to win the argument.
