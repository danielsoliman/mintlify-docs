#### Stop issuing orders. Start asking questions.

## The executive who never gave an order

Look, here is a data point worth your time.

Carnegie tells a story about having dinner with Ida Tarbell, the great biographer. While she was researching Owen D. Young, she interviewed a man who had shared an office with Young for three years.

In all that time, he never heard Young give a direct order. Not once.

No "do this." No "don't do that." Instead:

- "You might consider this."
- "Do you think that would work?"
- "What do you think of this?"
- "Maybe if we phrased it this way, it would be better."

Young let people do the work and learn from their mistakes.

**This isn't softness. It's a better operating system.** It lets people fix errors without losing face. It protects their pride. It turns potential rebellion into cooperation.

## Orders carry a hidden cost

Here's the thing. A harsh order can create resentment that lasts a long time, even when the order is completely justified.

Dan Santarelli, a teacher at a vocational school in Pennsylvania, shared a story with one of Carnegie's classes. A student had illegally parked his car in the entrance to one of the school's shops. Another instructor stormed into the classroom and demanded to know whose car it was. Then he threatened to chain it up and drag it out of there.

The student was wrong.

But from that day on, the whole class made that instructor's life miserable.

Think about the incentives. He won the argument and lost the room. One friendly question, plus a note that other cars needed to get through, would have gotten the car moved with zero blowback.

**Being right doesn't make the order free. You pay for it in goodwill.**

## Questions turn employees into co-owners

The fascinating thing here is that questions don't just make a request easier to swallow. They unlock creativity.

People back the decisions they helped make.

Ian Macdonald ran a small precision machine-parts plant in Johannesburg. A huge order landed on his desk, but the deadline looked impossible given the work already in the shop.

He didn't lean on his team to grind harder. He brought everyone together, laid out the situation, and explained what the deal would mean for the company and for them. Then he asked:

- Is there anything we can do to handle this order?
- Can anyone think of a different way to run it through the shop?
- Could we adjust hours or assignments to make it work?

The team flooded him with ideas, pushed him to take the deal, and delivered on time.

That's the difference between labor and buy-in. **Orders rent effort. Questions create owners.**

Imagine you're a founder staring down a brutal launch date. You can post the deadline in Slack and demand it gets hit. Or you can lay out the stakes and ask the team how to win. The first gets you compliance. The second gets you ideas you'd never have had alone.

<Tip>**Principle:** Ask questions instead of giving direct orders.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

Nobody wants to be bossed around.

Commands create resentment. Questions create ownership.

If you want people to run through walls for you, let them help design the plan.
