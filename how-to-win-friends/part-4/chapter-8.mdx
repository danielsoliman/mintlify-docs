#### Nobody invests in a position they've been told is worthless

## The two teachers

Look, Carnegie tells a story about a bachelor friend, around forty, whose fiancée talked him into dance lessons. He was still dancing like it was twenty years earlier.

**Teacher number one** told him the truth. Everything was wrong. Forget it all and start over.

He quit. Immediately.

**Teacher number two** said his style was a little old-fashioned, but the fundamentals were solid. A few new steps and he'd be fine. She kept praising what he did right and shrinking his mistakes. "You have a natural sense of rhythm."

He knew he was a fourth-rate dancer. He was literally paying her. He still wanted to believe her, and he got better because of it.

---

## Here's the thing about incentives

The first teacher priced the trade so badly that nobody rational would take it. Massive effort, total reset, no visible upside. Nobody wires money into that pitch deck.

Carnegie's point is simple. Tell your kid, your partner, or your employee that they're dumb at something, have no gift for it, and are doing it all wrong, and **you've destroyed almost every incentive to improve.**

Flip it:

- Be generous with encouragement.
- Make the thing look easy to do.
- Show you believe they can do it.
- Tell them they have an undeveloped flair for it.

Do that, and they'll practice until sunrise.

Imagine you're a founder and an engineer ships a messy first version. "This is a disaster, start over" versus "the core logic is right, tighten two things and ship." Same bug list. Completely different ROI calculation.

**Effort follows expected return. Your job is to make the return look reachable.**

## The bridge table

The fascinating thing here is how fast this works, even on people who swear they're not interested.

Carnegie spent a weekend with broadcaster Lowell Thomas, who invited him into a bridge game. Hard no. Carnegie knew nothing about bridge. Thomas didn't argue. He reframed.

"There is nothing to bridge except memory and judgment. You've written articles on memory. Bridge will be a cinch for you."

Before he knew it, Carnegie was at a bridge table for the first time in his life. Thomas didn't teach him a rule. He lowered the perceived cost of entry and tied it to something Carnegie was already good at.

## The guy who built a bridge empire

Now look at Ely Culbertson, whose bridge books sold over a million copies in a dozen languages. He almost never got there.

When he came to America in 1922, he tried to land a job teaching philosophy and sociology. Failed. Tried selling coal. Failed. Tried selling coffee. Failed again.

At bridge he was mediocre and stubborn. He ran so many post-mortems on every hand that nobody wanted to play with him.

Then he met a bridge teacher, Josephine Dillon. They fell in love and married. She noticed how carefully he analyzed his cards, and she convinced him he was a potential genius at the table.

Culbertson told Carnegie it was that encouragement, and that alone, that made him turn bridge into a profession.

The trait everyone else found annoying, she framed as an edge. **One person repriced his weakness as a strength, and it changed his career.**

## The flash card system

This is the most powerful story in the chapter.

Clarence M. Jones, an instructor in Carnegie's course in Cincinnati, told how this principle changed his son's life.

In 1970, his fifteen-year-old son David came to live with him. A 1958 car accident had left a bad scar on David's forehead. After the divorce he lived in Dallas, where school administrators, possibly because of the scar, decided he was brain-injured and put him in slow-learner classes.

At fifteen he was two years behind. He didn't know his multiplication tables, added on his fingers, and could barely read.

One bright spot: **he loved working on radios and TVs.** His dad found the hook. You want to be a TV technician? You need math.

Here's how he built the system:

- Four sets of flash cards: multiplication, division, addition, subtraction.
- Right answers went to a discard pile. Misses went to a repeat pile, with the correct answer given.
- Every right answer got a big reaction, especially on cards David had missed before.
- Every night, they ran the repeat pile until it was empty.
- Every night, a stopwatch.

The goal: all cards, zero mistakes, under eight minutes. Hit that and the nightly drill was over.

To David, that looked impossible. Night one took 52 minutes. Then 48. Then 45, 44, 41, then under 40.

They celebrated every drop with hugs and a family jig. By month's end, David was under eight minutes, perfect. Every small gain made him ask to go again.

He had made what Jones called "the fantastic discovery that learning was easy and fun."

---

## The compounding effect

This is where it gets wild.

Once David could multiply, algebra got easier. He brought home his first-ever B in math. His reading took off. Then he built a complex set of lever models for a science exhibit that won first prize at his school and third in all of Cincinnati.

This was a kid who'd flunked two grades, been labeled "brain-damaged," and been called "Frankenstein" by classmates. From late eighth grade through high school, he made the honor roll every time and joined the National Honor Society.

The system didn't change David's brain. It changed his expected return on effort. **Small, visible wins, celebrated loudly, compound into identity.** Each win makes the next one cheaper.

---

## What this means for you

Stop showing people how far they are from the finish line. Show them how close the next step is. Connect the new skill to something they already do well, shrink the first win, and celebrate every gain.

<Tip>**Principle:** Use encouragement. Make the fault look easy to fix, so improving feels like a trade worth taking.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

People don't quit because the work is hard.

They quit because the payoff looks out of reach.

Make the next step look winnable, and they'll take it. Then the one after that.
