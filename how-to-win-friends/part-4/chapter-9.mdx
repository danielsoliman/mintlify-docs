#### Anyone can give an order. Leaders make people want to say yes.

## Look, the "no" is where most people lose

It's 1915. Europe is tearing itself apart. Woodrow Wilson wants to send a personal peace emissary to talk to the warring powers.

His secretary of state, William Jennings Bryan, badly wants the job. It's his shot at history.

Wilson picks someone else: his close friend and advisor, Colonel Edward M. House. Now House has to deliver the bad news to Bryan without making an enemy.

Here's how House played it. He told Bryan the President thought it would be unwise for anyone to go officially. If Bryan went, it would attract too much attention. People would wonder why he was there.

Read that again. **House basically told Bryan he was too important for the job.** And Bryan walked away satisfied.

That's the rule: **always make the other person happy about doing the thing you suggest.** Even when the thing you suggest is "accept that you didn't get it."

---

## Here's the thing: framing is a product decision

Wilson used the same play when he asked William Gibbs McAdoo to be Secretary of the Treasury. That's about the highest honor a president can hand out. And Wilson still framed it so McAdoo felt doubly important.

In McAdoo's words, Wilson "created the impression that by accepting this great honor I would be doing him a favor."

Think about that. The person with all the leverage positioned himself as the one asking for help. Zero cost to Wilson. Huge upside in loyalty.

Now the flip side. Wilson didn't always run this play, and it cost him.

When he took the United States into the League of Nations fight, he left prominent Republicans like Elihu Root, Charles Evans Hughes and Henry Cabot Lodge at home. He brought unknown men from his own party to the peace conference instead. He snubbed the opposition. He refused to let them feel the League was partly their idea.

No ownership, no buy-in. Carnegie argues that this one failure of human relations wrecked Wilson's career, ruined his health, kept America out of the League and changed the course of history.

**When you cut people out of the cap table, don't be surprised when they vote against you.**

## The fascinating thing here is it works at every scale

This isn't just for presidents.

Dale O. Ferrier of Fort Wayne, Indiana, had a son, Jeff, whose chore was picking up pears from under the tree before the lawn got mowed. Jeff hated it. The job either didn't get done or got done badly.

Instead of a standoff, Ferrier offered a deal. One dollar for every bushel basket of pears. But one dollar off for every pear left on the ground afterward.

The result? Jeff picked up every pear. Ferrier then had to watch him to make sure he wasn't pulling pears off the tree to fill extra baskets.

That's incentive design in one backyard. **Same chore, new payoff structure, totally different behavior.** (And yes, the kid immediately found the exploit. Every good system gets gamed.)

Carnegie also describes a man he knew who had to turn down a steady stream of speaking invitations, many from friends and people he owed. He didn't just say "I'm too busy." He thanked them, said he was sorry he couldn't do it, and immediately suggested another speaker.

Think about the last time someone declined your DM with a referral instead of a flat no. You barely registered the rejection. You were already thinking about the next person. **Don't leave people holding a "no." Hand them a next step.**

## Look, titles are cheap. That's the point.

Gunter Schmidt managed a food store in West Germany. One employee kept failing to put the right price tags on the shelves. Customers complained. Reminders and confrontations went nowhere.

So Schmidt called her in and named her Supervisor of Price Tag Posting for the entire store. She was now responsible for every shelf.

Her attitude flipped. She did the job well from then on.

Childish? Maybe. That's what critics said about Napoleon when he created the Legion of Honor, handed out 15,000 crosses, made eighteen generals "Marshals of France" and called his troops the "Grand Army." People said he was giving toys to battle-hardened veterans.

Napoleon's reply: **"Men are ruled by toys."**

Carnegie's friend Mrs. Ernest Gent of Scarsdale, New York, had boys tearing up her lawn. Criticism failed. Coaxing failed. So she took the worst offender, named him her "detective" and put him in charge of keeping trespassers off.

Problem solved. Her detective built a bonfire, heated an iron red hot and threatened to brand any kid who stepped on the grass.

Imagine you're a founder whose most chaotic early employee keeps breaking the deploy process. You can write another angry Slack message. Or you can make them the owner of release quality. **Ownership turns your biggest problem into your strongest enforcer.**

## Here's the thing: this isn't manipulation if you run it honestly

Carnegie gives leaders a checklist for changing someone's attitude or behavior:

- **Be sincere.** Don't promise what you can't deliver. Focus on their benefit, not yours.
- **Know exactly what you want** the other person to do.
- **Be empathetic.** Ask what the other person actually wants.
- **Map the benefits** they'll get from doing what you suggest.
- **Match those benefits** to their wants.
- **Frame the request** so they see how they personally win.

Carnegie's example is a stockroom. The curt version: "John, customers are coming tomorrow. Sweep the stockroom, stack the shelves, polish the counter."

The better version explains the why. The job has to get done eventually, so doing it now means it won't hang over him later. Customers are touring tomorrow. A clean stockroom makes the company look sharp, and John gets credit for his part in that.

Will John be thrilled? Probably not. But he'll be happier than with the bare order, especially if he takes pride in his work.

Carnegie is realistic here. You won't win every time. **But if these approaches improve your hit rate by even 10 percent, you're a 10 percent better leader.** That's the ROI, and it compounds.

<Tip>**Principle:** Make the other person glad to do what you suggest. Frame every ask so they win too.</Tip>

## The stack, in a nutshell

A leader's job often means changing people's attitudes and behavior. Here's the full playbook from this part:

1. **Lead with praise and honest appreciation.** Earn the right to give feedback.
2. **Flag mistakes indirectly.** Let people spot the problem and fix it themselves.
3. **Own your mistakes first.** Talk about where you messed up before critiquing anyone else.
4. **Ask questions instead of giving orders.** People execute ideas they feel they own.
5. **Let the other person save face.** Never win the argument and lose the person.
6. **Praise every improvement, even the smallest.** Be "hearty in your approbation and lavish in your praise."
7. **Give people a great reputation to live up to.** They'll work to protect it.
8. **Use encouragement.** Make the fix look easy.
9. **Make people glad to do what you suggest.** Align the incentives so everyone wins.

---

**Bottom line:**

Orders get compliance. Aligned incentives get commitment.

Give people status, ownership and a clear reason to care.

The best ask is the one the other person is happy to say yes to.
