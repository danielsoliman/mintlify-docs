#### The most underpriced asset in the world is making someone feel like they matter

## The post office trade

Look, let's start with the smallest possible transaction.

Carnegie tells a story about standing in line at a New York post office to register a letter. The clerk behind the counter looked bored out of his mind. Weighing envelopes. Selling stamps. Making change. Same loop, year after year.

So Carnegie ran an experiment. He decided he was going to make that clerk like him.

His logic was simple. To make someone like you, you have to say something nice. Not about yourself. About them. So he asked himself one question: **"What is there about this person that I can honestly admire?"**

With strangers, that's sometimes hard. Here it was easy. The guy had a great head of hair.

So while the clerk weighed the envelope, Carnegie said, with real enthusiasm, "I certainly wish I had your head of hair."

The clerk looked up, half-startled, and lit up. "Well, it isn't as good as it used to be," he said. Carnegie told him it was still magnificent. They chatted. And the last thing the clerk said was: "Many people have admired my hair."

Carnegie bet the guy walked on air all day.

Total cost of the trade: one sentence. Total value delivered: one person's entire day.

---

## "What did you want out of him?"

Here's the thing.

When Carnegie told this story in public, a man came up afterward and asked: "What did you want to get out of him?"

Carnegie was furious. His answer, roughly: if we're so selfish that we can't hand out a little honest appreciation without demanding something back, we deserve the failure we're going to get.

But notice what he says next. He admits he *did* want something. He wanted something priceless, and he got it: the feeling of having done something for someone who couldn't do anything for him in return.

Expecting a clear payout from every interaction is a fine model for a term sheet. It's a terrible model for life.

**Some of the best returns you'll ever get don't show up on any P&L.**

---

## The one law that runs the whole system

The fascinating thing here is that Carnegie thinks there's essentially one law of human conduct. Obey it and you'll almost never get into trouble. Break it and you'll be in trouble constantly.

**The law: always make the other person feel important.**

This isn't a new idea Carnegie dreamed up. It's the oldest code in the human stack.

- John Dewey said the desire to be important is the deepest urge in human nature.
- William James said the deepest principle in human nature is the craving to be appreciated.
- Zoroaster taught it in Persia 2,500 years ago.
- Confucius taught it in China.
- Lao-tse taught it to his disciples.
- Buddha preached it on the banks of the Ganges.
- The sacred books of Hinduism taught it a thousand years before that.
- Jesus summed it up in one line: "Do unto others as you would have others do unto you."

When every major philosophical and religious tradition independently converges on the same rule, that's not a coincidence. That's a signal.

Think about what you actually want: approval, your real worth recognized, the feeling that you matter in your little world. Not cheap flattery. Sincere appreciation. As Charles Schwab put it, you want people to be "hearty in their approbation and lavish in their praise."

Everyone else wants the exact same thing.

So when do you apply the Golden Rule? **All the time. Everywhere.**

## Handing out the cash box

Look at how a guy named David G. Smith of Eau Claire, Wisconsin, handled a messy situation, which he shared with one of Carnegie's classes.

He'd been asked to run the refreshment booth at a charity concert. When he showed up, he found two older women standing at the stand, both in a terrible mood. Each one thought she was in charge.

Then a committee member walked up, handed Smith the cash box, thanked him for taking over, introduced the two women as his "helpers," and left.

Dead silence.

Smith read the room. He understood the cash box was the symbol of authority. So he handed it to Rose and said he might not keep the money straight, and he'd feel better if she handled it. Then he asked Jane to train the two teenagers on the soda machine and to own that part of the operation.

Result: Rose happily counted money, Jane ran the teenagers, and Smith enjoyed the concert.

That's an org design lesson. Smith didn't fight over the title. He gave each person a domain and made them feel essential. **Distribute importance, and the conflict disappears.**

---

## You don't need a big title to use this

Here's the thing. You don't need to be an ambassador or the chair of some committee to use this. You can run it every single day.

Carnegie's example: the server brings mashed potatoes when you ordered fries. Say, "I'm sorry to trouble you, but I prefer French fries." She'll probably say "no trouble at all" and be happy to fix it, because you showed her respect.

Imagine your delivery app drops the wrong order at your door. A rage DM to support gets you a canned response. "Sorry to bother you, I think there was a mix-up" gets you a fix.

"Sorry to trouble you." "Would you mind?" "Would you be so kind?" "Thank you."

Carnegie calls these the oil in the gears of everyday life. They cost nothing. They compound.

## The cold email that built a fortune

The fascinating thing here is how one act of sincere appreciation can change the entire trajectory of a life.

Hall Caine was a blacksmith's son with about eight years of schooling. By the early 1900s his novels, including *The Christian*, *The Deemster* and *The Manxman*, were best-sellers read by millions. When he died, he was the richest literary man of his era.

How did it start?

Caine loved poetry, especially the work of Dante Gabriel Rossetti. He wrote a lecture praising Rossetti's artistic achievement and sent a copy to Rossetti himself.

Rossetti was delighted. Any young man with that high an opinion of his work, Rossetti probably figured, must be brilliant. So he invited Caine to London to be his secretary.

That was the inflection point. Caine met the leading writers of the day and launched a career that ended with Greeba Castle, a tourist destination, and a multimillion-dollar estate. He might have died poor and unknown without that essay.

Think of it as the original cold email. Not "Can I pick your brain?" Not "Here's my pitch deck." Just sincere, specific admiration for someone's work. **That's the cold outreach that actually gets a reply.**

Rossetti thought he was important. Of course he did. Almost everyone does.

## "Am I good enough?"

Look, sometimes making someone feel important does more than win you a friend. It changes their life.

Ronald J. Rowland, an instructor of Carnegie's course in California who also taught arts and crafts, wrote about a student named Chris. Chris was quiet, shy and short on confidence. The kind of kid who gets overlooked.

Rowland also taught an advanced class that had become a status symbol. Getting in was a privilege. One Wednesday, watching Chris work diligently at his desk, Rowland sensed a hidden fire in him. So he asked Chris if he'd like to join the advanced class.

The fourteen-year-old fought back tears. "Who, me, Mr. Rowland? Am I good enough?"

"Yes, Chris, you are good enough."

Rowland had to step out, tearing up himself. Chris left class that day looking two inches taller. Afterward, Rowland hung a sign at the front of his classroom: "YOU ARE IMPORTANT."

**One sentence of recognition. A kid's entire self-image, rewritten.**

## Everyone thinks they're better than you at something

Here's the unvarnished truth, in Carnegie's words: almost everyone you meet feels superior to you in some way. The fastest route to their trust is to let them see, subtly and sincerely, that you recognize their importance.

Emerson said it best: "Every man I meet is my superior in some way. In that, I learn of him."

That's the right mental model. Everyone you meet has an edge you don't. Your job is to find it.

Carnegie adds a darker note: the people with the least to show often compensate with the loudest ego, "drest in a little brief authority," as Shakespeare put it. Insecurity usually announces itself.

## The aunt and the Packard

Now for the case studies. Carnegie shares how people in his courses ran this playbook in the real world.

A Connecticut attorney, Mr. R (he asked that his name be withheld because of his relatives), visited his wife's relatives on Long Island. His wife left him with an elderly aunt and went off to see the younger family members.

He had to give a talk on applying the principle of appreciation soon, so he figured this was good practice. He looked around for something he could honestly admire.

"This house was built about 1890, wasn't it?"

It was. He told her it reminded him of the house he was born in. Beautiful. Well built. Roomy. They don't build them like this anymore.

She lit up. She and her late husband had dreamed about this house for years. They'd designed it themselves, without an architect. It was, she said, "built with love."

She gave him the tour, and he admired the treasures she'd collected over a lifetime, from Wedgwood china to silk drapes from a French chateau.

Then she took him to the garage. Up on blocks sat a Packard in mint condition.

Her husband had bought it for her shortly before he died. She'd never ridden in it since. "You appreciate nice things," she said, "and I'm going to give this car to you."

He tried to refuse. He wasn't even a relative, and she had family who'd want it. "Relatives!" she said. They were just waiting for her to die to get that car. Sell it? Watch strangers drive her husband's gift around town? Never.

He couldn't turn it down without hurting her.

Here's the thing. This woman wasn't irrational. She was starving. Alone in a big house with her antiques and her memories, she'd once been young, beautiful and sought after. Now nobody gave her a moment of genuine warmth.

When someone finally did, it was like water in the desert. A Packard was the only gift big enough to match the gratitude.

**The scarcest resource isn't money. It's people who actually see you.**

## The judge and the puppy

Donald M. McMahon, superintendent at a landscaping firm in Rye, New York, shared another one.

Shortly after hearing Carnegie's talk, he was landscaping the estate of a well-known attorney. The owner came out to give instructions about where to plant rhododendrons and azaleas.

McMahon said: "Judge, you have a lovely hobby. I've been admiring your beautiful dogs. I understand you win a lot of blue ribbons every year at the show in Madison Square Garden."

The judge lit up and asked if McMahon wanted to see the kennel.

After nearly an hour of dogs, prizes and pedigrees, the judge asked if McMahon had kids. A son. Would the boy like a puppy? The judge gave him one, then typed out the pedigree and feeding instructions himself.

Final tally: a puppy worth several hundred dollars and an hour and fifteen minutes of a busy man's time. All because McMahon expressed honest admiration for the judge's hobby.

## Five minutes with George Eastman

Look, this is the big one.

George Eastman, of Kodak fame, invented the transparent film that made motion pictures possible and built a fortune of a hundred million dollars. He was one of the most famous businessmen on earth.

And he still craved small recognition, just like you and me.

When Eastman was building the Eastman School of Music and Kilbourn Hall in Rochester, James Adamson, president of the Superior Seating Company of New York, wanted the contract to supply the theater seats. He got a meeting through the architect.

Before the meeting, the architect gave him the brief: Eastman is busy and strict. Take more than five minutes and you're done. Pitch fast and get out.

Adamson walked in. Eastman looked up from his papers and asked what he could do for them.

Adamson didn't pitch. He said, "While we've been waiting, Mr. Eastman, I've been admiring your office. I'm in the interior woodworking business, and I never saw a more beautiful office in all my life."

Eastman said Adamson reminded him of something he'd almost forgotten. He'd loved the room when it was built, but these days he sometimes didn't even notice it for weeks.

Adamson ran his hand across a panel. "This is English oak, isn't it? A little different texture from Italian oak."

It was. Imported English oak, selected by a friend who specialized in fine woods.

That opened the floodgates. Eastman walked him around the room, then pointed out the hospitals and university he was funding. Adamson congratulated him on using his wealth to ease human suffering. Eastman pulled the first camera he ever owned out of a glass case.

Adamson asked about his early struggles. Eastman opened up: childhood poverty, a widowed mother running a boardinghouse, clerking at an insurance office, experimenting with dry photographic plates all night after working all day, sometimes sleeping in his clothes for 72 hours straight.

Adamson had walked in at 10:15 with a five-minute window. One hour passed. Then two.

Finally Eastman said he'd bought some chairs in Japan, the sun had peeled the paint, so he'd repainted them himself. Would Adamson like to come to lunch and see the job he'd done?

After lunch, Eastman proudly showed off chairs worth a few dollars. A multimillionaire, proud because he'd painted them himself.

The seating order was worth $90,000.

Who do you think got it?

Eastman and Adamson stayed close friends until Eastman's death.

Here's the lesson every founder pitching a VC needs to hear. Imagine you're one of twenty pitches on someone's calendar this week. Nineteen people will open with their TAM slide. **The one who opens with genuine curiosity about the person across the table is playing a completely different game.** Adamson never had to pitch. He earned the right to the deal by making the buyer feel seen.

## Retention is a recognition problem

Claude Marais, a restaurant owner in Rouen, France, used this principle to save a key employee.

Paulette had worked for him for five years and was the vital link between him and his staff of twenty-one. Then a registered letter arrived. She was resigning.

Marais was shocked. He thought he'd been fair. But he realized that because she was a friend as well as an employee, he'd probably taken her for granted and maybe demanded more of her than of anyone else.

So he pulled her aside. He told her he couldn't accept her resignation, that she meant a great deal to him and the company, and that she was as important to the restaurant's success as he was.

He repeated it in front of the whole staff, then again at his home with his family present.

Paulette withdrew her resignation. Marais said he could rely on her more than ever, and he kept reinforcing it.

Think about what this means for any company in 2026. Imagine a startup where the best engineer suddenly gives notice. Most founders reach for the counteroffer: more equity, a bigger title. But often the real problem was never comp. It was that nobody told her she mattered. **A lot of resignation letters are really just unpaid invoices for recognition.**

## The Disraeli rule

Carnegie closes with Disraeli, one of the shrewdest men who ever ran the British Empire:

"Talk to people about themselves and they will listen for hours."

That's the whole chapter in one sentence. The clerk, the aunt, the judge, Eastman: none were won by a pitch. They were won because someone made them the main character.

<Tip>**Principle:** Make the other person feel important, and do it sincerely. Fake recognition is noise. Real recognition is signal.</Tip>

## The stack, in a nutshell

Six ways to make people like you, rebuilt for 2026:

1. **Become genuinely interested in other people.** Curiosity beats self-promotion every time.
2. **Smile.** The cheapest trust signal you'll ever send.
3. **Remember people's names.** To that person, their name is the sweetest and most important sound in any language.
4. **Be a good listener.** Get others talking about themselves, then actually listen.
5. **Talk in terms of the other person's interests.** Lead with what they care about, not what you're selling.
6. **Make the other person feel important, and do it sincerely.** Recognition is the highest-ROI move in human relationships.

---

**Bottom line:**

Everyone you'll ever meet is running the same operating system. They want to feel like they matter.

Most people are too busy talking about themselves to notice.

Be the one who notices. Mean it.

That's not a tactic. That's an edge.
