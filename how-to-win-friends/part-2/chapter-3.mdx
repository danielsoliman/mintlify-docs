#### The cheapest edge in business is sitting on everyone's name tag

---

## The kid from the brickyard

Look, start with the origin story.

In 1898, in Stony Point, New York, a man named Jim Farley walked his horse out of the barn on a cold, snowy morning. The horse hadn't been exercised in days. It kicked its heels in the air and killed him on the spot.

He left behind a widow, three boys, and a few hundred dollars of insurance.

His oldest son, also named Jim, was ten. He went to work in a brickyard, hauling sand and flipping bricks to dry in the sun. He never set foot inside a high school.

Now fast-forward.

Before he turned forty-six, four colleges had given him honorary degrees. He became chairman of the Democratic National Committee and Postmaster General of the United States.

**Zero credentials. Zero capital. Massive outcome.**

So what was the edge?

Carnegie tells the story of interviewing Farley and asking for his secret. Farley said, "Hard work." Carnegie told him not to be funny. Farley asked what Carnegie thought it was.

Carnegie said he'd heard Farley could call ten thousand people by their first names.

Farley corrected him: "I can call fifty thousand people by their first names."

Not a party trick. A moat.

---

## Farley's system was a CRM before CRMs existed

Here's the thing. Farley didn't just have a good memory. He built a system.

While he was selling gypsum and serving as town clerk, he developed a simple process:

- Get the person's full name.
- Learn a few facts about their family, their business, and their politics.
- Lock all of it into the mental picture of that person.

A year later, he'd shake their hand, ask about their family, and ask about the hollyhocks in their backyard.

Every touchpoint built on the last. A relationship database running on wetware.

Then he scaled it.

Before Franklin D. Roosevelt's 1932 presidential campaign, Farley wrote hundreds of letters a day to people across the western and northwestern states. Then he covered twenty states and twelve thousand miles in nineteen days by buggy, train, car, and boat, meeting people over meals for real conversations.

When he got back East, he wrote to one person in each town and asked for a list of everyone he'd talked to. Thousands and thousands of names.

Every single one of them got a personal letter. "Dear Bill." "Dear Jane." Signed: "Jim."

**That ability helped put Roosevelt in the White House.**

---

## The incentive nobody talks about

The fascinating thing here is how simple the underlying insight is.

Farley figured out early that **the average person cares more about their own name than every other name on earth combined.**

Remember it and use it easily, and you've paid a subtle but powerful compliment. Forget it or misspell it, and you've put yourself at a real disadvantage.

Carnegie learned the downside the hard way. He once organized a public-speaking course in Paris and sent form letters to American residents there. The French typists filling in the names didn't know English well, and they botched a bunch of them. The manager of a large American bank in Paris wrote Carnegie a scathing reply. Why? His name was misspelled.

Think about the last cold email that misspelled your name. The deal was dead before the second sentence.

**A misspelled name is a signal that you don't care. People price that signal instantly.**

---

## The name everyone else shortcut

Here's where most people get lazy. When a name is hard to pronounce, they skip it or use a nickname.

Sid Levy, one of Carnegie's students, had a customer named Nicodemus Papadoulos. Everyone just called him "Nick."

Levy did something different. Before the meeting, he practiced the full name over and over. Then he walked in and said, "Good afternoon, Mr. Nicodemus Papadoulos."

The man went silent. For what felt like minutes, he said nothing. Then, with tears on his face, he told Levy that in fifteen years in this country, nobody had ever made the effort to call him by his right name.

Fifteen years. One person put in five minutes of prep.

**The effort is tiny. The ROI is enormous. And almost nobody does it.**

## Andrew Carnegie and the rabbit playbook

Andrew Carnegie didn't know much about making steel. He had hundreds of people working for him who knew far more about it than he did.

What he knew was people. And that's what made him rich.

When he was about ten years old in Scotland, he got a mother rabbit. Soon he had a whole litter and nothing to feed them. So he made the neighborhood kids an offer: go gather clover and dandelions to feed the rabbits, and he'd name a bunny after each of them.

It worked like magic.

No money, no food. He offered **naming rights**, and the kids did the work happily.

He never forgot that lesson.

Years later, he wanted to sell steel rails to the Pennsylvania Railroad. The president was J. Edgar Thomson. So Carnegie built a massive steel mill in Pittsburgh and named it the "Edgar Thomson Steel Works."

Quick question. When the Pennsylvania Railroad needed rails, where do you think Thomson bought them?

Yeah. Exactly.

## How a name turned a price war into a merger

Here's the next level of the same play.

Carnegie's Central Transportation Company was in a brutal fight with George Pullman's company over the Union Pacific Railroad's sleeping-car business. Both sides were slashing prices. Both were torching any chance of profit. Classic race to the bottom.

Both men went to New York to meet the Union Pacific board. Carnegie ran into Pullman at the St. Nicholas Hotel and said: "Good evening, Mr. Pullman, aren't we making a couple of fools of ourselves?"

Pullman wanted to know what he meant.

Carnegie pitched a merger. He laid out why working together beat destroying each other. Pullman wasn't sold.

Then Pullman asked one question: "What would you call the new company?"

Carnegie didn't hesitate. "Why, the Pullman Palace Car Company, of course."

Pullman's face lit up. "Come into my room. Let's talk it over."

That conversation made industrial history.

Imagine two VC-backed startups burning cash to undercut each other. Everyone's losing. **Carnegie gave away the name on the door and won the deal.**

He applied the same logic inside his company. He was proud that he could call many of his factory workers by their first names. And he claimed that while he was personally in charge, no strike ever hit his mills.

## The bigger you get, the colder it gets

Benton Love, chairman of Texas Commerce Bancshares, believed that the bigger a corporation gets, the colder it becomes. His fix: remember people's names.

He put it bluntly. An executive who says he can't remember names is really saying he can't remember a significant part of his business, and he's "operating on quicksand."

Same thing works at the front line. Karen Kirsech, a TWA flight attendant, made it a habit to learn as many passengers' names as she could and use them during service. The compliments poured in. One passenger wrote that he was going to fly nothing but TWA from now on because the airline felt personal.

No discount. No loyalty points. Just a name, and a customer who moved his entire spend.

**That's retention you can't buy with a promo code.**

---

## People will pay real money for their name

If you still doubt how much people value their name, look at the market.

P. T. Barnum, the greatest showman of his era, had no sons to carry on his name. So he offered his grandson, C. H. Seeley, $25,000 to call himself "Barnum" Seeley.

For centuries, nobles funded artists and writers so the work would be dedicated to them. The New York Public Library has its Astor and Lenox collections. The Metropolitan Museum carries the names of Benjamin Altman and J. P. Morgan. Churches have stained-glass windows honoring donors. University buildings carry the names of the people who wrote the biggest checks.

**People will spend fortunes so their name doesn't disappear.** Remember someone's name and you hand them that asset for free.

## "I'm too busy" is not a valid excuse

Here's the uncomfortable truth.

Most people don't put in the energy to concentrate, repeat, and lock names in. They tell themselves they're too busy.

You're probably not busier than Franklin D. Roosevelt.

Chrysler built a special car for Roosevelt, who couldn't use a standard one because his legs were paralyzed. W. F. Chamberlain and a mechanic delivered it to the White House. Chamberlain later wrote to Carnegie about the experience. He said he taught the President how to handle a car full of unusual gadgets, but Roosevelt taught him a lot about handling people.

Here's what Roosevelt did:

- Called Chamberlain by name and made him feel at ease.
- Showed real interest in everything Chamberlain demonstrated.
- Publicly thanked him in front of friends and staff for the time and effort he'd put into the car.
- Praised specific details: the radiator, the rear-view mirror, the clock, the spotlight, the upholstery, the driver's seat position, the monogrammed suitcases.
- Pointed those details out to Mrs. Roosevelt, Secretary of Labor Frances Perkins, and his secretary.
- Even told the White House porter to take good care of the suitcases.

Then he noted he'd kept the Federal Reserve Board waiting thirty minutes. But here's the part that matters most.

The mechanic who came along was shy. He stayed in the background. He was introduced once. He never spoke to the President. Before leaving, Roosevelt sought him out, shook his hand, called him by name, and thanked him for coming to Washington. Chamberlain said it wasn't perfunctory. Roosevelt meant it.

A few days later, Chamberlain got a signed photo and a thank-you note. He said he had no idea how Roosevelt found the time.

The President of the United States, with the Fed waiting, remembered the name of a mechanic he'd heard once.

**If he had time, you have time.**

## The playbook: how to actually remember names

Most of us meet someone, chat for a few minutes, and forget their name before we say goodbye.

Politicians learn this early: "To recall a voter's name is statesmanship. To forget it is oblivion."

And it's almost as true in business as it is in politics.

Napoleon III, Emperor of France and nephew of the great Napoleon, claimed he could remember the name of every person he met, despite all his royal duties. His method was simple:

1. If he didn't hear the name clearly, he said so and asked again.
2. If the name was unusual, he asked how it was spelled.
3. During the conversation, he repeated the name several times.
4. He linked the name to the person's face, expression, and appearance.
5. For important people, he wrote the name down later, studied it, locked it in, and then tore up the paper. Ear memory plus eye memory.

It takes effort. As Emerson put it, "Good manners are made up of petty sacrifices."

## It works at every level

This isn't just for presidents and emperors.

Ken Nottingham worked at General Motors in Indiana and usually ate at the company cafeteria. The woman behind the counter always had a scowl. He was just another sandwich to her. One slice of ham, weighed on the scale. One leaf of lettuce. A few chips.

Next day, same line, same woman, same scowl. This time he noticed her name tag. He smiled and said, "Hello, Eunice."

She forgot the scale. Piled on the ham. Three leaves of lettuce. Chips falling off the plate.

Same order. One variable changed: a single word.

Think about your pizza delivery driver or the support rep on chat. Use their name and you move from "transaction" to "relationship."

A name belongs to that person and nobody else. Lead with it, and whatever you ask or share carries more weight, from the server to the senior executive.

<Tip>**Principle:** Remember that a person's name is, to that person, the sweetest and most important sound in any language. Treat it like the high-value asset it is.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

A name costs you nothing to remember and is priceless to the person who owns it.

Farley built a political machine on it. Carnegie won deals with it. Roosevelt made a mechanic feel like a VIP with it.

Most people won't put in the effort. That's exactly why it works.

**Learn the name. Use the name. Compound the trust.**
