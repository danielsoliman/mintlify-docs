#### Stop pitching your product. Talk about their obsession.

## The Roosevelt research stack

Look, everyone who met Theodore Roosevelt walked away stunned by how much he knew.

Cowboy, Rough Rider, New York politician, diplomat. Didn't matter. Roosevelt always knew what to say.

The secret wasn't genius. **It was prep.** Whenever a visitor was coming, he stayed up late the night before reading about whatever that person cared about most.

Roosevelt understood what every great leader understands: **the fastest route to someone's attention is the thing they already love.**

Imagine a founder who walks into a pitch having read the investor's last ten posts and knowing which bets they're proudest of. Now imagine one who opens with slide one. Same product. Completely different meeting.

## The lawyer who didn't care about boats

Here's the thing: this lesson isn't complicated.

William Lyon Phelps, the Yale literature professor, learned it at eight years old. While he was visiting his Aunt Libby, a middle-aged man stopped by. After some small talk with the aunt, the visitor turned his attention to William.

The kid was obsessed with boats. So the man talked boats, and William was hooked.

After the guy left, William raved about him. Then his aunt dropped the reveal. The visitor was a New York lawyer who had zero interest in boats.

So why talk about boats the whole time?

"Because he is a gentleman," she said. "He saw you were interested in boats, and he talked about the things he knew would interest and please you."

Phelps never forgot it.

**The lawyer didn't sell himself. He made the conversation about the kid.**

## The million-dollar check play

The fascinating thing here is how well this works when you actually need something.

Edward L. Chalif did Boy Scout work. A big jamboree was coming up in Europe, and he wanted the president of one of America's largest corporations to pay for one scout's trip.

Big ask. Cold outreach. Low odds.

But Chalif heard that this executive had once written a check for a million dollars and later had it framed.

So Chalif didn't open with the Scouts, the jamboree, or the ask. He asked to see the check.

He said he wanted to tell his boys he'd seen one. The executive happily showed it off and explained how it came to be written. Eventually he said, "Oh, by the way, what was it you wanted to see me about?"

Now look at the return on that opening:

- Chalif asked for **one** boy's trip. He got **five** boys plus himself.
- He got a thousand-dollar letter of credit and seven weeks in Europe.
- He got introductions to the company's branch presidents, and the executive showed them around Paris himself.
- Later, the executive gave jobs to some of the boys whose families were struggling.

Chalif said that without warming the man up first, he wouldn't have found him "one-tenth as easy to approach."

**He led with their pride instead of his ask. The ask got a 5x upgrade.**

## Four years of cold calls vs. one conversation

Is this real business strategy, or just charm?

Henry G. Duvernoy ran a wholesale baking company in New York and wanted to sell bread to a particular hotel. His strategy was pure brute force:

- He called on the manager every week for four years.
- He went to the same social events.
- He even lived in the hotel to win the business.

Result? Zero.

Then Duvernoy changed tactics. Instead of pushing bread, he found out what lit the manager up: a society called the Hotel Greeters of America. The manager wasn't just a member. He was president of it, and of the International Greeters too.

So Duvernoy asked about the Greeters. The manager talked for half an hour, buzzing with energy. By the end, he had "sold" Duvernoy a membership.

Duvernoy never mentioned bread.

A few days later, the hotel's steward called for samples and prices. "I don't know what you did to the old boy," the steward told him, "but he sure is sold on you!"

Four years of effort, zero conversion. Then one conversation on the right topic, and the deal showed up on its own.

**Most people optimize for pitch volume. The real leverage is relevance.**

## Getting past the gatekeeper

Here's the thing about access: the most valuable people build walls.

After military service, Edward E. Harriman settled in Maryland's Cumberland Valley, where jobs were scarce. Many local companies were controlled by R. J. Funkhouser, a rags-to-riches maverick who was famously hard for job seekers to reach.

Harriman did his homework. Funkhouser was driven by power and money. But he was guarded by a stern secretary of fifteen years.

So Harriman studied *her* interests first. He told her he had a proposition that could bring her boss financial and political success, and he talked with her about her own role in that success. She got excited and set up the meeting.

Funkhouser barked, "How about it, young man?"

Harriman didn't ask for a job. He said: "Mr. Funkhouser, I believe I can make money for you."

Funkhouser offered him a seat. Harriman laid out his ideas and how they'd grow Funkhouser's businesses. He was hired on the spot, and they both prospered for more than twenty years.

**Two people, two sets of incentives, two pitches tailored to each.** That's not manipulation. That's understanding the system you're operating in.

## Both sides win

Look, this isn't a one-way extraction trick.

Howard Z. Herzig, a leader in employee communications, lived by this principle. Asked what he got out of it, he said every person gave him a different reward, but overall, each conversation enlarged his life.

Get curious about what others care about, and you don't just close more deals. Your world gets bigger.

**It's a positive-sum game.**

**Bottom line:**

Nobody cares about your pitch. They care about their own obsessions.

Do the research. Find what lights them up. Lead with that.

The ask gets easier, and both sides walk away richer.
