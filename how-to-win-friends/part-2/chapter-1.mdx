#### The best networker on Earth has four legs and zero followers

## The greatest relationship builder you've ever met

Look, before you buy another course on "building your personal brand," study the undefeated champion of making friends: the dog on your street.

Get within ten feet and his tail starts going. Pet him and he nearly loses his mind with joy. No hidden agenda. He's not pitching you a seed round or angling for a referral.

Here's the thing Carnegie points out that should rearrange your brain a little: **the dog is the only animal that doesn't have to work for a living.** A hen lays eggs. A cow gives milk. A canary sings. The dog just gives you love, and that's the whole business model.

Carnegie tells the story of his childhood dog, Tippy, a fifty-cent pup who waited in the yard every afternoon and sprinted up the hill the second he heard young Dale's voice. Five years of that, until Tippy was killed by lightning a few feet away. Carnegie called it the tragedy of his boyhood.

Tippy never read a psychology book. He didn't need to. He understood the one line this entire chapter rests on:

**You can make more friends in two months by becoming genuinely interested in other people than you can in two years by trying to get other people interested in you.**

---

## The market reality: nobody is thinking about you

Here's the uncomfortable truth.

People are not interested in you. They are not interested in me. They are interested in themselves. Morning, noon, and after dinner.

Carnegie cites a study the New York Telephone Company ran on phone conversations to find the most-used word. The winner? "I." It showed up 3,900 times across 500 calls.

Want the 2026 version? Think about the last group photo you were tagged in. Whose face did you zoom in on first?

Exactly.

**It doesn't work.** You won't build real, durable relationships by trying to impress people. Friends aren't acquired that way.

Napoleon tried it. In his last meeting with Josephine he told her he'd been as fortunate as any man on earth, and yet she was the only person in the world he could rely on. Historians doubt he could even rely on her.

The guy conquered most of Europe and ended with a network of maybe one. That's a terrible return on effort.

## The thesis behind the thesis

The fascinating thing here is that Carnegie found the sharpest version of this idea in a psychologist, not a salesman.

Alfred Adler, the Viennese psychologist, wrote in *What Life Should Mean to You*:

> "It is the individual who is not interested in his fellow men who has the greatest difficulties in life and provides the greatest injury to others. It is from among such individuals that all human failures spring."

Carnegie said you could read stacks of psychology books and not find a statement more important. I'd agree.

In systems language: **lack of interest in other people isn't a personality quirk. It's a root-cause failure** that crashes everything downstream.

Carnegie took a short-story writing course at NYU, and the editor of a major magazine told the class he could read a few paragraphs of any submission and tell whether the author liked people. His rule: "If the author doesn't like people, people won't like his or her stories."

If that's true for fiction on a page, it's ten times truer face-to-face. And it's true for every pitch deck, every cold DM, every product launch post you'll ever write. People can smell whether you actually care about them. The signal leaks through.

## The magician's edge

Carnegie spent an evening backstage with Howard Thurston, the dean of American magicians. Forty years of touring. More than 60 million paying customers. Almost $2 million in profit, which was enormous money at the time.

Thurston's background? He ran away from home as a kid, rode in boxcars, slept in haystacks, begged for food, and learned to read from railway signs. So it wasn't the credentials.

Was it superior knowledge of magic? No. Thurston told Carnegie that hundreds of books had been written on sleight of hand, and plenty of people knew as much as he did.

His edge came down to two things:

- **He was a master showman.** Every gesture, every tone of voice, every raised eyebrow was rehearsed and timed to the split second.
- **He was genuinely interested in his audience.**

That second one is the moat. Thurston said a lot of magicians looked at the crowd and thought, "a bunch of suckers out there. I'll fool them." Thurston's internal script was the opposite: he was grateful these people came, they made it possible for him to make a living doing something he loved, and he was going to give them his very best.

Before every show, he repeated to himself: "I love my audience. I love my audience."

Imagine two founders shipping the same product. One sees users as "conversion events." The other sees them as people who chose to spend their time and money with him. Give it five years and see which one has a loyal community.

**Same product. Different mindset. Wildly different outcome.**

## Interest compounds at any age

Carnegie tells the story of George Dyke of North Warren, Pennsylvania. He ran a service station for thirty years until a new highway was built right over it and forced him into retirement.

He wasn't great. He started traveling to hear other fiddlers play, and in his humble way he got curious about every musician he met: their backgrounds, their stories, what they cared about. He showed up at competitions. Before long, country music fans across the eastern United States knew him as "Uncle George, the Fiddle Scraper from Kinzua County."

When Carnegie's people heard him, he was seventy-two and loving every minute.

No special talent. No platform. Just **sustained interest in other people**, compounding over time.

That's the cheapest, highest-yielding asset most people ignore.

## Roosevelt and the people with no leverage

Here's where you separate the real thing from the performance.

Plenty of people are charming to the VIP in the room. Very few are genuinely warm to the people who can do nothing for them. That's the tell.

Theodore Roosevelt was off the charts on this. Even his staff loved him. His valet, James E. Amos, wrote a book called *Theodore Roosevelt, Hero to His Valet*. In it, Amos tells how his wife once asked Roosevelt what a bobwhite looked like. He described it in detail. Later, their phone rang in the little cottage on the Roosevelt estate. It was the President himself, calling to tell her there was a bobwhite outside her window right now if she wanted to look.

Whenever he walked past their cottage, even if they were out of sight, he'd call out a friendly greeting to them by name.

After his presidency, Roosevelt visited the White House while the Tafts were away and greeted the old staff by name, down to the scullery maids. According to Archie Butt, he asked Alice, the kitchen maid, if she still made corn bread. She said she made it for the staff, but nobody upstairs ate it.

"They show bad taste," Roosevelt boomed, "and I'll tell the President so when I see him."

She brought him a piece, and he walked to the office eating it, greeting gardeners and laborers along the way. Ike Hoover, the head usher for forty years, said with tears in his eyes that it was the only happy day they'd had in nearly two years, and none of them would trade it for a hundred-dollar bill.

**Think about the ROI on that.** Zero dollars spent. A few names remembered. A question about corn bread. And a loyalty that people still wrote about decades later.

## The soda clerk who saved the account

Now let's bring it down to pure business, because this one is a clean case study.

Edward M. Sykes, Jr., a sales rep for Johnson & Johnson in Massachusetts, had a drugstore account in Hingham. Every time he came in, he'd spend a few minutes chatting with the soda clerk and the sales clerk before talking to the owner about the order.

One day the owner told him to get lost. He was done with J&J. He felt the company was favoring food and discount chains over small drugstores like his.

He walked in, said hello to the clerks as usual, and went up to the owner. The owner smiled, welcomed him back, and **doubled his usual order.**

What happened? After Sykes left, the young soda clerk walked over to the owner and said Sykes was one of the few salespeople who ever bothered to say hello to him and the rest of the staff. If any salesperson deserved the business, it was him.

The owner agreed. He stayed a loyal customer.

Here's the lesson in incentive terms: **you never know who sits on the decision committee.** Every org has an informal influence network that isn't on the org chart. The assistant. The junior analyst. Treat them like they matter, because they do, and because they're often whispering in the decision-maker's ear.

## How to get time from people who don't give it

Look, the most sought-after people are drowning in requests. Carnegie figured out how to cut through.

When he ran a fiction-writing course at the Brooklyn Institute of Arts and Sciences, he wanted big-name authors like Kathleen Norris, Fannie Hurst, Ida Tarbell, Albert Payson Terhune, and Rupert Hughes to come speak. So his class wrote to them saying they admired their work and wanted to learn the secrets of their success. Each letter was signed by around 150 students.

And here's the clever part. They acknowledged the authors were too busy to prepare a lecture, so they enclosed a list of questions about the authors themselves and how they worked.

The authors loved it. They came to Brooklyn.

Carnegie used the same approach to bring in Leslie M. Shaw, Theodore Roosevelt's Treasury Secretary; George W. Wickersham, Taft's attorney general; William Jennings Bryan; Franklin D. Roosevelt; and many others.

Imagine you're trying to land a busy investor on your podcast. The typical cold DM is all about you: your show, your downloads. The Carnegie version is about them: specific admiration, specific questions, and an ask that respects their time.

**One is a request for a favor. The other is an invitation to talk about their favorite subject.** Guess which one gets a reply.

## Everyone wants a fan. Even the most hated man alive

Factory worker or king, everyone likes the people who admire them.

Carnegie's most extreme example: Kaiser Wilhelm at the end of World War I. He was probably the most despised man on Earth. Even his own country turned on him when he fled to Holland. Millions of people would have happily torn him apart.

In the middle of all that, one little boy wrote him a simple, sincere letter full of kindness and admiration, saying that no matter what anyone else thought, he'd always love Wilhelm as his Emperor.

The Kaiser was so moved he invited the boy to visit. The boy came with his mother. And the Kaiser married her.

The point: **sincere appreciation is undervalued in every market, and it's most undervalued exactly where it's most scarce.**

## Put in the reps

Genuine interest isn't a vibe. It's behavior that costs time and thoughtfulness, and that cost is what makes the signal credible.

**Do the homework.** When the Duke of Windsor was Prince of Wales and scheduled to tour South America, he spent months learning Spanish so he could give speeches in the local language. South Americans loved him for it.

Imagine you're expanding into a new market. The team that actually learned the customers' language and problems beats the one with a translated slide deck.

**Remember what matters to people.** Carnegie made a habit of learning his friends' birthdays. He didn't believe in astrology at all, but he'd ask people whether they thought their birth date had anything to do with their personality, then ask for the date. He'd repeat it to himself, jot it down the second they turned away, then log it in a birthday book. At the start of each year, the dates went on his calendar. When the day came, there was his letter or telegram.

What a hit it made. He was often the only person who remembered.

Today your phone can run that system for you, and most people still don't bother. **The tooling is free. The intent is the scarce input.**

**Greet people like you mean it.** When someone calls you, answer in a tone that says you're genuinely glad they did. Carnegie noted that many companies trained their phone operators to greet every caller with warmth and enthusiasm so the caller felt the company actually cared.

## Interest is a retention strategy

Here's where it becomes a business moat, not just a personal habit.

A depositor named Madeline Rosedale wrote a letter to the National Bank of North America in New York that the bank published. She said everyone was courteous and helpful, and what a pleasure it was, after waiting in a long line, to be greeted pleasantly. Then she got to the real reason: her mother had been hospitalized for five months, and a teller named Marie Petrucello regularly asked how her mother was doing.

There's no rate, no promo, no signup bonus that beats that. **A competitor can match your pricing in a week. They can't match the teller who asked about your mother.**

## The stamps that unlocked the data room

Now the hard-nosed example.

Charles R. Walters, at a large New York bank, had to prepare a confidential report on a corporation. Only one person had the facts he needed: the company's president.

As Walters was shown into the office, a secretary popped her head in and told the president she had no stamps for him that day. The president explained he collected stamps for his twelve-year-old son.

Then Walters started asking his questions. The president was vague and evasive. He clearly didn't want to talk. The meeting went nowhere.

Walters didn't know what to do. Then he remembered: stamps, twelve-year-old son. And his bank's foreign department received letters from all over the world, which meant stamps from every continent.

The next afternoon he came back and sent word he had some stamps for the boy.

Was he welcomed? The president shook his hand like he was running for Congress. "My George will love this one," he kept saying. "And look at this! This is a treasure."

They spent half an hour on stamps and photos of his son. Then the president spent more than an hour giving Walters everything he needed, unprompted. He called in his team, phoned associates, and loaded Walters up with facts, figures, and reports.

**Same person. Same request. Completely different outcome.** The only variable that changed was whether Walters showed interest in what the president cared about.

## The fuel salesman who flipped his position

Here's the thing about people who can't close: they usually think the problem is the buyer.

C. M. Knaphle, Jr., of Philadelphia, had tried for years to sell fuel to a large chain-store company. They kept buying from an out-of-town dealer and hauling it right past his office. One night he got up in front of Carnegie's class and tore into chain stores, calling them a curse on the nation.

And he still wondered why he couldn't sell to them.

Carnegie suggested a different approach. The class staged a debate on whether chain stores were doing the country more harm than good. Knaphle took the side defending the chain stores. Then he went straight to an executive at the company he'd been trashing and said he wasn't there to sell fuel. He was there to ask for a favor. He explained the debate and said he couldn't think of anyone more qualified to give him the facts.

He'd asked for one minute. The executive talked for one hour and forty-seven minutes, brought in a colleague who'd written a book on chain stores, and wrote to the National Chain Store Association for more material. His eyes lit up talking about what his company did for hundreds of communities. Knaphle admitted the man changed his whole attitude.

At the door, the executive put an arm around his shoulder, wished him luck, and asked him to come back and report how it went. His last words: come see me again in the spring, I'd like to place an order for fuel.

Knaphle called it almost a miracle. In his words, he'd made more headway in two hours by becoming genuinely interested in the man and his problems than he could have in ten years of trying to get the man interested in him and his product.

Carnegie's response: you didn't discover anything new. The Roman poet Publilius Syrus said it a hundred years before Christ: "We are interested in others when they are interested in us."

## The guardrail: it has to be real

Now, the cynics are already thinking: "Great, so I'll fake interest to close deals."

No. That's the wrong read.

Carnegie is explicit. Like every principle of human relations, **interest has to be sincere.** It has to pay off for both people, not just the one showing it. It's a two-way street.

Fake interest is a short position on your own reputation. Real interest is a long position that compounds.

Carnegie closes with a story from Martin Ginsberg, a student in his course on Long Island.

On Thanksgiving Day, when Ginsberg was ten, he was in a city hospital's welfare ward, facing major orthopedic surgery the next morning and months of pain after. His father was dead. His mother, alone and broke at home, couldn't visit.

He pulled the covers over his head and cried.

A young student nurse heard him. She pulled back the covers, wiped his tears, and told him she was lonely too, stuck working instead of being with her family. She asked if he'd have dinner with her. She brought two trays: turkey, mashed potatoes, cranberry sauce, ice cream. Her shift ended at 4 p.m. She stayed on her own time until almost 11, playing games and talking with him until he fell asleep.

Decades later, Ginsberg said not a Thanksgiving passed without him remembering that day, the fear and loneliness, and the warmth of a stranger who made it bearable.

No transaction. No angle. That's exactly why it lands.

**That's the real thing. Everything else in this chapter is just a scaled-down version of it.**

<Tip>**Principle:** Become genuinely interested in other people. Not as a tactic, but as a default setting.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

Everyone you meet is already obsessed with one topic: themselves.

You can fight that and lose, or align with it and win.

Attention is the scarcest asset in the world. **The people who get the most of it are the ones who give it away first.**
