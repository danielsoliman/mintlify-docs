#### The highest-ROI asset you own costs zero dollars

Look, most people are optimizing the wrong variable.

They spend on the outfit, the watch, the car, the perfectly lit profile photo. Then they walk into the room with a face that says "I'd rather be anywhere else."

Carnegie opens this chapter with a woman at a New York dinner party. She had inherited money and blown a good chunk of it on sables, diamonds and pearls. She desperately wanted to impress everyone.

But she did nothing about her face. It broadcast sourness and selfishness.

She missed what everybody in that room already knew: **the expression on your face matters far more than the clothes on your back.**

That's the whole chapter. The rest is evidence.

---

## Here's the thing: your face is a signal

Charles Schwab told Carnegie his smile had been worth a million dollars. Carnegie thought he was probably lowballing it.

Schwab's personality, his charm, his ability to make people like him, were almost entirely responsible for his extraordinary success. And the smile was a core part of that package.

Why does it work? Because actions are higher-bandwidth than words.

A smile says three things instantly:

- I like you.
- You make me happy.
- I'm glad to see you.

That's why dogs win. They are so pumped to see you that they nearly jump out of their skin. So, naturally, you're pumped to see them back.

A baby's smile does the same thing.

Carnegie shares a story from Dr. Stephen K. Sproul, a veterinarian in Raytown, Missouri. His waiting room was packed on a spring day with people getting their pets vaccinated. Nobody was talking. Everyone was mentally somewhere else, annoyed about "wasting time."

Then a young woman walked in with a nine-month-old baby and a kitten and sat next to a man visibly fed up with the wait. The baby looked up at him and flashed a huge smile.

He smiled back, of course. Then he started talking to the mom about her baby and his grandkids. Soon the whole room was chatting.

One smile. Zero cost. Entire room repriced.

**Emotion is a network effect. Somebody has to go first.**

## The fake smile gets priced in immediately

Let's be clear: an insincere grin doesn't fool anybody. People can tell it's mechanical, and they resent it. Think about the last time a sales rep hit you with a forced, dead-eyed smile. You didn't feel welcomed. You felt like a lead in a CRM.

Carnegie is talking about the real thing. A smile that comes from inside. The kind, as he puts it, "that will bring a good price in the marketplace."

The data backs this up. Professor James V. McConnell, a psychologist at the University of Michigan, said people who smile tend to manage, teach and sell more effectively, and to raise happier children. His logic: there's more information in a smile than a frown, which is why encouragement beats punishment as a teaching tool.

And here's a hiring signal for you. An employment manager at a large New York department store told Carnegie she'd rather hire a sales clerk who never finished grade school but had a pleasant smile than a PhD with a somber face.

Read that again. **Warmth beat credentials.**

## The smile works even when nobody can see it

The fascinating thing here is that a smile doesn't need to be visible to land.

Telephone companies used to run a program called "phone power" for employees who sold over the phone. The core advice: smile while you talk. It comes through in your voice.

Carnegie tells the story of Robert Cryer, who ran a computer department at a Cincinnati company. He needed a computer science PhD and finally found an ideal candidate graduating from Purdue. Problem: the kid had multiple offers, many from bigger, better-known companies.

Cryer won anyway.

When he later asked why, the new hire said the managers at the other companies sounded cold and businesslike on the phone. He felt like just another transaction. Cryer sounded genuinely glad to hear from him, like he actually wanted him on the team.

Cryer kept answering his phone with a smile after that.

Translate that to 2026. Imagine you're a founder competing for a top engineer against companies with bigger brands and deeper pockets. You can't win on comp or logo.

**You can win on how you make them feel on the call.** Tone of voice, energy in the DM, whether your follow-up reads like a form letter or a human who's excited. That's not soft stuff. That's edge.

---

## If it's not fun, it doesn't compound

The chairman of one of the largest rubber companies in the United States told Carnegie something counterintuitive. In his observation, people rarely succeed at anything unless they have fun doing it.

He didn't buy that hard work alone is the magic key. He'd watched people succeed because they loved running their business, then fail once the fun turned into grind and the joy drained out.

Energy is an input. When the joy goes, the output follows it down.

Carnegie boils it down to one line:

**You have to enjoy meeting people if you expect them to enjoy meeting you.**

## The one-week experiment

Carnegie didn't just theorize. He ran the test, over and over.

He asked thousands of business people to smile at someone every hour of the day for one week, then come back to class and report the results.

One letter came from William B. Steinhardt, a New York stockbroker. Carnegie says his case wasn't unusual. It was typical of hundreds.

Steinhardt had been married for over eighteen years. In all that time, he barely smiled at his wife or said two dozen words to her before leaving for work. By his own account, he was one of the worst grouches on Broadway.

So he tried it. The next morning he looked at his "glum mug" in the mirror and told himself to wipe the scowl off. At breakfast, he greeted his wife with "Good morning, my dear" and a smile.

She was shocked. He told her to expect it every day, and he kept it up.

The results over the next two months:

- **At home:** more happiness in two months than in the entire previous year.
- **On the commute:** he smiled at the elevator operator, the doorman, the subway cashier. They smiled back.
- **At work:** he handled complaints cheerfully and found disputes got resolved much more easily. In his words, smiles were bringing him "dollars, many dollars every day."
- **In the office:** a clerk he shared space with admitted he'd thought Steinhardt was a terrible grouch. Only recently had he changed his mind. He said Steinhardt seemed really human when he smiled.

Then he stacked the other principles on top: no more criticism, praise instead of condemnation, and seeing things from the other person's point of view.

His verdict: a different man, a happier man, a richer man. Richer in friendships and happiness, which he called "the only things that matter much after all."

That's the trade. One week of effort, a lifetime of upside.

## Don't feel like smiling? Act first

Here's the obvious objection: "I'm not in the mood."

Carnegie's answer is blunt. Force it. If you're alone, whistle, hum, or sing. Act as if you're already happy, and you'll tend to become happy.

This isn't wishful thinking. It's a control-systems insight from the psychologist and philosopher William James. His argument: action seems to follow feeling, but they actually move together. You can't directly control your feelings. You can directly control your actions. So regulate the action, and the feeling follows.

In James's words, the "sovereign voluntary path to cheerfulness" is to sit up cheerfully and act and speak as if cheerfulness were already there.

You don't wait for the market to turn. You change the inputs you control.

## Happiness is an inside job

Everybody is chasing happiness. Carnegie says there's one reliable way to get it: control your thoughts.

Happiness doesn't depend on outward conditions. It depends on inner ones.

It's not what you have, who you are, or where you are. It's what you think about it. Two people can do the same job with the same money and status, and one is miserable while the other is happy. The difference is mental attitude. Carnegie says he saw as many happy faces among poor laborers in brutal tropical heat as in air-conditioned offices in New York or Chicago.

Shakespeare: "There is nothing either good or bad, but thinking makes it so."

Lincoln: most folks are about as happy as they make up their minds to be.

Carnegie saw that truth up close at the Long Island Railroad station in New York. Thirty or forty boys with disabilities, on canes and crutches, were struggling up the stairs. One had to be carried. And they were laughing. Full of joy.

He asked one of the men in charge about it. The answer: when a boy realizes he'll live with a disability for life, he's shocked at first. Then he gets past the shock, accepts it, and becomes as happy as any other kid.

Carnegie said he felt like taking his hat off to those boys. It was a lesson he hoped he'd never forget.

## Nobody is coming to you. Go first

Now, the tactical playbook.

Carnegie tells the story of María González of Guadalajara, Mexico. She worked alone in a closed-off room, envying the chatter and laughter of her coworkers. But when she passed them in the hall, she shyly looked away.

After a few weeks, she gave herself a talking-to: "María, you can't expect those women to come to you. You have to go out and meet them."

The next time she walked to the water cooler, she put on her brightest smile and said, "Hi, how are you today?" to everyone she passed.

The effect was immediate. Smiles and hellos came right back. Acquaintances turned into friendships. Her job and her life got better.

Think about how many people sit in a Slack channel or a group chat for months, waiting for someone to reach out. **The person who goes first captures the relationship.** That's not luck. That's initiative.

Carnegie also quotes the essayist Elbert Hubbard at length. The short version: head high, greet people with a smile, put soul into every handshake, don't waste a minute on your enemies, and keep picturing the useful person you want to become. "Thought is supreme," Hubbard wrote. "We become like that on which our hearts are fixed."

Carnegie's warning: reading it does nothing. Applying it does everything.

## The value of a smile

There's an old Chinese proverb Carnegie says we should paste inside our hats:

**"A man without a smiling face must not open a shop."**

Translation: if you can't make people feel welcome, don't go into business. Customer experience is the business.

To someone who has seen a dozen people frown or turn away that day, especially someone under pressure from a boss, customers or family, your smile is the sun breaking through the clouds. It tells them things aren't hopeless.

Carnegie closes with an ad a New York department store ran during the Christmas rush, when its staff was running on fumes. The gist: a smile costs nothing but creates much. It enriches the receiver without making the giver poorer. It can't be bought, begged, borrowed or stolen. It's worthless until you give it away.

And the kicker: if a tired salesperson is too worn out to smile at you, leave one of yours. Because **nobody needs a smile more than the people who have none left to give.**

<Tip>**Principle:** Smile. A real one, not a performance.</Tip>

---

**Bottom line:**

A smile is the rare asset with zero cost and near-unlimited upside.

It wins hires you can't outbid, calms rooms you didn't create, and repairs relationships you forgot you were neglecting.

You don't need to feel it first. Act first. The feeling follows.

**Go first. Smile.**
