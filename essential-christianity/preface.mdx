## History of the book

<Info>
  The original book was sourced from BBC Radio Talks between 1941-1944.\
  \
  Published as a single book in 1952.
</Info>

The content in this book didn't start as a manuscript. It started as a series of podcast episodes and newsletter deep-dives I dropped between 2023 and 2025. When I finally decided to package this into a book, I cleaned up a few things, but for the most part, I kept the original energy.

Here’s the thing: a podcast should sound like a conversation. It shouldn’t sound like someone reading a white paper out loud. When I’m on the mic, I use the same slang and contractions I use when I’m grabbing coffee with a friend. In the first draft of the book, I tried to keep that "don’t" and "we’ve" vibe. I even used italics to show where I was putting emphasis with my voice.

Looking back, I think that was a mistake. Writing and speaking are two different skill sets. If you’re a speaker, you use tone and volume for leverage. If you’re a writer, you use sentence structure. In this 2026 edition, I’ve smoothed out the "podcast-isms" and reframed the sentences to be punchier without losing that "common man" tone I’ve always aimed for. I also updated a few sections where my understanding of the market has evolved over the last decade, or where I realized my original message was getting lost in the noise.

## Why this book

Let me be clear about one thing: I am not here to help you choose a "denomination." I’m not going to tell you to be an Anglican, a Baptist, or a Catholic. That’s intentional. I listed those alphabetically for a reason. There’s no secret about where I stand—I’m a regular guy who goes to a standard Episcopal church. I’m not "high-church," I’m not "low-church," I’m just there.

But I’m not trying to sell you on my specific setup. Since I became a Christian, I realized the biggest value add I could provide for people who don't believe yet is to explain and defend the "Layer 1" protocol of the faith—the core beliefs that almost every Christian has agreed on since the beginning.

I have two reasons for this. First, the stuff that divides Christians usually involves high-level theological theory or obscure history. To be honest, that’s out of my depth. I’d be a tourist in those waters, and you need an expert for that. Second, arguing over the 1% differences doesn't actually onboard anyone. It actually does the opposite. If we spend all our time fighting over the edges, we just look like a broken organization to the outside world. We should only be debating those specifics among people who already agree on the fundamentals: there is one God, and Jesus Christ is His Son.

### Approach to Christianity

Finally, I noticed that all the "top-tier" authors were already fighting the "Civil War" inside the church. The frontline defending the core foundation—what Richard Baxter calls "Mere" Christianity—was actually the thinnest part of the line. I saw a supply-and-demand imbalance, and I went where I could be most useful.

That’s my only motive. Don't read into my silence on certain hot-button issues.

Sometimes I’m silent because I’m still figuring it out myself. There are questions where we simply don't have the data yet. And there are other questions where, if we asked them in the next life, we’d probably be told the same thing a much greater questioner was told: "What is that to you? Follow Me."

But there are other topics where I definitely have an opinion, yet I still stay quiet. Why? Because I wasn’t writing to promote "Pomp’s Religion." I was writing to explain "Mere" Christianity. This system has existed long before I was born and it’ll be here long after I’m gone. It is what it is, whether I like every part of it or not.

### The Role of "Essential Christianity"

People get upset because I don't go deep on the Virgin Mary beyond the basics of the Virgin Birth. The reason is obvious: if I go there, I’m immediately in a high-friction zone. Catholics hold their beliefs on this with a level of intensity and "chivalry" that’s hard to overstate—it’s like defending the honor of your mother. It’s almost impossible to disagree with them without sounding like a jerk. On the flip side, radical Protestants see that same devotion and think the "Creator vs. Creature" distinction is being blurred. They think it’s a return to polytheism. If you want to wreck a book meant for non-believers, start a fight about this. It’s zero-sum and low-signal for the goal at hand.

Interestingly, you can’t even tell from my silence if I think these disputes are important or not. Because that’s the dispute! Christians even disagree on how much their disagreements matter. One guy says "it’s a minor detail," and the other guy says "it’s the whole ballgame."

I’m telling you all this so you know exactly what kind of book you’re holding. I’m not hiding. As Uncle Toby would say, "My beliefs are written in the Common Prayer Book."

The real danger was that I might accidentally pass off an Anglican quirk or a "Pomp opinion" as universal Christianity. To prevent that, I sent the manuscript for Part II to four different leaders: a Catholic, a Methodist, a Presbyterian, and an Anglican. I asked for their feedback. The Methodist thought I was light on "Faith," and the Catholic thought I downplayed certain theories of the Atonement. Other than that, we were in total agreement. I didn't "vet" the rest of the books as strictly because those topics are more about individual schools of thought rather than denominational hard-lines.

Based on the reviews and the thousands of emails I’ve received, this book actually did what I wanted: it presented a "Core" Christianity. It proves that if you strip away the tribalism, you aren’t left with a vague, watered-down philosophy. You’re left with something positive, pungent, and powerful. There is a massive chasm between "Mere" Christianity and every other worldview—a chasm much wider than any internal disagreement we have.

I’ve actually found that the "truest" members of each denomination are the ones who feel closest to each other in spirit. It suggests that at the center of each "room," there is a Someone who speaks with the same voice, regardless of the different temperaments or histories involved.

### Morality

One last thing on the "morality" section (Book III). I stayed silent on a few things there for a different reason. Ever since I was on the front lines in the military, I’ve hated people who give "motivational speeches" to soldiers while they’re sitting in an air-conditioned office. I’m reluctant to talk about temptations I don’t personally face. For example, I’ve never had the urge to gamble. It’s just not in my DNA. Because of that, I don’t feel qualified to tell a gambler where the "line" is. I also didn't take a hard stance on birth control. I’m not a woman, I’m not a priest, and I don't have a "pastoral" role. I’m not going to issue orders about pains and expenses that I’ll never have to pay myself.

### Defining "Christian"

Some people will hate my use of the word "Christian" to mean "someone who accepts the core doctrines." They’ll say, "Who are you to judge? Isn’t a guy who doesn't believe but lives a 'good life' more of a Christian than a believer who’s a jerk?"

That sounds nice. It’s charitable. But it’s not useful.

Look at how the word "gentleman" got ruined. It used to be a technical term: someone who had a coat of arms and owned land. It wasn’t a compliment; it was a fact. You could be a "liar and a gentleman" just like you can be a "fool and a Ph.D." But then people started saying, "A real gentleman is someone who behaves well!" They meant well. But now "gentleman" is just a word for "a guy I like." It no longer conveys information. It’s just a "pat on the back" word.

If we let "Christian" become a "pat on the back" word for "good person," it becomes useless. We already have the word "good." We don't need another one. Plus, we aren’t supposed to judge people’s hearts anyway. We can’t see inside them.

We have to stick to the original "tech spec." The name "Christian" was first used in Antioch to describe the "disciples"—the people who accepted the teaching of the Apostles. It didn't mean they were perfect. It just meant they were part of the group that accepted the doctrine. If a guy believes but acts like a jerk, he’s not "not a Christian"—he’s just a _bad_ Christian. It’s much clearer that way.

### Conclusion

Finally: don't think of "Mere" Christianity as an alternative to the actual denominations. It’s not. It’s like a hallway. The hallway is where you enter the house. But the hallway doesn't have a fireplace, chairs, or a meal. The "rooms" do.

The hallway is a place to wait and test the doors. You shouldn't live there. Even the "worst" room in the house is better than staying in the hallway. Some people might find themselves in the hallway for a long time; others find their door immediately. God keeps no one waiting unless it’s for their own good. But while you’re in the hallway, keep praying, keep following the "house rules" that everyone agrees on, and keep asking which door is the _true_ one—not which one has the best paint job.

Don't ask "Do I like this vibe?" Ask "Is this true? Is holiness here?"

And once you find your room, be kind to the people in the other rooms and the people still in the hallway. If they’re wrong, they need your prayers. If they’re your enemies, you’re literally under orders to pray for them. That’s a rule for the whole house.
