## The Human Glitch

Look, let me tell you something I've been thinking about lately. There's this bizarre contradiction about human nature that nobody really talks about, and honestly, it's the most important thing to understand about how we're all wired.

Here's the deal: Humans are obsessed with _fairness_. We talk about it constantly. We call it morality, decency, playing by the rules, whatever you want to label it. We're literally haunted by this idea that we _ought_ to behave a certain way.

But here's the kicker—**we don't actually do it.** Not consistently. Not even close.

I know what you're probably thinking: "Pomp, isn't that just... normal? People aren't perfect. Why are you making a big deal out of this?"

Fair question. And yeah, that would be a solid response if I was trying to calculate exactly how much blame each of us deserves for being mediocre. But that's not what I'm doing here. I'm not interested in the blame game. What I _am_ interested in is understanding what's actually true about reality.

And here's where it gets weird.

## The Tree Test

Take a rock. Just a regular stone. It sits there being a stone. Nobody blames it for anything. Sure, you might say "this stone is the wrong shape for my project," but you're not actually _angry_ at the stone, right? You know that given the geological conditions, the weather, the soil—that stone literally could not have been any different. It's just being what it is.

Same with a tree. A tree doesn't give you as much shade as you'd like? Well, that sucks for your backyard plans, but you're not morally outraged at the tree. The tree is just obeying the laws of nature.

Here’s the realization: When we talk about "laws of nature"—like gravity—those aren't really "laws" in the sense of orders. They’re just descriptions of what things _actually do_. Stones fall. They don't "remember" an order to fall; they just fall. There is no "ought" involved. It’s just 1:1 data.

### **Where Everything Changes**

But humans? That's a completely different ballgame.

The thing is, when we talk about the Human Protocol—decent behavior, fairness, all that—we're NOT just describing what humans actually do. Because here's the proof: people straight-up _don't_ do it. And nobody does it completely.

Gravity tells you what stones _will_ do. The Human Protocol tells you what humans _ought_ to do—and they don't do it.

So you've got two different things happening here:

1. **The facts**: How humans actually behave
2. **Something else**: How humans _should_ behave

In the rest of the universe, you might just have the facts. Electrons and molecules do their thing, results happen, end of story. But with humans, there's something _extra_. There's this gap between reality and the standard that reality isn't meeting. And we all know that gap exists.

## **You Can't Explain It Away**

Now, people _try_ to rationalize this away. They say, "Look, when you say someone _ought_ to behave differently, you just mean that their behavior is inconvenient to you."

Nope. That doesn't hold up.

Here's why: Two guys are sitting in the last seat on a train. Guy A got there first. Guy B snuck in while I wasn't looking and moved my bag. Both are equally inconvenient to me. But I blame Guy B and not Guy A. I'm not actually angry—except maybe for a second—at a guy who accidentally trips me. But I _am_ furious at a guy who tries to trip me even if he fails. The first guy hurt me. The second guy didn't. Yet one earns my rage and one doesn't. That's not about convenience.

Or consider wartime. A traitor to the enemy side is _useful_ to us. We might even pay him. But we view him as human garbage. We don't respect the behavior even though it benefits us. You can't explain moral judgment through utility. It doesn't work.

And decent behavior in _ourselves_? Come on. That's not what pays:

- Taking \$30 when you could grab \$300
- Doing honest work when cheating is easy
- Respecting boundaries when you don't want to
- Staying in danger when you could escape
- Keeping promises that hurt you to keep
- Telling the truth when it makes you look stupid

These aren't profitable. They're the opposite of profitable. Yet we _know_ we ought to do them.

## **The Circular Argument Problem**

Some people say, "Well, decent behavior is what helps society as a whole." Okay, sure. That's actually true—you can't build a functioning society where everyone's ripping each other off.

But that's not actually explaining _why_ we should care about society.

If I ask you, "Why should I be unselfish?" and you answer, "Because it benefits society," then I can just respond, "Yeah, but why should I care about society if it doesn't benefit _me_ personally?" Now you're stuck saying, "Well, you _ought_ to care about society," which just brings us back to the beginning. You're arguing in circles.

It's like if someone asked, "What's the point of football?" and you said, "To score goals." Well yeah, but scoring goals _is_ football. You're just saying "football is football"—true, but useless.

Same thing here. If the answer to "Why be decent?" is "To benefit society," and being decent basically means trying to benefit society, then you're just saying "You should be decent because you should be decent." You're not actually explaining anything.

## **Here's the Real Answer**

This is what actually matters: **Men ought to be unselfish. They ought to be fair.**

Not that they _like_ to be or _are_ unselfish. They ought to be.

The LHuman Protocol isn't just a description of how humans behave, like gravity describes how objects behave. It's not some random preference we invented for our own convenience either, because immoral behavior doesn't always inconvenience us—sometimes it helps us.

What this means is that this Rule of Right and Wrong—the Human Protocol, whatever you want to call it—**has to be something real.** Something that actually exists. Not something we made up. Not just a feeling we have.

But it's also not a "fact" in the normal sense. It's not observable in the way behavior is observable.

So here's what we're forced to conclude: There's more than one kind of reality. There's the reality of what people actually do. And then there's another kind of reality—something real, something that exists, something none of us created—that tells us what we _ought_ to do.

That's the strangest thing about human beings. And it changes everything about how we understand the world.
