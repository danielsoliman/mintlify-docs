## The Signal in the Noise

Look, everyone has seen people getting into it on Twitter or arguing in a checkout line. Sometimes it’s just noise, and sometimes it’s actually heated. But if you stop and look at the "signal" behind the noise, you’ll learn something foundational about how humans operate.

People say stuff like: “How would you feel if I did that to you?” — “I was in this lane first.” — “Leave him alone, he’s not hurting anyone.” — “Why do you get to skip the line?” — “I Venmo’d you for lunch last week, it’s your turn.” — “You gave me your word.”

## The Shared Protocol

We hear this every single day. It doesn’t matter if you’re a billionaire or a guy working minimum wage, or if you’re a kid or a retiree.

The fascinating thing here is that the person complaining isn't just saying, “I don’t like what you’re doing.” They are appealing to a shared "Protocol"—a standard of behavior they expect the other person to already have downloaded. And the other person almost never says, “I don't recognize your protocol.” Instead, they try to find a loophole. They argue that their specific situation didn't actually break the rule, or that there was an "edge case" that excused it.

It’s like they both have this underlying Code of Fair Play or Morality that they’ve already agreed on. If they hadn’t, they’d just fight like animals. You can’t "quarrel" unless there’s a shared baseline of what’s right and wrong. You can’t call a "foul" in a game of basketball unless everyone has agreed to the same rulebook beforehand.

## Software vs Hard Coding

This used to be called the "Law of Nature." Today, when we say "laws of nature," we’re usually talking about gravity or physics—the "hard-coded" stuff. But the old-school thinkers called the Law of Right and Wrong the "Law of Nature," they really meant the "Law of Human Nature"—the specific "Software" for humans.

Just like a rock is governed by gravity, humans have a law. But there’s a massive difference: a rock doesn't have a choice. It has to obey gravity. A human being can choose to follow the Human Protocol or they can choose to ignore it.

Think of it this way: You’re subject to multiple sets of laws. As a physical object, you’re subject to gravity. If you jump off a ledge, you’re falling—you don’t get a vote. As a biological organism, you’re subject to chemistry and biology. But the law that is unique to humans—the one we don’t share with rocks or plants—is the one we can choose to break if we want.

People called it the "Law of Nature" because they assumed it was intuitive. You didn't need to go to Harvard to learn it. Sure, you might find a few "broken" files—people who are morally color-blind—but for the most part, the standard for decent behavior was obvious.

## The Standard of Decency

Some folks argue that the idea of a universal moral law is flawed because different cultures have different morals. But that’s not entirely true. While there are differences, they’re not as vast as you might think. If you compare the moral teachings of ancient civilizations like the Egyptians, Babylonians, and Greeks, you’ll find they’re remarkably similar to each other and to ours. If you imagine a society where cowardice is celebrated or betrayal is a point of pride, it’s as absurd as saying two plus two equals five. People have always agreed that selfishness isn’t admirable, even if they’ve disagreed on other specifics.

The most striking thing is when someone claims not to believe in real right and wrong, they often contradict themselves. They might break a promise to you, but if you do the same to them, they’ll cry foul. A nation might dismiss treaties, but then argue that a particular treaty was unfair. If there’s no right or wrong, what makes one treaty fair and another not? It’s clear they know the Law of Nature just like everyone else.

So, we’re forced to accept that real right and wrong exist. People might get it wrong sometimes, just like they make mistakes in math, but it’s not just opinion. If we agree on that, here’s the next point: none of us truly follow this Law of Nature. If you’re an exception, I apologize—this isn’t for you. But for the rest of us, let’s be real. I’m not preaching; I’m just pointing out that today, or this month, or even this very day, we’ve failed to live up to the standards we expect from others.

We have excuses, sure. Maybe you were unfair to the kids because you were exhausted. That sketchy money deal happened when you were broke. And that promise you didn’t keep? You didn’t know how busy you’d be. We all have reasons for our behavior, especially when it’s bad. But if we didn’t believe in decent behavior, why would we need excuses at all? The truth is, we believe in decency so much that we can’t stand admitting we’re breaking the rules, so we shift the blame.

These are the two key points: first, humans everywhere have this idea that they should behave a certain way, and they can’t shake it. Second, they don’t always behave that way. They know the Law of Nature, and they break it. These facts are the foundation for understanding ourselves and the universe we live in.
