> Let's talk about something the internet has completely broken: our collective understanding of sexual morality. Here's the thing — the most important signals are always the ones people are actively avoiding. So let's go.

## First, Let's Separate Two Things Everyone Conflates

Look, before we get into the hard stuff, we need to make a distinction that almost nobody makes correctly.

There is **chastity** — a moral standard. And there is **modesty** — a social standard. These are not the same thing, and mixing them up is one of the most expensive intellectual mistakes you can make.

Modesty is contextual. It's a community protocol. What's considered appropriate in a Miami rooftop pool party in 2026 is different from what was appropriate in a corporate boardroom in 1955. That's fine. Social norms are version-controlled. They update. They should update.

Chastity is different. It's not a social protocol. It's a deeper operating principle. A woman in 1890 covered head-to-toe and a woman in 2026 in a bikini at a beach resort can both be equally chaste — or equally not. The clothes are the UI. Chastity is the underlying code.

So when older generations see younger people being more open about their bodies or their sexuality and immediately conclude _"corrupt,"_ that's a category error. And when younger people see older generations maintaining traditional standards and immediately label them _"repressed prudes,"_ that's also a category error.

The real question has never been about dress code. It's about intent and integrity. If you're violating social norms specifically to manufacture lust in yourself or others, that's a chastity problem. If you're doing it because you're oblivious or because the norms genuinely shifted, that's just a social calibration issue — bad manners at worst, not a moral failure.

We can be adults about this.

## Here's the Actual Rule — And It's Brutal

I'm not going to soften this. The Christian rule on chastity is one of the most demanding standards in human history:

**Either full commitment within marriage, or complete abstinence outside it.**

That's it. No middle ground. No "mostly faithful." No "it's complicated."

Now, I'll be direct with you: this rule is so demanding, so contrary to how most people operate in 2026, that you're left with only two logical conclusions:

1. The rule is wrong.
2. Our sexual instinct, as currently configured, is running a corrupted program.

There's no third option. The math doesn't work any other way.

I think it's option two. Here's my evidence.

## The Appetite Has Gone Way Off-Script

Every human appetite has a biological function. Hunger exists to fuel the body. The sexual drive exists, biologically, to reproduce.

Now, compare how far each appetite drifts from its core function.

If you let someone eat freely, with zero discipline, they might consume two or three times what they need. That's real. That's a problem. But it's a bounded problem. Nobody eats enough for fifty people. The appetite overshoots its target, but not by an insane margin.

Now model the sexual appetite the same way. A healthy person, acting on every impulse, with no framework — the overshoot isn't 2x or 3x. It's orders of magnitude beyond the biological function. The appetite isn't slightly miscalibrated. It's running a completely different program than the one it was designed for.

This is a system that has drifted _dramatically_ from its original spec.

Here's an analogy that lands harder in 2026: Imagine a food delivery app — let's say a next-gen DoorDash — where the entire business model is built not around getting you food, but around showing you videos of other people eating food. Just the videos. Just the anticipation. And people are _addicted_ to it. Billions of daily active users just... scrolling pictures of food they'll never eat.

You'd immediately say: _something is deeply broken with these people's relationship to food._

That's the current state of the sexual content economy. We have built a multi-trillion dollar industry — social media feeds, streaming platforms, entire algorithmic systems — optimized entirely around sexual stimulation with no connection to its actual biological or relational function. And we're calling this _normal_.

## The "Starvation" Hypothesis Doesn't Hold Up

Here's the counter-argument you'll hear: _"People are obsessed with sex because they're sexually repressed. Open it all up, remove the shame, and the obsession goes away."_

It's a clean hypothesis. Let's stress-test it.

If the obsession is caused by scarcity, then increasing supply should reduce demand. That's basic economics.

So — has supply increased? Dramatically. Access to sexual content and sexual activity is higher in 2026 than at any point in recorded human history. The friction has been engineered out of the system almost entirely. Apps, algorithms, contraception, shifting social norms — every barrier has been systematically lowered.

Has the obsession decreased? No. It has _intensified_.

The starvation hypothesis fails on the data.

And here's the second problem with that argument: appetites don't work like that. The appetite for food doesn't decrease when you eat more — it recalibrates to expect more. The same dynamic applies here. Indulgence doesn't satisfy the appetite. It _trains_ the appetite to demand more. The gorged and the starving both think about food constantly, just for different reasons.

The "just open it all up and everything will be fine" framework was a hypothesis. The last two decades were the experiment. We have the results. The hypothesis was wrong.

## We've Been Fed Bad Data

Let me be direct about something that I think is one of the most important points in this entire piece.

For the last twenty-plus years, there has been a coordinated — if not always intentional — campaign to convince you that the current state of the sexual instinct is healthy, normal, and fine. That any discomfort you feel about it is a legacy bug from outdated religious programming that needs to be patched out.

Here's what's actually true: **sex itself is not the problem.** The physical reality of human reproduction, the pleasure associated with it, the intimacy — none of that is the problem. The Christian framework, properly understood, doesn't say the body is bad. It doesn't say pleasure is bad. In fact, the original theological argument is that if humanity hadn't gone off-script, sexual pleasure would be _more_ intense, not less. The body is considered good. Matter is considered good.

The problem is the _state_ the instinct has gotten into. And those are completely different claims.

Here's the analogy: There's nothing wrong with food. There would be something deeply wrong with a civilization where half the population spent their days scrolling through food porn, had lost the ability to enjoy an actual meal, and were being systematically manipulated by a \$500 billion industry designed to keep their hunger response permanently activated so they'd keep clicking.

That second scenario — that's the problem. Not the food.

And here's the business model hiding underneath all of it: **an obsessed person has no sales resistance.** If you can keep someone's sexual appetite perpetually inflamed — never satisfied, always wanting — you have a customer with infinite demand and zero discipline. That is an extraordinarily valuable consumer to own.

There are people getting very, very rich off your inability to get this area of your life under control. That's not a conspiracy theory. That's an incentive structure. Follow the money.

## The Standard Is Hard. Do It Anyway.

Now let's talk about execution, because this is where most frameworks fall apart.

**Objection #1: "It's impossible."**

Look — when something is optional, you calculate probability before attempting it. When something is necessary, you don't. You execute and figure it out.

Think about the first time anyone tried to build a company with no revenue model, or climb a mountain with no established route, or ship a product that had never existed before. Every one of those things looked impossible until someone did it. The human capacity to achieve things that seem impossible _before_ attempting them is one of the most consistently underestimated variables in any planning model.

Perfect chastity won't be achieved through pure willpower and optimized systems. You'll need help beyond yourself — call that God, call that grace, call that whatever framework you operate in. But the operational principle is the same: after every failure, you reset, you request support, and you try again. The goal isn't a perfect record. The goal is to never stop trying.

The process of consistently trying — even while failing — is itself the training. It builds something in you that matters more than any single outcome.

**Objection #2: "Resisting these desires is psychologically dangerous — repression causes damage."**

This is a real misread of the actual psychology.

_Repression_ is a technical term. It means a desire that has been pushed into the subconscious — usually early in development — and can no longer be consciously recognized. It shows up in disguised forms. That is a genuine psychological risk.

_Resistance_ is completely different. Consciously recognizing a desire and choosing not to act on it is not repression. It is the opposite of repression. It requires full awareness.

In fact, people who are seriously working on this area of their lives tend to develop _more_ self-knowledge about their own sexuality than anyone else. They study it the way a serious investor studies a volatile asset class — with discipline, with curiosity, with the goal of understanding the patterns rather than being controlled by them. Virtue creates clarity. Indulgence creates noise.

## The Bottom Line

Here's the realization I want you to walk away with:

**Sexual morality is not the center of the moral framework — but the way we've broken it reveals something important about every other system in our lives.**

The Christian position is not that sexual sin is the worst sin. It's actually the opposite. The sins of the flesh — acting on physical appetites beyond their proper function — are considered the _least_ serious category of moral failure.

The worst failures? They're cognitive and spiritual. The pleasure of manipulating others. The satisfaction of being the one who's always right. The quiet thrill of watching someone else fail. The love of power for its own sake. Those are the dangerous ones — precisely because they're invisible, they're dressed up as virtues, and they scale.

A self-righteous person who has their physical appetites perfectly controlled but who runs on contempt, status games, and the need to dominate — that person is in far worse shape than someone who has genuinely struggled with physical temptation and keeps getting back up.

The goal is to be neither.

The goal is to get your whole system running on the right operating principles — not just the parts that are visible to others.

That's the signal.
