Hope isn't soft. Hope isn't cope. In Christian theology, Hope is a core virtue — and the argument is that fixing your eyes on eternity isn't escapism or delusion. It's actually the _highest-leverage_ orientation a person can have.

Here's the thing most people get completely backwards: they assume that the people most obsessed with the next world are the ones who do the least in this one. History destroys that thesis entirely.

The Apostles planted a movement that restructured the most powerful empire on Earth. The architects of the medieval world — its cathedrals, its universities, its legal frameworks — were men who prayed more than they slept. The English Evangelicals who _abolished the transatlantic slave trade_ weren't motivated by political capital or public approval ratings. They were motivated by Heaven. These weren't people who opted out of the present world. They were its most consequential operators — precisely _because_ they were optimizing for something beyond it.

The moment Christians stopped thinking seriously about eternity, their impact on the present collapsed. That's not a coincidence. That's cause and effect.

> **The counterintuitive principle: Aim at Heaven, and you get Earth thrown in. Aim only at Earth, and you get neither.**

It sounds paradoxical. But you can see this dynamic running everywhere once you know what to look for. Obsess over your health metrics — track every macro, stress every biomarker — and you become a hypochondriac. You get less healthy, not more. But pursue things you actually love — movement, competition, meaningful work — and health shows up as a byproduct. The same logic applies to civilization itself. If "saving civilization" becomes the primary goal, you've already lost. You need to want something _more_ than civilization to actually preserve it.

## **The Desire That Doesn't Resolve**

Look — most of us struggle to genuinely _want_ Heaven. And I get it. The concept feels abstract. The closest most people get is wanting to see the people they've lost. But that's not the full picture.

There are two reasons we struggle here.

**First:** we've been trained out of it. Every system around us — education, media, social feeds — is optimized to keep our attention locked on this world. We are not taught to look further.

**Second:** when the real desire for something beyond this world _does_ show up, we don't recognize it for what it is.

Here's what I mean. Think about the first time you fell in love. Or the first time you landed in a foreign city that felt electric. Or the first time you discovered a subject that completely consumed you — maybe it was markets, maybe it was code, maybe it was physics. There was a _moment_ in there. A feeling. Something you were reaching for that felt enormous.

And then — you stayed. You got the relationship, the trips, the expertise. And it was good. Maybe genuinely great. But the thing you were originally reaching for? It quietly slipped away. The feeling didn't scale with the acquisition.

I'm not talking about bad marriages or mediocre vacations. I'm talking about the _best_ versions of those things. The great marriage. The dream trip. The career that objectively worked out. And still — something evaded you.

This is one of the most universal human experiences, and almost nobody talks about it directly.

There are three ways people respond to this gap — two of them are wrong.

### **Response #1: The Serial Optimizer (Wrong)**

This person concludes the problem is the _specific_ thing they chose, not the structure of the desire itself.

_Next relationship, this time it'll click. Next company, next city, next asset class, next exit — this time I'll finally feel it._

You know this person. You've probably been this person. The VC who chases the next fund raise immediately after closing the last one. The founder who sells for \$50M and feels nothing, so they immediately start another company hoping the feeling will be bigger at \$500M. The person cycling through relationships, always convinced the current one is finally "the real thing," always quietly disappointed when the initial spark doesn't compound.

These are not bad people. They're just running the wrong mental model. They keep iterating on the variable when the architecture itself is the issue.

### **Response #2: The Rational Defeatist (Also Wrong, But More Respectable)**

This person does the math, concludes the desire is irrational, and systematically suppresses it.

_"Sure, I felt that way when I was young. But I've grown up. I've learned to manage expectations."_

They optimize for contentment. They stop chasing signals that they've decided are noise. They become stable, functional, low-drama — and they quietly look down on anyone still "chasing the dream" as naive.

This is a more sophisticated response than the first one. It produces a calmer life. But here's the critical flaw in the logic:

**What if the desire isn't irrational? What if it's pointing at something real?**

If there is, in fact, something on the other side of death that actually satisfies that desire — something that doesn't fade, doesn't disappoint, doesn't evade you — then suppressing your capacity to want it is the worst possible trade you could make. You'd be voluntarily downgrading your long-term upside to manage short-term volatility. That's not rationality. That's risk-aversion masquerading as wisdom.

You'd only discover the mistake one moment too late.

### **Response #3: The Christian Framework (The Correct Read)**

The Christian argument is this: **desires don't exist without corresponding satisfactions.**

This isn't wishful thinking. It's pattern recognition.

A newborn is hungry — food exists. A child wants to swim — water exists. These aren't cosmic jokes. The desire is a signal pointing toward a real thing. So when you find a desire inside yourself that _nothing in this world can satisfy_ — not the relationship, not the exit, not the status, not the experience — the most logical explanation isn't that the universe is broken. It's that you were built for a different environment.

The things of this world aren't frauds. They're previews. They're not meant to fully deliver — they're meant to point. The first hit of a great idea, the early days of a great partnership, the moment a business model clicks into place — these are real. They're just not the destination. They're signals pointing toward something the signal itself cannot contain.

The move, then, is this: **Don't despise the earthly things. But don't confuse them for the final thing.** Keep the deeper desire alive. Don't let it get buried under busyness or cynicism or the next optimization target. Make the pursuit of that true destination the organizing principle of your life — and bring others along with you.

## **A Note on the Optics Objection**

Some people mock the idea of Heaven by making it sound like an eternal HOA meeting with mandatory harp practice. If that's your objection, you're not engaging with the actual argument — you're arguing against a caricature.

The imagery in scripture — harps, gold, crowns — is symbolic. It's humans trying to use language to point at something language can't fully contain. Music gets used because for many people, music is the closest this-world experience to something that feels like infinity. Gold gets used because it doesn't rust — it's pointing at timelessness and incorruptibility. Crowns point at the idea that being united with God means sharing in something of His nature — power, joy, splendor.

Taking that imagery literally is like reading "be wise as serpents" and concluding Jesus was advocating for reptilian behavior. It's a failure of reading comprehension, not a theological insight.

## **The Bottom Line**

**The highest-leverage orientation you can have is to want something beyond what this world can deliver — and to let that desire organize your behavior in the present.**

This isn't about checking out. History's most impactful operators were the ones with the longest time horizons — and the longest time horizon available is eternity. Aim there, and you'll build something worth building here. Aim only here, and you'll find that even the wins feel strangely hollow.

The desire that nothing in this world satisfies? That's not a bug. That's the most important signal you have.

Don't suppress it. Follow it.
