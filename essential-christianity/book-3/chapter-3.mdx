#### The Human Protocol: Why Nobody Wants to Run the Full Stack

Look, we need to get something straight about human coordination—the thing most people call "morality" or "ethics" but what I'm going to call **the Human Protocol**.

Here's what's fascinating: When you actually study the great operators throughout history—the people who built systems that lasted—they weren't inventing new rules. They were reminding people of the rules everyone already knew but desperately wanted to ignore.

The Golden Rule? "Treat others how you want to be treated"? That's not some revolutionary insight from 2,000 years ago. That's **Layer 1 of the human operating system**. It's baked into the protocol. Everyone knows it. We've always known it.

The real moral teachers—the ones who actually moved the needle—they weren't selling you a new framework. The charlatans and grifters? Those are the ones constantly pitching you the "new paradigm." The legitimate teachers? They're just doing the hard, boring work of bringing you back to fundamentals you keep trying to avoid. It's like a coach making you run the same drill over and over, or a teacher making you redo the problem you keep getting wrong. Not sexy. But necessary.

## Christianity Isn't a Product Roadmap

Here's the second thing people get wrong: Christianity (or any serious moral framework) **is not a detailed implementation guide** for your specific time and place.

Think about it. A protocol designed for all humans across all contexts can't give you a step-by-step playbook for 2026 America. What worked in 1st-century Rome won't map 1:1 to modern-day New York. The contexts are too different.

And honestly? That's not the point.

When the protocol says "feed the hungry," it's not teaching you how to cook. When it says "read the primary sources," it's not giving you a linguistics course. **It's a directive layer, not an execution layer.** It points your existing skills—economics, engineering, design, whatever—toward the right objectives. It's the energy source that powers the machine, assuming you're willing to plug in.

People love to say, "The Church should give us a lead." And sure, that's true—if you understand what that actually means.

When they say "the Church," they should mean **the entire network of people running the protocol**—not just the clergy. And when they say "give us a lead," they should mean that Christians with the right skill sets should be in the arena: economists, founders, policymakers. And those people should be **optimizing for the Human Protocol in everything they do**.

If that happened—and if the rest of us were actually ready to listen—we'd solve our coordination problems fast.

But here's what people usually mean: They want the clergy to publish a white paper on economic policy. Which is absurd. The clergy are specialists trained for a very specific function—helping people think about infinite time horizons, not quarterly earnings. Asking them to design your trade policy is like asking a cryptographer to run your marketing team. Wrong skill set.

The application of these principles to education, labor markets, media—that has to come from **Christian educators, Christian operators, Christian builders**. Just like great Christian art comes from Christian artists, not from a committee of bishops trying to write screenplays in their spare time.

## What a Fully Optimized System Actually Looks Like (And Why You'll Hate It)

That said, the source material does give us a pretty clear picture of what a society running the full Human Protocol would look like. And I'll be honest: **it's probably more than most of us can handle**.

First, no free riders. If you don't work, you don't eat. Everyone works with their hands. And here's the kicker: everyone's work has to **produce something actually good**. No manufacturing garbage products and then spending 10x the budget on ads to manipulate people into buying them. No monetizing anxiety. No engagement-farming. Just value creation.

Also, no flexing. No status games. No "building in public" just to farm clout. To that extent, it would look like what we'd call a **left-leaning system**.

On the other hand, it's **heavily hierarchical**. Obedience to legitimate authority. Respect for parents. And—brace yourself—traditional family structures with husbands as heads of household. That part's going to make a lot of people close the tab.

Third, it's a **high-energy, optimistic culture**. Lots of celebration. Worry and anxiety are considered bugs, not features. Courtesy matters. And it has zero tolerance for busybodies and people who can't mind their own business.

If you visited this society, here's what you'd think: "Wow, the economic system feels like democratic socialism. But the family structure and social norms feel... old-fashioned. Maybe even aristocratic."

**Each of us would like parts of it. Almost none of us would like the whole thing.**

And that's exactly what you'd expect if this is the **total design spec for the human machine**. We've all forked the code in different directions, and each of us wants to pretend _our_ fork is the canonical version. That's why we don't make progress. That's why people fighting for opposite things both claim they're "fighting for what's right."

## The Incentive Misalignment Nobody Wants to Talk About

Here's something wild. The ancient Greeks, the Old Testament Jews, and the medieval Christian scholars all agreed on one thing: **Don't lend money at interest**.

And yet, our entire modern economic system is built on interest—what we call investment. The foundation of venture capital, mortgages, corporate debt, everything.

Now, maybe they were wrong. Maybe they couldn't foresee the joint-stock company and were only thinking about predatory moneylenders. I'm not an economist—I can't tell you if the investment system is why we're in the mess we're in. **This is where we need Christian economists in the arena**, not just Christian pastors tweeting Bible verses.

But I'd be lying if I didn't tell you: three of the most successful civilizations in history all condemned the very thing we've based our entire financial system on. That should at least make you pause.

## The Uncomfortable Math on Giving

One more thing. The source material says you should work **"so that you may have something to give to those in need."** Charity—direct giving to the poor—is non-negotiable. In one of the most intense parables, it's literally the deciding factor.

Some people today say, "Charity shouldn't be necessary. We should build a system where there are no poor people." And sure, maybe we should build that system.

But if you think that means you can **stop giving in the meantime**, you've completely parted ways with the protocol.

I don't think there's a formula for how much to give. But here's the only safe heuristic: **Give more than you can spare.**

If your spending on comforts, luxuries, and entertainment is at or above the standard for your income bracket, you're probably not giving enough. If your charitable giving doesn't pinch—if it doesn't force you to skip things you'd like to do—it's too small. There should be trade-offs. Real ones.

And sometimes, specific cases—family members, neighbors, employees—might demand even more. To the point where it threatens your own position. That's the design.

For most of us, the real blocker isn't greed. It's **fear**. Fear of insecurity. Fear of not having enough. And we need to recognize that for what it is: a temptation, not a rational constraint.

Sometimes it's pride. We'd rather spend on showy generosity—the stuff people see, like big tips and fancy dinners—than on the people who actually need help.

## The Real Snag

Here's my guess: Some of you are mad I didn't go further left. Some of you are mad I went too far. If that's true, **we've hit the real problem**.

Most of us aren't reading this to find out what the protocol actually says. We're reading it hoping to find **ammunition for the position we already hold**. We want an ally. We're being offered a standard—or a judge.

I'm guilty of this too. There are parts of this I wanted to leave out.

And that's why none of this matters unless we go the long way around. A society running the Human Protocol isn't going to show up until most of us actually want it. And we're not going to want it until we're running the protocol ourselves. I can tweet "treat others how you want to be treated" until I'm blue in the face, but I can't execute it until I genuinely care about others as much as I care about myself. And I can't do that until I care about the source of the protocol more than I care about my own optimization. And I can't do _that_ without obedience to something bigger than me.

So yeah, we're being pushed inward. From social systems to something deeper. From the outside game to the inside game.

**Because the longest way around is the shortest way home.**
