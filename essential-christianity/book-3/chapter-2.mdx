#### The Four Core Principles That Run Everything

Look, I'm going to level with you.

I usually break down human behavior into three buckets—think of it like ships in a convoy that need to stay coordinated. That framework is tight, efficient, gets the point across fast. Perfect for a 10-minute podcast hit.

But there's another mental model that's been battle-tested for literally thousands of years, and it's worth unpacking here because it maps perfectly onto how high-performers actually operate in 2026.

The old-school philosophers identified seven core operating principles—seven "virtues" if you want the classical term. Four of them are what they called "Cardinal" virtues (from the Latin word for "hinge"—as in, everything pivots on these). The other three are "Theological" virtues, which we'll get to later because they require a different frame entirely.

Today we're talking about the four Cardinal ones. These are the Layer 1 principles that _every_ functional civilization recognizes, regardless of their belief system:

**Critical Thinking, Self-Control, Justice, and Fortitude.**

Let's break down what these actually mean in practice.

---

## Critical Thinking

Critical Thinking is practical intelligence. It's thinking three moves ahead. It's asking "what happens next?" before you deploy capital—financial, social, or otherwise.

Here's the thing: most people in 2026 don't even register Critical Thinking as a virtue anymore. We've built this entire culture around "authentic expression" and "following your heart," and somehow that's gotten twisted into the idea that _thinking too hard_ about consequences makes you inauthentic or calculating.

Even worse, some people have weaponized the "childlike faith" concept to justify intellectual laziness. They'll say: "Jesus said we need to be like children, so as long as my heart's in the right place, it doesn't matter if I'm strategic about it."

That's a catastrophic misread.

Children are _extremely_ prudent about things they care about. Watch a 7-year-old optimize their Fortnite strategy or negotiate for more screen time—they're thinking probabilistically, running scenarios, adapting tactics in real-time.

What Jesus actually said was: be "harmless as doves" but _also_ "wise as serpents." Translation: maintain the emotional simplicity and trust of a child, but bring adult-level pattern recognition and strategic depth to everything you do.

The signal here is clear: **you're supposed to use every bit of cognitive horsepower you have.**

Donating to charity? Great. But that doesn't mean you get to skip due diligence on whether it's actually effective or just overhead-bloated virtue signaling. Thinking about God? Fantastic. But you don't get to coast on the same surface-level understanding you had when you were five. Upgrade your mental models. Read the hard books. Wrestle with the contradictions.

God doesn't love you less if you were born with a 95 IQ instead of 140. He's not running a meritocracy based on raw intelligence. But He absolutely expects you to _maximize whatever processing power you've got._

The correct framework isn't "be good and don't worry about being smart." It's: **"be good, which necessarily includes being as smart as you can possibly be."**

God has zero patience for intellectual slackers, just like He has zero patience for any other kind of slacker.

If you're seriously considering this path, I'm telling you straight up: it's going to require everything you have. Your whole stack. Brains included.

But here's the upside—and this is one of the most underrated feedback loops in existence: the more honestly you pursue this, the sharper your thinking becomes. Christianity isn't just compatible with intellectual rigor; _it is itself an education system._ That's why an uneducated guy like John Bunyan could write _Pilgrim's Progress_—a book that's still getting cited 350 years later.

---

## Restraint: Optimization, Not Deprivation

Okay, Restraint is tricky because the word has been completely hijacked.

In 2026, when people hear "restraint," they think total abstinence—usually around alcohol. But that's not what the original concept meant _at all._

Restraint wasn't about avoiding pleasure. It was about _optimizing_ pleasure. It meant going to the right level and stopping there. Not zero. Not excess. **The Goldilocks zone.**

Christianity is not a teetotaling religion—that's Islam. There's no blanket prohibition here.

Now, might there be specific situations where a specific person should abstain from alcohol? Absolutely:

- Maybe you're someone who can't moderate—one drink turns into ten
- Maybe you're reallocating that budget to higher-ROI uses
- Maybe you're around people struggling with addiction and your participation would be net-negative for the group

But here's the key: in those cases, you're _choosing_ to abstain from something you don't consider inherently bad. You're making a strategic sacrifice. You're not looking down on people who can handle it responsibly.

One of the clearest markers of a toxic personality type: **someone who can't give something up without needing everyone else to give it up too.** That's not the play.

A Christian might give up all kinds of things for specific reasons—marriage, meat, alcohol, social media, whatever. But the second they start claiming those things are evil _in themselves,_ or judging people who use them well, they stray from the principle.

And here's the other thing modern culture gets wrong: we've narrowed Restraint down to just the alcohol question, which lets people completely miss how indulgent they are about everything else.

The guy who's built his entire identity around golf? Indulgent.

The woman who thinks about her Peloton stats 24/7? Indulgent.

The dude who's refreshing his portfolio every 30 seconds? Indulgent.

Sure, these don't produce the same visible failure modes as alcoholism—you don't crash your car because you were scrolling Twitter too hard (well, actually...). But **God sees through the externals.** He's looking at what you've made the center of your operating system, and if it's _anything_ other than the one thing that's supposed to be there, you're out of balance.

---

## Justice: Fairness at Every Layer

Justice is bigger than courtrooms and contracts.

It's the old-school term for what we'd now call _fairness across all domains._ It includes:

- Honesty in your DMs
- Actually delivering what you promised
- Give-and-take in negotiations
- Not moving the goalposts mid-game
- Truthfulness even when it's costly

It's the entire relational and transactional stack.

---

## Fortitude: Guts

Fortitude covers both types of courage:

1. The kind that runs toward danger
2. The kind that endures pain without breaking (Grit)

In modern terms? **Guts.**

And you'll notice pretty quickly: you can't practice _any_ of the other virtues for long without this one coming online. Critical Thinking without Fortitude is just analysis paralysis. Restraint without Fortitude is just caving to social pressure. Justice without Fortitude is just performative fairness when it's convenient.

---

## The Difference Between Actions and Character

Here's the part most people miss, and it's _critical._

There's a massive difference between:

- Doing one just action
- Being a just person

A bad tennis player can occasionally hit a great shot. That doesn't make them a great player.

A great player is someone whose muscle memory, reflexes, and neural pathways have been trained through _thousands_ of good shots. They've developed a certain quality—a "tone"—that exists even when they're not on the court. Same way a mathematician has a certain way of thinking that's there even when they're not doing math.

When you repeatedly do just actions, you eventually develop a certain _quality of character._ And it's that quality—not the individual actions—that we actually mean when we talk about virtue.

**This distinction matters for three reasons:**

### 1. Motive Matters

If you only focus on the actions themselves, you might think: "As long as I did the right thing, who cares _why_ I did it?"

Wrong.

Right actions done for wrong reasons don't build the internal character quality. And it's the character that actually matters long-term.

If a bad tennis player hits a great shot, not because they saw the strategic opening, but because they got angry and swung hard out of frustration—sure, maybe they luck into winning that point. But it's not making them a better player. It's not building the skill.

### 2. God Wants a Type of Person, Not Just Compliance

You might think God just wants obedience to a ruleset. Like He's running some cosmic compliance department.

Nope.

He wants people _of a particular sort._ He's optimizing for character development, not checkbox behavior.

### 3. These Qualities Are Permanent Upgrades

You might think: "Okay, these virtues are useful for navigating earth, but in the afterlife we won't need them anymore. No conflicts, so no need for Justice. No dangers, so no need for Fortitude."

Partially true—but you're missing the point.

Sure, there probably won't be _occasions_ for courageous or just _actions_ in the next world. But there will be infinite occasions for _being the kind of person_ who can only be formed by doing those actions here.

God's not going to refuse you entry because you didn't check enough boxes. The point is: **if you haven't developed at least the beginnings of these character qualities, no external conditions could possibly make you happy in the way He's designed happiness to work.**

Heaven isn't a location you unlock.

It's a state you're _capable_ of experiencing—and you build that capacity here, through the deliberate practice of becoming a certain kind of person.

---

**Bottom line:**

These four principles—Critical Thinking, Restraint, Justice, Fortitude—aren't arbitrary rules.

They're the operating system for human flourishing.

And the goal isn't to white-knuckle your way through a checklist of good behaviors.

The goal is to become the kind of person for whom those behaviors flow naturally—because you've rewired yourself at the character level.

That's the long game.

That's the only game worth playing.
