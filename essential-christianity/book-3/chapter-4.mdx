Look, I'm going to be direct here: you can't build a high-functioning society on top of broken individuals. That's not idealism—that's just how systems work.

When people hear me say "we need better people before we can have better institutions," they think I'm punting to some distant future where everyone magically gets their act together. That's not what I'm saying.

**Here's what I mean:** We have to run both processes in parallel:

1. **The macro optimization:** Figure out how "treat others the way you want to be treated" actually scales in 2026—in our companies, our protocols, our incentive structures.
2. **The personal debugging:** Become the kind of person who would actually _execute_ on those principles if we figured out the implementation details.

Today I want to start unpacking what that second part looks like—what the spec actually is for a high-functioning human being.

But before we get into the weeds, I need to address two things that always come up.

---

## The Therapy Question

Since we're talking about "fixing" humans, you're probably wondering: how does this relate to therapy? To psychoanalysis? To all the mental health optimization we're doing in 2026?

**Here's the key distinction:** There's a difference between the _clinical technique_ of therapy—the actual medical practice—and the _philosophical worldview_ that Freud and others bolted onto it.

The philosophy? That's in direct conflict with what I'm talking about here. It's also in direct conflict with Jung's framework, by the way. And here's the thing: when Freud was doing clinical work, he was operating in his domain of expertise. When he started making sweeping philosophical claims, he was an amateur stepping outside his lane.

I'll listen to him on the first thing. I'll ignore him on the second.

And I'm comfortable doing that because I've seen him talk about subjects I _do_ know something about—like language and linguistics—and he was completely out of his depth.

**But the therapeutic technique itself?** That's not contradictory to what I'm describing. In fact, there's real overlap. It would probably help if more people understood both frameworks. They're just solving different parts of the problem.

---

## Two Different Layers of the Stack

When you make a choice—any choice—two things are happening:

1. **The decision itself:** The actual act of choosing.
2. **The inputs:** The feelings, impulses, and psychological patterns that your brain is presenting as options.

Now, those inputs can come in two flavors:

- **Normal/baseline inputs:** The kind of fear, desire, or anger that's standard-issue for humans. Fear of actual danger. Attraction to a potential partner. Hunger when you haven't eaten.
- **Corrupted inputs:** Irrational phobias. Compulsions that don't make sense. Psychological patterns that got wired wrong somewhere in your subconscious.

**What therapy does:** It debugs the corrupted inputs. It gives you cleaner data to work with when you're making decisions.

**What I'm talking about:** The decisions themselves. What you _do_ with the inputs, clean or corrupted.

---

## The Three Soldiers

Here's a concrete example.

Imagine three people heading into a high-stakes, dangerous situation—say, a war.

**Person A** has normal, baseline fear. The kind anyone would have. But they override it through sheer willpower and do their duty anyway.

**Person B and C** have _irrational_, exaggerated fear—something got wired wrong in their subconscious. No amount of willpower can touch it.

Now a therapist comes in and fixes B and C. Rewires the fear response. Puts them back at baseline, just like Person A.

**That's where the therapeutic work ends and the moral question begins.**

Because now that they're operating with clean inputs, B and C can go two completely different directions:

- **Person B** might say: "Thank God I'm not paralyzed by that anymore. Now I can finally do what I always wanted to do—step up and do the hard thing."
- **Person C** might say: "Great, now that I'm not freaking out, I can focus on protecting myself even more efficiently. I can look out for Number One and hide it better from everyone else."

**That difference is a moral one.** Therapy can't touch it. You can give someone the cleanest possible psychological inputs, and they still have to choose: Do I optimize for myself, or do I optimize for something bigger?

That free choice—that's the only thing morality cares about.

---

## Disease vs. Decision

The corrupted inputs—the bad psychological wiring—that's not a moral failing. **That's a bug.** It doesn't need repentance; it needs debugging.

And this is _critical_ to understand:

We judge people by their external results. The scoreboard. The outcomes.

But the actual scorekeeper—reality, the universe, whatever you want to call it—judges by the _choices_ made with the inputs available.

**The person with the irrational cat phobia who forces themselves to pick up a cat for a good reason?** That might represent more courage than someone without the phobia winning a medal of honor.

**The person who was raised in an environment that taught them cruelty was normal, and they manage one small act of kindness—or refrain from one cruel act—even though their peers will mock them for it?** That might be a harder choice than you or I giving up our entire career for a friend.

---

## The Reverse Angle

Flip this around:

Some of us who look like we have it together—who seem like decent people—might actually be _underperforming_ relative to the hand we were dealt.

Good genetics. Good upbringing. Clean psychological wiring. And we're doing... what, exactly, with all that?

Meanwhile, someone else got dealt a catastrophically bad hand—terrible psychological inputs, brutal upbringing, corrupted incentive structures—and they're grinding out small wins against massive resistance.

**Can you honestly say how you'd perform if you had their starting conditions?**

That's why the protocol is: don't judge. You're only seeing the external results—what someone did with their raw materials. You're not seeing the raw materials themselves.

Most of what makes up a person's psychological profile is tied to their biology. When that goes away, what's left is the core decision-making engine—the thing that chose, that optimized as best it could with what it had.

**And that's what gets evaluated in the long run.** Not the inputs. The choices.

A lot of the "good" things we think are ours—our patience, our good mood, our baseline optimism—might just be good digestion or clean neurochemistry. A lot of the "bad" things in others might be trauma or broken wiring.

Strip all that away, and you see people as they actually are. **There will be surprises.**

---

## The Compounding Question

Here's the second thing people get wrong:

They think morality is a **transaction.** "Follow the rules, get the reward. Break the rules, get punished."

That's not how this works.

**Here's the better model:**

Every time you make a choice, you're adjusting the core decision-making engine—the part of you that chooses. You're nudging it in one direction or another.

Over the course of your entire life, across thousands and thousands of micro-decisions, you're **compounding** that core self into one of two end states:

1. **A system in harmony.** Aligned with reality, with other people, with itself. This state is characterized by clarity, power, peace, and knowledge.
2. **A system at war.** Misaligned with reality, with others, with itself. This state is characterized by chaos, rage, isolation, and impotence.

To exist in the first state _is_ the reward. To exist in the second state _is_ the punishment.

You're progressing toward one or the other with every single choice.

---

## Why the Strict/Lenient Paradox Makes Sense

This explains something that used to confuse me about old moral frameworks:

They seem incredibly strict about small things—a bad thought, a moment of anger—and then incredibly lenient about massive things—betrayal, violence, catastrophic failures.

"Just repent and you're fine."

**But here's why that's actually correct:**

They're not thinking about the _external impact_. They're thinking about the **mark on the core decision-making engine.**

One person might be positioned such that their anger kills thousands. Another person might be positioned such that their anger just makes people laugh at them.

**But the mark on the core self might be identical.**

Both did something that, unless corrected, makes it harder to avoid rage next time. Makes the rage worse when it happens again.

Both can reverse the damage if they seriously commit to debugging. Both are doomed if they don't.

**The external scale doesn't matter.** What matters is the direction of the compounding.

---

## The Diagnostic Paradox

One last thing:

The better you get, the more clearly you see what's still broken in you.

The worse you get, the less you understand your own problems.

**A moderately broken person knows they're not great. A completely broken person thinks they're fine.**

This is just common sense:

- You understand sleep when you're awake, not when you're asleep.
- You see math errors when your mind is sharp, not while you're making them.
- You understand drunkenness when you're sober, not when you're drunk.

**High-functioning people understand both high and low performance. Low-functioning people understand neither.**

---

**Bottom line:**

You can't optimize the system without optimizing the individuals. And optimizing individuals means debugging the corrupted inputs _and_ training the core decision-making engine to make better choices.

Both matter. But they're different problems.

Don't confuse them.
