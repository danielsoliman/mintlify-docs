Quick question before we get into it.

Is following Christ hard or easy?

Most people pick a side. One camp says it's brutal, a life of sacrifice and saying no to everything fun. The other camp says it's simple, just believe and you're good. Both camps are wrong, and both are right. The answer is that it's harder than anything you've tried and easier than what you're doing right now.

That sounds like a fortune cookie. It isn't. By the end of this piece, I think you'll see it's just math.

---

## This isn't a side quest

Look, last time we talked about "putting on Christ," acting the part of a son of God until the role becomes the reality. Fake it, then make it. Not in a hustle-culture way, but in the way a new hire shadows the best person on the team until the habits are their own.

Here's what I need you to understand: **that isn't one feature in the product. That is the product.**

It's not an advanced module for the spiritual power users. It's not a bonus track. It's the entire offering. Christianity has nothing else on the menu. And once you see that, you start to see how radically different it is from what most of us mean when we say "being a good person."

---

## The tax model of being good

Here's the thing. Before anyone takes Christianity seriously, they're almost always running the same default operating system. I call it the tax model.

It works like this. You start with yourself. Your wants, your goals, your plans for the weekend, your portfolio, your ambitions. That's the baseline. Then you acknowledge that something else has a claim on you. Call it morality. Call it decency. Call it "the good of society." Whatever the label, it shows up and starts taking a cut.

Some of the things you wanted to do get flagged as wrong. Fine, you cut them. Some of the things you didn't want to do get flagged as right. Fine, you do them. And the whole time, the quiet hope running in the background is this: once I've paid what I owe, there'll be enough left over to live my actual life.

It's exactly how you think about April 15th. You pay your taxes. You're not a cheat. But you're absolutely hoping there's enough left in the account after the IRS is done with you.

**Your natural self is still the starting point. Morality is just the withholding.**

---

## Why the tax model always blows up

Now here's where it gets uncomfortable.

If you run the tax model honestly, one of two things happens. Either you quit trying to be good, or you become genuinely miserable.

Why? Because the tax rate keeps going up. The more seriously you listen to your conscience, the more it asks of you. That's not a bug, that's how conscience works. Every time you pay the bill, you discover a new line item you didn't know about.

Meanwhile, your natural self, the one you're trying to protect, is getting squeezed from every direction. Starved, boxed in, second-guessed. And it gets angry. Really angry.

So you hit a fork. Option one: you rage-quit. You decide the whole "be good" project was a scam and you go back to doing whatever you want. Option two is worse. You keep paying, but you resent every dollar. You become the person who "lives for others" and makes sure everyone knows it. The martyr in the group chat. The one who keeps a running ledger of every favor and wonders out loud why nobody appreciates them.

And I'll be honest with you: **that person is harder to be around than someone who's just openly selfish.** At least the selfish guy isn't sending you invoices for his sacrifices.

This is the core failure of the tax model. You're trying to run two businesses with one set of resources, and both of them go under.

---

## The term sheet nobody expects

The Christian model is completely different. It's harder, and it's easier.

Christ doesn't come to the table asking for a percentage. He's not asking for ten percent of your income, a couple of hours on Sunday, and a few vices you were going to give up anyway. That's the tax model with better branding.

His term sheet is one line: **I want all of it.**

Not your time. Not your money. Not your work. You. The whole entity.

Think about it like the difference between a minority investment and a full acquisition. A minority investor wants a seat on the board, some reporting, maybe a veto on a few big decisions. You still run the company. A full acquirer doesn't want to tweak your strategy. They want to absorb the whole thing, retire the old cap table, and run it on new infrastructure.

That's the offer. And the striking part is that it isn't only your bad habits on the block. It's the stuff you think is perfectly innocent too. The ambitions you're proud of. The desires that seem harmless. The whole package. He doesn't want to trim the tree. He wants to take it down to the roots. He doesn't want to patch the cavity. He wants to pull the tooth.

And in exchange? You get a new self. Actually, you get *His* self. His will becomes your will. The old operating system doesn't get an update. It gets replaced.

---

## Both hard and easy, at the same time

The fascinating thing here is that Christ describes this path in two ways that seem to contradict each other. In one breath, he says take up your cross, which is basically signing up to be executed. In the next, he says his yoke is easy and his burden is light.

He means both. And once you see the mechanism, it's obvious why.

Let me give you two analogies.

**First, the AI shortcut problem.** Give two students the same hard calculus problem. One sits with it, struggles, and actually understands why it works. The other pastes it into a chatbot, copies the answer, and moves on. In the moment, the second kid wins. Less effort, same grade on the homework. But fast forward to the final exam. The kid who understood the concept is breezing through problems in minutes, maybe even enjoying it. The shortcut kid is pulling all-nighters trying to cram a semester of material that never stuck. **Laziness is the most expensive strategy in the long run.**

**Second, the hard conversation problem.** Every founder knows this one. There's a co-founder issue, or a key hire who isn't working out, or a product that's clearly failing. The scary move is to deal with it today. Have the conversation. Make the call. It takes real courage. But if you dodge it, three months later the problem has metastasized, the team is fractured, and you're in a far more dangerous spot. **The cowardly move is almost always the riskiest move.**

Same thing here.

The terrifying thing, the nearly impossible thing, is handing over your whole self. Every wish, every hedge, every backup plan. But that's actually *easier* than what most of us are attempting instead.

---

## You can't get figs from a thistle

Here's what we're all actually trying to do. We want to stay "ourselves." We want personal happiness to remain the primary objective. And we want to be good on top of that.

We let our hearts run on their default settings, optimized for money, comfort, status, pleasure, and then we expect them to somehow output honesty, humility, and self-control. That's the play almost everyone is running.

And it's exactly the play Christ said would fail. A thistle doesn't grow figs.

Think about it in terms of a codebase. If your core architecture was built to maximize one thing, you can't bolt on a different objective function and expect clean results. You can write patches. You can add filters. You can moderate the outputs. But the system will keep producing what it was designed to produce. If you want a fundamentally different output, you don't need a patch. **You need a rewrite.**

Mowing the lawn keeps the grass short. It doesn't turn the lawn into a wheat field. For that, the ground has to be torn up and replanted.

---

## The real battle happens at 6 a.m.

Here's what nobody tells you about where the fight actually takes place.

It's not in the dramatic moments. It's not the big moral crisis or the once-in-a-decade temptation. It's the moment you wake up.

Before your feet hit the floor, every want and worry and plan for the day comes at you like a flood of push notifications. The emails. The anxieties. The ambitions. The grudges. All of them demanding attention.

**The first job of the day is to swipe all of that away.** To deliberately listen to a different voice. To take a different point of view. To let a bigger, steadier, quieter life come in. And then to keep doing it, over and over, all day long. Stepping back from the constant churn of your own fretting. Getting out of the wind.

You won't be good at it at first. You'll manage it for moments. But those moments matter, because now the work is happening at the right layer of the stack. Not the UI. The kernel.

It's the difference between a coat of paint and a dye. Paint sits on the surface and chips the first time something scrapes it. Dye soaks into the fiber until there's no separating the color from the material.

When Christ said "be perfect," he wasn't giving a motivational keynote. He meant it. He meant the full treatment. Yes, it's hard. But the compromise we all secretly want, the one where we keep our old selves and somehow also become good, isn't just harder. It's impossible.

> **It's hard for an egg to become a bird. It's a lot harder for an egg to learn to fly while staying an egg.**

That's us. We're eggs right now. And here's the uncomfortable truth: you can't stay a decent, ordinary egg forever. Eventually you either hatch or you go bad. There is no third option.

---

## What is any of this actually for?

Let me zoom out, because it's easy to lose the plot here.

Think about a government. It looks like it has a hundred different jobs: defense, courts, taxes, regulation, infrastructure. But strip it down to first principles and the purpose is simple. It exists to protect the ordinary happiness of ordinary people. Two friends watching the game on a Sunday. A couple cooking dinner together. Someone reading a book on their porch. Someone tending a garden. That's the whole point. If all the laws and agencies and armies aren't protecting and multiplying those moments, they're overhead.

The Church works the same way. It looks like it has a dozen missions: buildings, schools, outreach, services, programs. But it really has one job. **To pull people into Christ. To turn them into little Christs.** If it isn't doing that, then the buildings, the clergy, the sermons, even the Bible itself, are just burn rate.

God didn't become a man for any other reason. And there's a serious case that the entire universe exists for the same reason. The Bible says everything was made for Christ and that everything will eventually be gathered together in him.

How that plays out for the whole cosmos, nobody knows. We have no idea what might exist in distant galaxies or how the plan applies to the rest of creation. And honestly, that's what you'd expect. We've been shown the part of the roadmap that concerns us. Need-to-know basis.

Lewis floated a guess, and I like it as a hypothesis. Maybe it works through network effects. When humans love animals, those animals become more than they would have been on their own. Think about the dog who basically has a personality because his family treats him like a person. When humans study and use and appreciate the natural world, it gets pulled into our story. So maybe when humans are brought into Christ, they bring the rest of creation with them. Every node that joins the network increases the value of everything connected to it. But that's speculation, and he said so.

---

## The bottom line

Here's the realization.

Most of us are running the tax model. We protect the self, pay our moral dues, and hope there's something left over. And it fails every single time, either in surrender or in quiet resentment.

The Christian offer isn't a better tax rate. It's a full acquisition. Hand over everything, the bad and the "harmless," and receive a new self in return.

**That's the hardest deal you'll ever sign. It's also the only one that closes.** Everything else is an egg trying to fly.

What we have been told clearly is this: we can be drawn into Christ, made part of the gift the Son offers back to the Father. That's what we were built for. Not one goal among many. The goal.

And there are hints that when that happens, a lot of other broken things start to get fixed too. The bad dream ends. And it's morning.

_That's the signal. Everything else is noise._
