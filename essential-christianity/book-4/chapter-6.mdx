Quick one this week. Well, quick by my standards.

Last week's piece on "toy soldiers" coming to life generated more replies than almost anything I've written this year. Most of them were great. A few of them flagged real gaps in the argument. And when smart readers find a gap, you don't ignore it. You patch it publicly.

So consider this the post-launch patch notes. Two clarifications. Both matter more than they look.

---

## Note one: why not just launch with sons?

Look, one reader sent me a DM that I haven't been able to stop thinking about. Paraphrasing:

_"Pomp, if God's end goal was sons and not toy soldiers, why run this expensive two-step process? Why build creatures first and then go through a painful transformation to turn them into sons? Why not just beget a whole family of sons on day one and skip the drama?"_

That's a sharp question. It's the founder asking, "Why didn't we just build the final product instead of shipping an MVP and iterating?"

Here's the thing: the answer has two layers. One is straightforward. The other is probably above the pay grade of every human who has ever lived, including me.

**The easy layer first.**

The upgrade from creature to son was never supposed to hurt. The pain isn't a feature of the design. It's the cost of a bad decision humanity made a very long time ago, when we walked away from the source.

Why could we walk away? Because we were given free will.

Why were we given free will? Because a world of perfectly obedient automatons can't love anything. And a system that can't love can't access the highest form of happiness available. Free will was the high-stakes bet for long-term ROI. It opened up the possibility of the best outcome, which necessarily opened up the possibility of the worst one too.

So the painful process isn't the plan failing. It's the plan running on a cap table we damaged ourselves.

**Now the hard layer.**

Christians all agree there is, in the full original sense, exactly one Son of God. If you push and ask, "Okay, but could there have been many?" you're wading into very deep water.

Think about what "could have been" actually means. When we use that phrase about ordinary things, we're running a dependency chain. Your pizza could have arrived hot if the driver had taken a different route. The driver would have taken a different route if the app had routed him differently. The app would have routed him differently if traffic data had been updated. Every "could have been" is just a pointer to some other thing upstream that could have been different.

But God isn't a node in the dependency chain. God is the base layer. The root fact that every other fact depends on. There's nothing upstream. So asking whether the rock-bottom reality "could have been otherwise" is a category error. It is what it is. Full stop.

## The "many sons" problem is actually a math problem

The fascinating thing here is that even if you set that aside, the idea of many eternal sons starts breaking down the second you look at it closely.

To have many of anything, those things need to be different from each other somehow. Otherwise, what makes them many?

Think about two identical Bitcoin mining rigs. Same model, same chips, same firmware. Why are they two machines and not one? Because they sit in different racks. They're made of different physical atoms. In other words, the only way to tell them apart is to drag in space and matter. You need a physical universe to make "two of the same thing" coherent.

Now, the Father and the Son are distinguishable without any of that. One begets. The other is begotten. Their relationships point in different directions. That's a real difference that doesn't require a single atom.

But if you imagine several sons, every one of them would relate to the Father the exact same way, and to each other the exact same way. So what separates them? Nothing.

**When you think you're imagining "many sons before the universe existed," you're almost always secretly picturing figures standing around in some kind of space.**

You smuggled the universe in through the back door. Strip it out, and the idea dissolves into words with nothing behind them.

Here's a speculative thought, and I want to be clear it's speculation: maybe that's the whole reason for the natural world. Maybe space, time, and matter exist precisely to make "many" possible. Maybe the only way to get a multitude of eternal spirits is to first build a multitude of natural creatures inside a universe and then upgrade them.

Is that the answer? I don't know. Nobody does. But it reframes the "inefficient" two-step process in a very different light. The MVP wasn't a detour. It might have been the only path to scale.

## Note two: one organism does not mean one size fits all

The second clarification is about the network.

Earlier in this series I made the case that humanity is, in a real sense, one thing. One giant organism. One tree with billions of branches.

Some readers heard that and thought I was saying individuals don't matter. That you, your kid, your coworker are just interchangeable units inside some bigger collective, whether it's a class, a race, a nation, or a demographic bucket.

That is the opposite of what I'm saying. And I want to be very precise about why.

Look, parts of a single organism are usually radically different from each other. Things that aren't connected are often nearly identical.

Six identical pennies are completely separate and completely alike. Your eyes and your liver couldn't look more different, yet neither one is alive at all except as part of your body, running on the same shared life.

Christianity doesn't see people as line items in a spreadsheet or members of a cohort. It sees them as **organs in a body.** Each one different. Each one contributing something no other part can.

This is network effects done right. The best networks don't make every node identical. They make every node more valuable because it's different and connected at the same time. A marketplace where every seller sells the same thing is worthless. Diversity of function is the whole point.

So here are two practical gut-checks:

**When you catch yourself trying to make your kids, your team, or your neighbors into copies of yourself, stop.** They were probably never meant to be you. You're different organs designed to do different jobs.

**When you catch yourself saying someone else's problem is "not my business," stop again.** They're different from you, sure. But they're part of the same body. Their failure is your failure, whether your balance sheet shows it this quarter or not.

Forget the shared body and you become an **Individualist.** Forget the differences and you become a **Totalitarian.**

> **A Christian doesn't get to be either one.**

## The devil ships bugs in pairs

Here's the part I find most useful from an operator's perspective.

Right now, I have a strong urge to tell you which of those two errors is worse. And I'd bet you have a strong urge to tell me. Maybe you think the Individualist is the bigger threat. Maybe you think it's the Totalitarian. Everybody has a take.

That urge is the trap.

Errors almost never show up alone. They get released into the world in pairs of opposites. And the move is always the same: get you so obsessed with debating which one is worse that your hatred of one pushes you straight into the other.

You see this in markets constantly. Someone gets burned by excessive leverage, so they swear off all risk and sit in cash while inflation eats them alive. Someone gets burned by being too conservative, so they go max degen on the next cycle. They spent so much energy avoiding the last mistake that they sprinted face-first into its mirror image.

Same thing in politics. Same thing in management. The founder who hated their micromanaging old boss becomes so hands-off that the company drifts into chaos. Overcorrection isn't a strategy. It's just the other bug.

**Don't get baited into ranking the errors. Keep your eyes on the target and walk the line between them.**

You don't need an opinion on which ditch is deeper. You just need to stay on the road.

---

## The bottom line

Two notes. One takeaway.

On the first: the painful process of turning creatures into sons isn't a design flaw. The pain comes from our own exit. And the two-step build might be the only way to get many sons at all. When you hit questions about the base layer of reality, be humble about what "could have been" even means.

On the second: you're not a lone wolf, and you're not a cog. You're an organ. Different from everyone else, connected to everyone else, and responsible to everyone else.

**The individual matters precisely because the body is real. The body is healthy precisely because the individuals are different.**

Hold both. Rank neither. Walk the line.

_That's the signal. Everything else is noise._
