Last week's piece got more replies than anything I've written this year.

Most of them said some version of the same thing: _"Pomp, if the standard is 'be perfect,' then I'm out. I can't hit that. Nobody can. So what's the point of even trying?"_

I get it. If you read "be perfect" as an entry requirement, a velvet rope where you have to show perfection at the door before anyone helps you, then yes, the whole thing looks hopeless. Nobody gets in.

But that's not the deal. I think the deal is almost the exact opposite, and once you see it, the whole framework flips.

The offer isn't _"be perfect or I won't help you."_ The offer is _"the only help I give is the kind that makes you perfect. You might want something smaller. You won't get something smaller."_

That's a very different term sheet. Let's walk through it.

---

## The urgent care problem

Look, everyone has done this.

You wake up with a nagging pain. Your back, a tooth, whatever. You know exactly what will happen if you book a real doctor. They'll give you something for the pain, sure. But they'll also order the bloodwork. They'll ask about your sleep. They'll find the other three things you've been ignoring for two years. Next thing you know, you're on a twelve-week protocol you never signed up for.

So what do you do? You don't book the doctor. You go to urgent care, grab the quick fix, and get out. Or you wait until the pain is so bad you have no choice.

Here's the thing: **you weren't avoiding the doctor because you doubted they'd help. You were avoiding them because you knew they'd help too much.**

You wanted relief tonight. They wanted you actually healthy. Those are not the same product. You can't get the first one from a serious physician without also getting the second.

God is the serious physician. Not urgent care.

Plenty of people show up with one specific problem. Maybe it's a temper that keeps blowing up their marriage. Maybe it's the drinking. Maybe it's some private habit they're ashamed of, or a cowardice that keeps them from ever making the hard call at work. They come in wanting that one thing fixed.

And He'll fix it. But He won't stop there. You opened the door for one repair. He's coming in to do the whole house.

---

## Read the term sheet before you sign

This is why the original pitch came with a warning label: **count the cost.**

Think about any serious founder taking money from a serious investor. The good ones read the terms. They know exactly what they're giving up and what they're committing to. The ones who skip that step are the ones who end up shocked eighteen months later when the board starts asking hard questions.

The terms here are stated plainly. Paraphrasing the whole thing into 2026 English, it goes something like this:

> **"If you let me in, I'm going to finish the job. Not part of it. All of it. You can walk away at any point, because the choice is yours. But if you don't walk away, I won't stop until you're the finished product I designed you to be. Whatever it costs you, whatever it costs me."**

That's not a soft pitch. That's a founder telling you exactly what kind of company they're building, and saying if you're looking for a lifestyle business, look elsewhere.

There's one exit clause, and only one. **You.** Nothing else in the universe can stop the process. Only your own decision to push Him out.

---

## Easy to please, hard to satisfy

Now here's the part people miss, and it matters just as much as the first part.

The same person who won't settle for anything less than total transformation is also thrilled with your first clumsy attempt to do the right thing tomorrow morning.

There's an old line from George MacDonald that Lewis loved, and it's one of the best pieces of compressed wisdom I've come across: **God is easy to please, but hard to satisfy.**

Think about a parent watching a toddler take their first steps. The kid wobbles, takes two steps, falls on their face. The parent is ecstatic. Filming it. Sending it to the family group chat. Pure joy.

But that same parent would be deeply worried if their twenty-five-year-old was still walking like that. The joy at step one doesn't mean step one is the goal. It means step one is exactly what step one should look like.

Here's the fascinating thing about great early-stage investors. They get genuinely excited about your first ten customers. They'll celebrate that win with you. But they did not write the check for ten customers. They wrote it for the company you could become. Both things are true at the same time, and there's no contradiction.

So the practical upshot cuts two ways:

- **Your failures right now shouldn't discourage you.** Every time you fall, you get picked back up. Nobody expected you to get there on your own effort. That was never the model.
- **But you need to know where this is going.** The destination is total. If you don't understand that from day one, you're going to start resisting at some point down the road.

---

## The "good enough" trap

Let me be blunt about where most people go wrong.

You let Him in. A couple of obvious problems get fixed. The temper calms down. The drinking stops. You're noticeably better than you were.

And then a quiet thought creeps in, one you'd never say out loud: _"Okay, that's enough. Thanks. I'm good now. You can leave."_

We dress this up as humility. _"I never wanted to be a saint. I just wanted to be a normal, decent person."_ It sounds modest. It sounds grounded.

**It's not humility. It's the fatal mistake.**

Here's why. The question was never what _you_ planned to become. The question is what you were _designed_ to become. You're not the founder of you. You're the product. He's the builder. And the product doesn't get to set the roadmap.

Think about how much you've already changed without being consulted. Before you were born, you went through stages that looked nothing like a human. Early on, you were a cluster of cells. Then something closer to a fish. Only later did you start looking like a baby. If you'd been conscious back then, you'd probably have been perfectly content to stay a cluster of cells. Why upgrade? Things were fine.

But the plan was always bigger than your preferences at any given stage. And the same thing is happening now, just at a higher level.

You may be totally content to stay "a normal person." He's building something else.

**Shrinking back from that plan isn't humility. It's laziness dressed up as modesty, and a little bit of cowardice.** And going along with it isn't arrogance or delusions of grandeur. It's just following the spec.

---

## Two truths that have to run together

Here's another way to frame the whole thing, because both halves have to be held at the same time.

**Truth one: you can't make it through the next twenty-four hours as a decent person on your own.** Seriously. Without support, any of us is one bad day away from something terrible. Your own willpower is not a reliable risk management system. It never was.

**Truth two: the ceiling is unlimited.** Every act of courage, holiness, and selflessness ever recorded in the greatest people in history? That's not the upper bound for a special few. That's the target He has for every single one of us. It won't be finished in this life. But He intends to get you as far down the road as possible before you die.

Most people hold one of these and drop the other. Hold only the first and you get despair. Hold only the second and you get pride. You need both running in parallel.

---

## Why it gets harder after you get better

Here's what blindsides a lot of people.

You turn your life around. Some bad habits are gone. You're making progress. And you naturally expect the road to smooth out from here. You did the work. Shouldn't the market reward you?

Then the hits come. A health scare. Money gets tight. Brand-new temptations you didn't even know you had.

And you think: _"Okay, I understand needing a wake-up call back when I was a mess. But why now? I'm doing everything right."_

Look, think about how a great company scales. Every new stage brings harder problems, not easier ones. The challenges at Series C make the Seed-stage problems look like a warm-up. Nobody thinks that's unfair. That's just what growth looks like. You don't get Series C problems unless you've earned a Series C.

**The difficulty isn't a sign you're going backward. It's a sign you've been promoted.**

You're being pushed into situations that require more courage, more patience, more love than you ever thought you'd need. It feels unnecessary because you have no idea yet how big the thing He's building actually is.

---

## The renovation that got out of hand

I'm going to borrow one more image from MacDonald, because I don't think anyone has ever topped it. I'll just put it in modern terms.

Imagine you're a house, and you've hired a contractor.

At first, the work makes total sense. He fixes the plumbing. Patches the roof. Rewires the electrical that's been sketchy for years. You knew all that needed doing. No surprises.

Then he starts tearing out walls. Ripping up the foundation in places that seemed fine. It's loud, it's painful, and it seems completely unnecessary. You're thinking: _"What are you doing? I didn't ask for any of this."_

Here's the thing: **he's not renovating the house you had in mind.** You were picturing a nicely updated starter home. He's building a mansion. New wings, extra floors, courtyards, towers. And the reason he's going that big?

He plans to live there himself.

---

## The bottom line

"Be perfect" isn't a motivational poster. It's not hype, and it's not an impossible demand designed to make you feel bad.

It's a product roadmap.

He isn't asking you to make yourself perfect. He's telling you He's going to build you into the kind of being who actually can be. Old scripture says that people are meant to be "gods," and He intends to deliver on that line. Take the weakest, most broken person you know, maybe the one in your mirror. The plan is to turn that person into something radiant, immortal, overflowing with energy and joy and wisdom and love at a level you can't currently imagine. A clean mirror reflecting back the goodness of the one who made it.

The process is long. Parts of it will hurt. You can walk away at any time.

But if you stay, understand what you signed up for.

**You came in asking for a patch. He's shipping the full rebuild. No partial fills. Nothing less.**

He meant exactly what He said.
