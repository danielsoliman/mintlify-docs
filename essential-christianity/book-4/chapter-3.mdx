## The scaling objection

Look, last time we talked about prayer. And while that's still fresh, I want to tackle one of the most common objections I hear about the entire concept.

A founder I know put it to me bluntly over dinner: _"Pomp, I can get to the idea of God. Fine. What I can't get to is the idea of Him actually listening to hundreds of millions of people who are all talking to Him at the exact same moment. That doesn't scale."_

And honestly? A lot of smart people feel this way. It's an infrastructure objection. They're picturing God as a server, and they're doing the math on concurrent requests, and the numbers look insane.

Here's the thing. Notice where the pain actually lives in that objection. It's in four words: **at the same moment.**

Nobody struggles with the idea of God giving attention to a billion people one at a time, if He had unlimited time to work through the queue. That part is easy to imagine. The problem is the bottleneck. It's the idea of cramming too many requests into one tiny slice of time.

So the real question isn't about God's capacity. It's about time.

---

## Time is how we receive reality, not how reality is built

Here's what time looks like from where you and I sit.

Life shows up one frame at a time. Each moment loads, renders, and gets garbage-collected before the next one arrives. And there's very little bandwidth in any single frame. You can't take a call, answer a DM, and read a term sheet in the same instant without dropping something. That's just the architecture we run on.

And because that's our entire experience, we make a massive assumption without even noticing it. We assume that this sequence of past, present, and future isn't just the way _we_ experience things. We assume it's the way _everything_ exists. The universe, physics, God Himself, all of it supposedly marching forward on the same clock we are.

That assumption is weaker than it looks. The idea that some realities sit entirely off the clock isn't a fringe take. Church thinkers were arguing it centuries ago, academic philosophy adopted it, and modern physics has been poking holes in our "universal clock" intuition ever since.

**My conviction: God doesn't run on our clock.**

There's no sequence of frames He's waiting through. So picture a hundred million requests landing tonight at 10:30. There's no 10:30 inbox on His end that has to absorb them all at once. Every timestamp in history, from the Big Bang to this evening's prayer rush, sits equally open in front of Him.

Here's the version that gets me. A pilot in a failing aircraft gets maybe half a second to say something to God. On our side, that's a blip. On His side, there's no countdown. That half-second gets an unlimited audience.

Let that one sit for a second.

---

## The showrunner analogy

The fascinating thing here is that we actually have a decent model for this in our own lives. Not a perfect one. But close enough to get a feel for it.

Think about a writer working on a streaming series. In the script, the main character sets her phone down, and in the very next beat, someone knocks on the door. For her, there's zero gap. Phone down, knock. One continuous moment inside the world of the show.

But the writer isn't living inside the show's timeline. Between those two lines of script, the writer can grab lunch, hit the gym, and burn an entire afternoon obsessing over that one character. Her backstory, her fears, what she wants. Every other cast member can sit untouched while she gets the writer's complete focus.

And that afternoon never makes the final cut. Inside her world, it adds zero seconds of runtime.

That's the shape of it. The episode's runtime doesn't dictate the writer's schedule, and our universe's runtime doesn't dictate God's. **His attention isn't a scarce resource that gets split eight billion ways.** No batch processing. No being lumped into a cohort and handled in aggregate.

Your conversation with Him is a private channel. Not a seat in a packed webinar. A one-on-one, with no one else on the call.

And here's the part that hits hardest: the cross wasn't a bulk transaction either. It wasn't priced per-capita across humanity. It was personal, addressed to you by name, with the same weight it would carry if the entire population of Earth were exactly one.

---

## Where the analogy breaks

Now, I promised you signal over noise, so let me be honest about where my analogy fails.

The writer only escapes the show's timeline by stepping into a different timeline, the real one. They're still burning real hours on a real clock. They just swapped one stream for another.

God doesn't do that. There's no bigger, slower stream He retreats into. **Sequence just isn't His operating environment.** We get our lives streamed to us in tiny packets. He gets nothing streamed, because nothing is pending. The dot-com crash and the 2060 IPO class are equally accessible to Him. Not because He's flipping between tabs, but because what He is doesn't come in installments.

Here's the cleanest visual I've found:

> **Picture time as a line on a chart. You're a data point crawling along that line. God isn't a faster data point. He's the entire chart.**

Our experience is a strict queue. Q2 doesn't unlock until Q1 closes out, and Q3 stays locked until Q2 is done. The chart itself has no such problem. Every quarter is printed on it at once, fully visible, the whole time.

## The bug this fixes: the incarnation

Okay, so why should you care about this beyond a cool thought experiment?

Because it resolves some genuinely difficult problems in Christianity. Problems I personally got stuck on.

Here's one that used to bother me. The Christian claim is that the God who sustains every atom in existence took on a human body. My pushback was basically an ops question: who's on call for the universe during the diaper years? During naptime? How do you hold omniscience and _also_ be the guy in a crowd asking who just bumped into you?

Look closely at those objections. Every single one is powered by a time word. _While_ He was a baby. _At the same time._

What I was doing, without realizing it, was modeling God's life as one long timeline, and then treating Jesus's life on Earth as a short segment carved out of that timeline. Like a founder who takes a two-year leave to do a stint in government, then returns to run the company. A chapter cut out of a longer career.

That's the default mental model for most people. A product roadmap. The incarnation sits in the "upcoming" column, then ships, then becomes a case study God references afterward.

I think that whole roadmap is a fiction. There's no shared axis where you can line up thirty-odd years in Roman-occupied Judea against the existence of a God who isn't bounded by space or time. A better frame: baked permanently into who God is, with no start date and no end date, is the fact that He includes a fully human experience. Fatigue, sleep, limited information, all of it.

On our side, sure, that shows up as a specific window, a few decades ending at a Roman execution. And we naturally assume what's a window for us must be a window for Him.

**It isn't, because God has no backstory.** Backstory is something only partial beings have.

Run the numbers on what a timeline does to you. Most of your life is either already spent, locked in the archive and unrecoverable, or still unissued and inaccessible. Your actual working balance is the present instant, and it expires the moment you notice it. Imagine running a company where you could only ever see one day of cash, with every prior day wiped and every future day hidden. That's the human condition.

God is fully capitalized at all times. And one of the quieter hopes in all of this is that we won't be stuck on that daily allowance forever.

## The bug this fixes: free will

Here's the second problem this clears up, and it's a big one.

If God is all-knowing, then your next move is already on His radar. And if the answer is already on the board, what exactly is "choice"? It starts to feel like the outcome is priced in, and you're just a bot executing a pre-written strategy.

Same root cause as before. We're quietly modeling God as a fellow traveler on the timeline who just happens to have a much better forecasting desk. And to be fair, if God were basically the ultimate quant, predicting your trades before you place them, the free will problem would be real and ugly.

So flip the model. Take God off the timeline entirely.

From outside the line, your Thursday isn't a projection. It's just as present to Him as your Tuesday. There's no "earlier" He has to recall and no "later" He has to estimate. Last week isn't a memory to Him, since it never left. Next week isn't a forecast, since it's already in view.

Here's the gut check. Does it feel like your choice to read this sentence is less yours because God sees you reading it? Of course not. Nobody feels coerced by being seen. Visibility and control are two different things.

Apply that same logic to next week. He's not modeling your behavior or front-running your trade. He's simply present at the moment you decide, the same way He's present right now.

**Watching isn't forecasting. And it definitely isn't puppeteering.**

Technically, His knowledge of your decision is downstream of you making it. It's just that "after you decide" is already within His view. The bet is yours. The risk is yours. The upside is yours. He's just not stuck waiting for the closing bell.

## The bottom line

Here's the realization I want you to walk away with:

Almost every objection about God being "too busy," or the incarnation being "logically impossible," or free will being "already decided," is secretly an assumption about time. We take the constraint we live under, one moment at a time with almost no bandwidth, and we project it onto God without asking whether it applies.

It doesn't.

You're not a ticket in an overloaded support queue. You're not a rounding error in an aggregate. And you're not an NPC running a pre-written script.

**God isn't a faster player on the timeline. He's the whole chart. And from there, He has all the time in eternity to give you His full, undivided attention.**

That's the signal. Everything else is latency.
