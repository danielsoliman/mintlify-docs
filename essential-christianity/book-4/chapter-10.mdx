I get one question in my DMs more than almost any other when I write about this stuff.

It goes something like this: _"Pomp, if Christianity is actually true, why aren't Christians obviously better people than everyone else? I know plenty of churchgoers who are jerks and plenty of atheists who are genuinely great humans. Explain that."_

It's a fair question. But it's built on two very different assumptions. One of them is completely legitimate. The other one is a broken model that falls apart the second you stress-test it.

Let's separate the signal from the noise.

---

## The part of the critique that's right

Look, I'll start by conceding the strongest version of the argument.

**If someone says they've become a Christian and their actual behavior doesn't change, you should be suspicious.**

If they're still just as petty, just as jealous, just as status-obsessed, just as quick to trash people behind their backs as they were before, then whatever "conversion" happened was probably mostly in their head. And this isn't just a test for the day someone signs up. It's the test you should run on yourself every single time you think you've made progress.

New feelings, big insights, a sudden obsession with theology podcasts: none of it counts unless it shows up in how you actually treat people.

It's like a fitness tracker. You can _feel_ like you're getting in shape, but if your bloodwork keeps getting worse, the feeling is worthless. The data wins.

So the outside world is completely right to judge Christianity by its outputs. Jesus literally told people to judge his followers by what they produce, like evaluating an orchard by its harvest. That's the founder's own KPI.

And here's the uncomfortable part for believers: **every time a Christian behaves badly, they are actively damaging the brand.** Have the best product in the world, staff it with rude and dishonest people, and the market will conclude the product is garbage. Sloppy lives create bad press. And bad press makes people doubt the whole thing is true.

That part of the critique is legit. Own it.

---

## The part of the critique that's broken

Here's the thing though. There's a second, more aggressive version of the demand, and it doesn't hold up.

The second version says: before I'll take Christianity seriously, show me a world split cleanly into two teams, Christians and non-Christians, where every single person on Team Christian is visibly nicer than every single person on the other team.

That sounds rigorous. It isn't, for three reasons.

---

## Reason one: the data isn't clean

Look, the world doesn't sort neatly into 100% Christians and 100% non-Christians. That's not how humans work.

Some people are drifting out of the faith but still wear the label, a few of them from pulpits. Others are drifting _in_ and wouldn't call themselves Christian yet. You've got skeptics who push back on half the official doctrine, yet can't stop orbiting Jesus, and are probably more his than they'd ever admit. You've also got folks from entirely different faith traditions who seem to be getting quietly steered toward whatever in their own system rhymes with Christ's teaching. Picture a Buddhist friend whose whole life keeps compounding around compassion while the rest of the doctrine becomes background noise. Where exactly does that person land on your spreadsheet? And then there's the massive cohort of people who are just confused, running a dozen contradictory beliefs at the same time without noticing.

You can run group-level stats on, say, golden retrievers versus huskies because the labels are fixed. A retriever never gradually becomes a husky. But humans are constantly migrating between categories, sometimes gradually, sometimes overnight.

Even worse: **most people making this comparison aren't thinking about real people at all.** "The hypocritical Christian" and "the enlightened atheist" are cartoons from movies and viral clips, not datasets.

If you want to have this conversation seriously, name two actual humans you personally know. Otherwise you're just backtesting a strategy on fake data.

---

## Reason two: you're asking the wrong question

Okay, so let's say you do it right. You pick two real people from your own life.

Let's call them Megan and Jake.

Megan is a Christian with a sharp tongue, anxious and quick to take offense. Jake doesn't believe in anything, and he's the guy everyone loves: easygoing, generous, never a bad word about anyone.

The lazy conclusion: "See? Christianity doesn't work."

But that's not the claim Christianity makes. If it's true, here's what should actually follow:

1. **Megan as a Christian is better than Megan would be without it.**
2. **If Jake became a Christian, Jake would be better than Jake is now.**

That's the claim. Not "Megan beats Jake."

Say a supplement genuinely works. Then anyone taking it should be healthier than they'd otherwise be. But if I take it and inherited terrible genetics, I might still be in worse shape than a 22-year-old who's never taken a supplement in his life. That doesn't prove the supplement is fake. It proves I started from a different baseline.

**You don't compare outputs without controlling for inputs.**

Megan and Jake each came into the world with a temperament shaped by genetics, childhood, trauma, luck, and a hundred things neither of them chose. Christianity's actual pitch is basically an acquisition offer: let God take over operations on whatever temperament you've got.

So the fair metric is simple: when that takeover actually happens, does the business get better?

Think about two factories. Factory A is running on decades-old equipment, half the machines held together with duct tape. Factory B just got a fully automated, state-of-the-art line. Factory B's output is higher. Obviously. But given the equipment, it's honestly a miracle Factory A produces anything. And given what Factory B has to work with, its output might actually be way below what it should be.

Any serious investor judging the CEO looks at the equipment he inherited, not just the quarterly numbers. The good CEO at Factory A will upgrade the machinery, but that takes time, and low output during the transition doesn't make him a bad operator.

---

## Reason three: niceness was never the goal

Here's the part most people completely miss.

Everything I've said so far could leave you with the wrong impression: that the mission is just getting Megan to Jake's level. That Jake is already a finished product. That faith is a remediation program for difficult personalities, and the well-adjusted can opt out.

**That would be a catastrophic misread of the entire thing.**

From where God sits, Jake and Megan are in the exact same boat. Both need rescuing. And weirdly, how pleasant either of them is turns out to be a footnote.

Look at where Jake's niceness actually comes from: good genes, a stable childhood, solid brain chemistry. And God made all of those causes. Which means **Jake didn't earn that niceness. He was handed it. It's an allocation from God, not a contribution to God.**

It's also fragile. Let Jake's health tank or a brutal depression hit, and a lot of that easy charm could evaporate.

Same thing on Megan's side. Natural causes, running in a world that's been broken for a long time, produced her anxious wiring and narrow outlook. God fully intends to fix that eventually. But that's not the hard part for him. That's an engineering problem, and he has unlimited engineering resources.

**The hard part is something he can't just manufacture.**

---

## The one thing God can't force

Here's the thing that actually matters. It's the thing God is watching for in both Megan and Jake.

Will they turn toward him?

That's the whole game. It's the one thing that can't be produced by sheer power, because by its very nature it has to be freely given. It's the only purpose they were built for, and it's the only thing either of them can freely hand over or freely refuse.

Picture free will like a compass needle trembling inside each of them. Except this is a needle that gets to _choose._ It can swing around and lock onto true North. Or it can refuse to.

God can nudge it. He can create conditions that help it. But he can't reach in and physically force it into position, because the moment he does, it's not free will anymore. It's just another machine.

This is the highest-stakes bet in the system: building free agents means accepting they can say no, because it's the only setup where the yes is worth anything.

So the question is: will Megan and Jake offer up their natures, whatever condition they're in, to God? Whether those natures happen to be pleasant or difficult at that moment is secondary. God can handle that part.

Don't misread me. God sees a nasty temperament as bad and a nice one as genuinely good, the way sunlight or clean water is good. But those are things he gives and we receive, and creating them costs him basically nothing.

**Winning over a will that's in revolt? That price tag was a cross.**

That tells you exactly where the value is. You price things by what they cost to produce.

---

## Nice is a depreciating asset

Here's the piece that should make every naturally pleasant person reading this a little uncomfortable.

If Jake never turns, his niceness doesn't last. It can't. It's part of nature, and nature is temporary. Jake's personality is like a great sunset: a temporary alignment of variables that happens to look amazing. Give it a little time and the variables drift apart. Gone.

Jake had a shot at converting that temporary pattern into something permanent. He passed.

And here's the paradox, which I think is one of the sharpest ideas in this whole book:

**Jake only truly owns his niceness once he stops claiming it. Hoard it as his own, and it's a lease that expires. Hand it back as the gift it was, and the title transfers to him for real.**

Why? Because now he's participating in his own creation instead of just coasting on the starting kit. The heir who thinks the inheritance proves his talent usually blows it. The one who treats it as a trust becomes a real owner.

> Grip something as yours and you lose it. Surrender it and it's yours forever. Weird economics, but those are the terms.

---

## Why the "wrong" people keep showing up

So we shouldn't be shocked when we find Christians who are still difficult people. If you think about it, there's actually a reason to _expect_ difficult people to turn to Christ in larger numbers than pleasant ones.

That was the exact complaint about Jesus while he was alive: he kept attracting the wrong crowd. People still say it.

Jesus kept flagging wealth as a risk factor. Blessings went to the poor; the rich got a warning label about how tough their path in would be. Mostly he meant literal money. But run the same logic on a different balance sheet and it still holds.

One of money's biggest dangers is that it makes you so comfortable that you never notice you need anything deeper. When every problem gets solved with a wire transfer, you forget you're completely dependent on God at every moment.

**Natural gifts work exactly the same way.**

If you've got a stable nervous system, a sharp mind, good health, and a great upbringing, you're going to be pretty satisfied with your character as-is. "Why bring God into it?" Decent behavior comes easily. You're not getting wrecked by addiction or rage. Everybody says you're a great person, and honestly, you agree.

You likely book all that goodness as your own earnings. And that's the trap. Plenty of people with a stacked starting position never feel any pull toward Christ. Their natural decency carries them right up to the moment it doesn't. A health scare, a betrayal, a breakdown, and suddenly the self-image they'd been running on stops clearing. Often that crash is the first time they're even open to the conversation.

That's what being "rich" looks like in this framework. Comfort makes the door look unnecessary.

Now flip it. Consider the people with a rough draw. The anxious, the isolated, the bitter, the unstable, the people fighting brutal compulsions every single day. If they make any serious attempt at being good, they figure out in record time that they can't do it alone. There's no comfortable middle option on their menu. It's go all in with Jesus, or sink.

Those are the "poor." Those are the people Jesus specifically came looking for. And yes, the respectable crowd still sneers: "If there were anything to this, people like _that_ wouldn't be in it."

---

## A warning and a lifeline

There's something here for everyone.

**If you're a naturally good person, be careful.** A bigger allocation comes with bigger accountability. If you confuse your natural advantages with personal merit, and you're satisfied with just being nice, you're still in rebellion. And all those gifts will only make the fall harder, the corruption more sophisticated, the bad example more damaging. Don't forget that the devil began as one of the most gifted beings ever created. Talent was never his problem. Measured against him, your natural advantages are a rounding error.

But if you're struggling, if you grew up in a house full of petty jealousy and pointless fights, if you're stuck with compulsions you never chose, if insecurity makes you snap at the people you love most, **don't give up.** None of this surprises God. He's seen the spec sheet on the beat-up vehicle you were issued.

Keep going. Do what you can.

One day, maybe in the next life, maybe much sooner, he's going to scrap that machine and hand you a new one. And when that day comes, don't be surprised if you outperform everyone, including your own expectations. Anyone who learned to handle a car with no brakes and a busted steering column is going to be terrifying behind the wheel of something that actually works. Some of the last will be first.

---

## Better people versus new people

Niceness, a healthy and well-integrated personality, is a genuinely great thing. We should use every tool we have, from medicine to education to policy, to help as many people as possible grow up healthy and stable, the same way we should fight hunger.

But don't make the mistake of thinking that if we pulled it off and made everyone nice, we'd have saved anyone.

Imagine a planet of perfectly well-adjusted people, all happy with themselves, with zero interest in anything beyond that, and their backs turned on God. That planet is in just as much trouble as a broken one. Arguably more, because nobody there thinks anything is wrong.

**Getting better and getting rescued are two different products.**

The rescue does make you better, now in small ways and someday in ways you can't even model yet. But the goal was never to ship a slightly better version of the old model. The goal is to build a completely new kind of human.

Think of it this way. The project isn't coaching a horse to post better jump times. It's a horse growing wings. Fully finished, that animal flies over obstacles no horse could touch, and it wins the old game without even trying. Mid-transformation, though, it's clumsy. The bumps forming on its back look like a malfunction, and nobody watching would bet they're the start of flight.

It's an early-stage startup judged on a mature incumbent's metrics. Of course it looks worse right now. It's building a new category, not a better version of the old thing.

---

## The bottom line

Look, if you're hunting for a reason to dismiss Christianity (people often do right when they start to worry it might be true), you'll find one easily. Just point at some obnoxious, petty Christian and say, "That's your new kind of human? I'll stick with the old model."

But if you've started to suspect, for other reasons, that Christianity might actually be true, you'll know deep down that this is a dodge.

What do you really know about anyone else's inner life? Their temptations, their baseline, their struggles, the hand they were dealt? Almost nothing.

There's exactly one person in the entire universe whose inner life you actually have full visibility into. And it's the only one whose outcome is in your hands.

**If God is real, you're in this one-on-one.** You can't deflect with takes about your neighbors or something you read online. All that commentary evaporates when what we call "reality" turns out to have been a haze, and you're face to face with the God who was in the room the entire time.

Stop benchmarking other people. Run the audit on yourself.
