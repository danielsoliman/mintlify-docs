Look, we need to talk about something that's going to sound completely insane at first. But stick with me because the logic here is airtight.

Here's where we are: This person we're discussing either **was exactly what He claimed to be**, or He was completely delusional, or something even darker. And here's the thing—when you actually look at the data, the "lunatic" hypothesis doesn't hold up. Neither does the "con artist" theory. Which leaves us with one option that, however wild it sounds, is the only one that maps to reality: **He was and is God**.

Think of it like this: God deployed into enemy-occupied territory. In human form. That's the play.

**So What Was the Actual Mission?**

Obviously, teaching was part of it. But here's what's fascinating: when you dig into the primary sources—the New Testament, early Christian writings—they're not focused on the teaching. They're obsessed with **His death and resurrection**. That's where they think the entire value proposition lives. The core thesis is that He came here specifically to suffer and die.

Now, before I understood any of this, I thought Christianity was just one specific theory about _why_ the death mattered. The version I'd heard went like this: humanity betrayed God and joined the rebellion, so God needed to punish someone, and Christ volunteered to take the hit instead. Honestly, that theory used to sound both immoral and idiotic to me. It's gotten slightly less ridiculous over time, but that's not my point.

**Here's what I eventually figured out: that theory isn't Christianity. No theory is Christianity.**

The actual Christian claim is simple: **Christ's death somehow reconciled us with God and gave us a clean slate.** How it works mechanically? That's a separate question. Different frameworks have been proposed over the centuries. What every Christian agrees on is that **it works**. The mechanism is debatable. The outcome is not.

**Think of It Like Nutrition**

Everyone knows that when you're exhausted and starving, food fixes you. You don't need to understand macros, micronutrients, or metabolic pathways. People were eating meals and feeling better thousands of years before we discovered vitamins. And if tomorrow we completely abandon vitamin theory, people will keep eating and keep feeling better.

**Theories about Christ's death are not Christianity. They're just frameworks for understanding how it works.**

Different denominations weight these theories differently. The Church of England doesn't mandate any single one. The Catholic Church goes a bit further. But I think they'd all agree: **the thing itself is infinitely more important than any explanation**. They'd probably also admit that no human explanation will ever fully capture the reality.

I'm not a theologian. I'm just telling you how I personally think about this.

**The Formula vs. The Mental Model**

You know how physicists like Jeans or Eddington explain atoms? They give you a mental picture—analogies, diagrams, thought experiments. But then they explicitly tell you: **this picture is not what we actually believe**. What they believe is a mathematical formula. The pictures are just scaffolding to help you grasp the formula. They're not literally true the way the formula is. They're approximations. If they help, use them. If they don't, drop them.

**We're in the same situation here.**

Christians believe that Christ's death is the point in history where something **completely outside our reality broke through into it**. If we can't even properly visualize the atoms that make up our world, we're definitely not going to be able to visualize this. In fact, if we _could_ fully understand it, that would prove it's not what it claims to be—something inconceivable, uncreated, from beyond the system, striking down into the system like lightning.

You might ask: "What good is it if we can't understand it?"

Easy answer: **You can eat dinner without understanding exactly how digestion works.** You can accept what Christ did without knowing the mechanism. In fact, you won't know how it works until you've already accepted it.

**Here's the Formula**

We're told that Christ was killed for us. That His death cleared our ledger. That by dying, He broke death itself.

**That's the formula. That's Christianity. That's what you're asked to believe.**

Any theories about _how_ His death accomplished this are secondary. They're mental models—diagrams to help you think about it. Use them if they help. Don't confuse them with the thing itself.

That said, some of these models are worth examining.

**The "Substitution" Model (And Why It Sounds Broken)**

The most common one is the theory I mentioned earlier: we're let off the hook because Christ volunteered to take the punishment instead.

On the surface, this sounds absurd. If God was willing to let us off, **why didn't He just do it?** What's the point of punishing an innocent person instead? If you're thinking about punishment in a legal/criminal sense, there is no point.

But if you think about it as a **debt**, it makes perfect sense for someone with assets to pay on behalf of someone who's broke. Or if you interpret "paying the penalty" not as "being punished" but as "covering the cost" or "footing the bill," then yeah—**this is standard operating procedure**. When someone gets themselves into a hole, the cost of getting them out usually falls on a friend who has the resources.

**What Was the "Hole"?**

Humanity tried to operate independently. Tried to act like we owned ourselves. **Fallen humans aren't just imperfect products that need iteration—we're rebels who need to surrender.**

Surrender means laying down your arms. Admitting you were wrong. Realizing you've been on the wrong path. Starting over from scratch. **That's the only way out.** Christians call this process repentance.

And repentance is **brutal**. It's not just eating humble pie. It means unlearning all the self-importance and ego we've been optimizing for over millennia. It means killing part of yourself. Undergoing a kind of death.

Here's the catch: **It takes a good person to repent well. But only a bad person needs to repent.** The worse you are, the more you need it—and the less capable you are of doing it. The only person who could repent perfectly would be a perfect person, who wouldn't need to repent at all.

This repentance—this willing submission to humiliation and death—**isn't something God arbitrarily demands before He'll take you back**. It's simply a description of what going back to Him actually looks like. If you ask God to take you back without it, you're asking Him to let you go back without going back. **It's logically impossible.**

So we have to go through with it. But the same brokenness that makes us need it also makes us incapable of doing it.

Can we do it if God helps us? Yes. But what does "God helping us" actually mean?

**It means God putting part of Himself into us.** He gives us a piece of His reasoning power—that's how we think. He puts some of His love into us—that's how we love. When you teach a kid to write, you hold their hand while they form the letters. They're forming the letters because you're forming them.

We love and reason because God loves and reasons and **holds our hand while we do it**.

If we hadn't fallen, this would be straightforward. But now we need God's help to do something **God Himself, in His own nature, never does**: surrender, suffer, submit, die. There's nothing in God's nature that corresponds to this. **The one path we need His help with most is a path God has never walked.**

God can only share what He has. And this thing? He doesn't have it.

**Unless...**

**Unless God became human.**

Suppose our human nature—which can suffer and die—merged with God's nature in one person. Then that person could help us. He could surrender His will, suffer, and die **because He was human**. And He could do it perfectly **because He was God**.

You and I can only go through this process if God does it in us. But God can only do it if He becomes human. **Our attempts at dying to ourselves will only work if we share in God's dying.** Just like our thinking only works because it's a drop from the ocean of His intelligence. But we can't share God's dying unless God dies. And He can't die unless He becomes human.

**That's how He pays our debt. That's how He suffers something He Himself didn't need to suffer.**

**"But If He Was God, It Must Have Been Easy"**

I've heard people say: "If Jesus was God, then His suffering doesn't count because it must have been easy for Him."

What's staggering to me isn't the ingratitude—it's the **complete misunderstanding of the mechanics**.

In one sense, they're right. They've actually understated their case. The perfect submission, the perfect suffering, the perfect death weren't just easier for Jesus because He was God—**they were only possible because He was God**.

But that's a bizarre reason not to accept them.

The teacher can form letters for the child **because the teacher is an adult who knows how to write**. Yes, that makes it easier for the teacher. And **only because it's easier for the teacher can the teacher help the child**. If the child rejected help because "it's easy for grown-ups" and waited to learn from another child who also couldn't write (and therefore had no "unfair advantage"), the child would never learn.

If I'm drowning in a river and someone with one foot still on the bank reaches out to save me, should I shout back, "No! That's not fair! You have an advantage!"? **That advantage is the only reason they can help me.**

Where else are you going to look for help if not to something stronger than yourself?

**Bottom Line**

This is my framework for understanding what Christians call the Atonement. But remember: **this is just another mental model**. Don't mistake it for the thing itself. And if it doesn't help you, drop it.

The formula is what matters. The formula works.
